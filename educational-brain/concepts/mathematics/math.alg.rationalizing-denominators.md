## Learning Objective
- The learner can rationalize a denominator containing a single radical term by multiplying numerator and denominator by that same radical.
- The learner can rationalize a denominator containing a binomial with a radical (a + √b) by multiplying by its conjugate (a − √b), using the difference-of-squares identity to eliminate the radical.
- The learner can explain why multiplying numerator and denominator by the identical quantity — a radical over itself, or a conjugate over itself — always equals multiplying by 1, so the fraction's value is preserved even though its form changes.

## Core Understanding
Rationalizing a denominator means rewriting a fraction so that its denominator contains no radical, without changing the fraction's actual value — and the entire technique rests on one fact, applied in two different shapes depending on the denominator's structure: multiplying a fraction's numerator and denominator by the identical nonzero quantity is always multiplying by 1, which changes the fraction's *form* but never its *value*. For a denominator that is a single radical term, the identical quantity to multiply by is that same radical (√b/√b), because √b × √b = b eliminates the radical directly by definition. For a denominator that is a binomial containing a radical (a + √b), multiplying by that same radical alone does not work — expanding (a+√b)(√b) still leaves a radical term — so instead the identical quantity to multiply by is the *conjugate*, a − √b, chosen specifically because (a+√b)(a−√b) is a difference of squares, a² − b, which eliminates the radical entirely by cancelling the cross terms that a straightforward radical-alone multiplication would not. This is why the method genuinely has two cases rather than one universal formula: the tool that clears a single-term radical denominator (multiply by the same radical) and the tool that clears a binomial radical denominator (multiply by the conjugate) are both instances of "multiply by 1 in a form chosen to eliminate the radical," but the specific form of that "1" depends entirely on whether the radical stands alone or is added to (or subtracted from) another term.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — clear a lone radical denominator by multiplying by that exact radical.** 3/√5 becomes (3√5)/5 by multiplying top and bottom by √5. *Upgrade trigger*: a denominator where the radical is not alone but part of a sum, like 3+√2, where "multiply by that exact radical" produces an expression that still contains a radical, not a clean elimination. *Shelf life*: one session.
2. **Intermediate — a binomial radical denominator needs its conjugate, not the radical alone.** For a+√b, multiply by a−√b instead, because (a+√b)(a−√b)=a²−b has no radical left in it. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to justify *why* the conjugate specifically works when the radical alone does not — this model states the correct tool but not yet the underlying reason. *Shelf life*: durable and remains the correct procedure permanently.
3. **Advanced — both techniques are instances of multiplying by a form of 1, chosen to trigger an identity that eliminates the radical.** The single-radical case uses √b×√b=b; the binomial case uses the difference-of-squares identity (a+b)(a−b)=a²−b² — different identities, same underlying justification (multiplying by 1 changes form, not value). *Upgrade trigger*: encountering a denominator this concept's two standard cases don't immediately cover (e.g. a sum of two distinct radical terms), motivating a search for which identity would eliminate the radical there.
4. **Expert — rationalization is a special case of a general algebraic move: multiplying by a strategically chosen unit to force a target structure to appear.** The same move (multiply by 1, in a specially chosen form) recurs whenever an expression's form needs changing without changing its value — completing the square, clearing complex-number denominators by their conjugate, and rewriting trigonometric expressions all reuse this identical justification. *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the model that makes each of those later techniques feel like a familiar move applied in a new setting rather than an unrelated new trick.

## Why Students Fail
The most common and structurally simplest failure occurs even in the single-radical case: the learner multiplies by a radical that does not actually match the denominator — reaching for a convenient or nearby number in the problem rather than the denominator's own specific radical — which fails to eliminate the correct radical and may introduce an unrelated one. The second, more damaging failure occurs once binomial denominators are introduced: the learner attempts the already-familiar single-radical technique (multiply by the radical alone) on a binomial denominator, because that technique worked reliably before and nothing about the new problem visually signals that the method must change — but multiplying (a+√b) by √b alone does not eliminate the radical (the expansion still contains a term with √b in it), so the technique silently fails to achieve its stated purpose while looking, to an inattentive eye, like ordinary algebra was performed correctly. A third, narrower failure appears specifically at the conjugate-formation step: having correctly recognised that a conjugate is needed, the learner mis-forms it — flipping both signs of the binomial rather than only the sign between the two terms, or flipping the wrong one — producing an expression that is not the genuine conjugate and therefore does not trigger the difference-of-squares cancellation the technique depends on.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1..MC-3) and its repair actions B01–B03, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — WRONG-RADICAL-USED-AS-MULTIPLIER** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of "multiply by some radical" without the specific constraint that the multiplier must exactly match the denominator's own radical.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: multiplying by an unrelated radical, or a radical drawn from the numerator rather than the denominator.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): present Example 1 and check whether the multiplier matches the denominator's radical exactly.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — re-identify the denominator's exact radical first, in isolation, before selecting any multiplier, then multiply numerator and denominator by precisely that same radical.
  - **Verification of death**: given 7/√3, the learner selects √3 as the multiplier unprompted, without needing to be told which radical to use.

- **MC-2 — CONJUGATE-NOT-USED-FOR-BINOMIAL-RADICAL-DENOMINATOR** (foundational)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation — the single-radical technique is applied unmodified to a structurally different denominator shape, because the technique "worked before" and the notation gives no explicit signal that a different tool is now required.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: for a denominator like 2+√3, multiplying by √3 alone rather than by the conjugate 2−√3.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): present Example 2 and check whether the radical alone or the full conjugate is used.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — attempt the radical-alone approach directly, showing the radical remains present in the result, then re-derive using the conjugate to show it disappears, making the scope boundary between the two techniques directly observed rather than asserted.
  - **Verification of death**: given a new binomial radical denominator, the learner reaches for the conjugate directly, without first attempting (and failing at) the single-radical technique.

- **MC-3 — CONJUGATE-SIGN-NOT-FLIPPED-CORRECTLY** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 4, notation-induced — "form the conjugate" is read as "change the signs" generically, rather than the specific instruction "change only the sign between the two terms," inviting either both signs to flip or the wrong one to flip.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: forming the conjugate of a+√b as −a−√b (both signs flipped) or −a+√b (the wrong sign flipped) rather than the correct a−√b.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): review a submitted conjugate for an incorrect sign flip.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — restate the rule precisely: flip only the sign *between* the two terms, keep each term's own value the same.
  - **Verification of death**: given a mixed set of binomial radical expressions, the learner forms the correct conjugate for each, flipping only the connecting sign every time.

## Analogies
- **Best — the exact-match key.** Just as a specific lock requires its own exact key (not any key that looks similar), eliminating a specific radical requires multiplying by exactly that radical — a near-miss multiplier does not open the lock. This directly targets MC-1's mismatched-multiplier error.
- **Alternative — the demolition tool matched to the structure.** A single support beam (a lone radical) can be removed with one straightforward tool (multiply by itself); a load-bearing wall shared between two connected rooms (a binomial with a radical) requires a different, specifically-designed tool (the conjugate) — using the first tool on the second job doesn't work, however well it worked before. This targets MC-2's technique-scope confusion directly.
- **Story analogy** — a chemical neutralisation reaction where only a specifically matched reagent (the conjugate, not just any related compound) triggers the reaction that removes the target substance (the radical) — a near-miss reagent leaves the substance unreacted.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "just multiply by whatever radical is in the problem."** This licenses MC-1 directly; the correct instruction is "multiply by the *denominator's own* radical, specifically," never any other radical that happens to appear in the problem.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "rationalizing always means multiplying by the radical."** This is exactly what produces MC-2 when the denominator is a binomial; the correct, more general frame is "rationalizing means multiplying by whatever form of 1 eliminates the radical — sometimes that's the radical itself, sometimes it's the conjugate."

## Demonstrations
- **The mismatched-multiplier collision.** Attempt to rationalize 3/√5 by multiplying by an unrelated radical (say √3), showing the original denominator's √5 remains present in the result — then repeat correctly with √5 itself, showing it vanishes. *Predict, before multiplying, whether the mismatched radical will work* — the failed attempt is the demonstration, operationalising Blueprint B01's repair.
- **The failed-single-radical-on-a-binomial attempt.** Attempt to rationalize 2/(3+√2) by multiplying by √2 alone, expanding fully and showing a radical term genuinely survives in the result — then repeat correctly using the conjugate 3−√2, showing the difference-of-squares expansion eliminates it entirely. *Predict, before attempting the radical-alone approach, whether it will succeed* — this directly operationalises Blueprint B02's repair, which the Blueprint's own Teaching Notes specifically designed to require *demonstrating* the failure, not merely stating a rule to avoid it.
- **The conjugate-formation drill.** Present several binomial radical expressions and require the learner to form each one's conjugate, flipping only the connecting sign, before any multiplication is attempted — isolating the conjugate-formation judgment (MC-3's territory) from the broader technique-selection judgment (MC-2's territory).

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for both core techniques — the single-radical multiplier and the conjugate — since each is a specific, deliberately chosen application of an already-known identity (√b×√b=b, and the difference-of-squares pattern from `math.alg.factoring-special`), not a principle to be re-derived from scratch. What is genuinely discoverable, and central to this concept's own distinguishing content, is *why* the single-radical technique fails on a binomial denominator and what property the correct replacement must have: (1) **Need** — "Rationalize 2/(3+√2). Try multiplying by √2 alone, like before." (2) **Playground** — expand (3+√2)(√2) fully, observing that a radical term remains. (3) **Invention** — "What property would a multiplier need to have, to guarantee the radical disappears from a sum like 3+√2?" (4) **Collision** — offer the learner's own proposed multiplier (if it still contains √2 in some form) and test whether it genuinely eliminates the radical from the product. (5) **Formalisation** — the conjugate (3−√2) works specifically because (3+√2)(3−√2) is a difference of squares, 9−2=7, with no radical remaining — the cross terms that a straightforward radical-alone multiplication leaves behind are exactly what the conjugate's opposite sign cancels. (6) **Compression** — "For a sum with a radical, use its conjugate — the opposite-sign twin that turns the multiplication into a difference of squares."

## Teaching Sequence
The single-radical technique (TA-A01, targeting MC-1) is secured first, establishing the general "multiply by a form of 1 to clear the radical" logic on the simplest possible case, before the binomial/conjugate case (TA-A02, targeting MC-2) is introduced — a learner who has not yet internalised *why* multiplying by a matched radical works has no foundation from which to understand why the conjugate is a *different but analogous* matched multiplier for a structurally different denominator. The binomial case is deliberately taught via the failed-attempt-first sequence (attempt the single-radical method, observe its failure, then introduce the conjugate as the correct alternative) rather than presenting the conjugate as a given rule, specifically because the Blueprint's own Teaching Notes identify MC-2 as arising from technique overgeneralisation — seeing the familiar method genuinely fail is what motivates and justifies the new one, rather than the new method feeling like an arbitrary addition to memorise. The conjugate-sign-formation drill (targeting MC-3) is interleaved throughout the binomial-case teaching rather than isolated as a separate stage, since MC-3's error (an incorrectly formed conjugate) can occur on any binomial denominator regardless of how far into the broader technique the learner has progressed. The Blueprint's own Component 5 sequence (TA-A01 single-radical technique via conceptual shift, TA-A02 binomial/conjugate contrast pair, TA-A03 numerical value-preservation verification, TA-A04 mastery gate) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Demonstration** — the failed-single-radical-on-a-binomial attempt, run as a genuine live failure rather than a stated warning, so the technique-scope boundary between the two methods is directly observed.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "will this multiplier actually clear the radical?" asked before every rationalization attempt, building the habit of checking the tool against the denominator's actual structure rather than defaulting to whichever technique was most recently practised.
- **DO: Worked example, the conjugate-formation drill** — practised as its own isolated skill (form the conjugate, nothing more) before being embedded in a full rationalization problem, separating MC-3's narrower error from MC-2's broader technique-selection error.
- **Does NOT fit: introducing binomial denominators before the single-radical case is independently fluent.** The conjugate's justification (a specially-matched form of 1, analogous to but different from the single-radical case) depends on the learner already understanding the simpler case's own logic.
- **Does NOT fit: presenting the conjugate rule directly, without first letting the single-radical method visibly fail on a binomial denominator.** Per the Blueprint's own explicit Teaching Notes, this "demonstrate the failure first" design is deliberate and central to preventing MC-2 from persisting.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence for binomial denominators is "the radical alone won't clear this one — you need its conjugate." Slow down on "won't," making the failure of the familiar method explicit before offering the replacement. Listen for a learner reaching immediately for "multiply by the radical" on a binomial denominator, with no pause to notice the denominator's different shape — the unpaused, reflexive reach for the familiar tool predicts MC-2 reliably. For conjugate formation, listen for whether the learner narrates "flip the sign in the middle" versus a vaguer "flip the signs" — the vaguer phrasing, even when the written work happens to come out correct, signals the precise rule (only the connecting sign) has not fully landed and MC-3 remains a live risk under pressure. A confident, unprompted "this is a binomial, so I need the conjugate" stated before any multiplication begins is the strongest positive signal this concept produces. Channel reality is owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals
- **A multiplier that does not match the denominator's own radical exactly** — MC-1; route to the mismatched-multiplier collision, never to a bare restatement of "use the matching radical."
- **The single-radical technique attempted on a binomial denominator (radical alone, not the conjugate)** — MC-2, cleanly diagnostic; route to the failed-single-radical-on-a-binomial attempt so the failure is felt directly rather than merely corrected.
- **A conjugate with both signs flipped, or the wrong sign flipped** — MC-3; route to the conjugate-formation drill in isolation, separate from the broader rationalization procedure.
- **The conjugate selected immediately and correctly upon recognising a binomial denominator, with no attempted single-radical detour** — the strongest positive signal available, indicating MC-2's technique-scope boundary has genuinely been internalised.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A04 gate, MAMR ⌈0.8×5⌉ = 4/5, including its P76 wave-amplitude transfer probe, whose part (b) specifically requires *demonstrating* why the radical-alone attempt fails on the given binomial denominator rather than simply avoiding it — a gate passed without that explanatory demonstration component does not certify the deeper technique-selection understanding, only the mechanical ability to apply the conjugate when prompted to.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance is "I tried multiplying by the radical but it's still there" facing a binomial denominator. The concept-specific smaller question validates the observation directly and redirects: **"You're right, it's still there — that method is for when the radical is alone. This denominator has two terms. What tool do we use for that?"** — confirming the learner's own correct observation (the failure is real, not a mistake in execution) before introducing the conjugate as the structurally appropriate replacement. If the freeze is specifically at forming the conjugate, shrink to the isolated rule: **"Just this one part — what sign is between the two terms right now? What does it become?"** — isolating the single sign-flip judgment from the larger rationalization procedure surrounding it. Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.
