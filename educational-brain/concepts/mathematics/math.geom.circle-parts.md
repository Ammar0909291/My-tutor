## Learning Objective
Students will name and define the core parts of a circle — radius, diameter, chord, arc, sector, and segment — explain the relationships between them (diameter = 2 × radius; diameter is the longest chord; an arc is a portion of the circumference), distinguish between the terms precisely when labelling diagrams, and apply their understanding to set up area and circumference calculations and angle-in-circle problems.

## Core Understanding
A circle is defined by its centre and radius; every point on the boundary is exactly one radius away from the centre. From this single fact all part-names derive: the **radius** connects centre to boundary; the **diameter** is a chord that passes through the centre (and equals 2r, the longest possible chord); a **chord** connects any two boundary points (diameter is the special case); an **arc** is the curved boundary between two points (minor arc < semicircle, major arc > semicircle); a **sector** is the "pie slice" region bounded by two radii and the arc between them; a **segment** is the region between a chord and its arc (not to be confused with a sector — a segment has NO radius sides). Key relationship: diameter = 2 × radius. The semicircle is the special case where the chord is the diameter, splitting the circle into two equal halves.

## Mental Models
1. **The pizza-slice model for sector vs. segment**: a sector is a pizza slice (two straight cuts from the centre — radii — plus the crust arc); a segment is the piece left after you remove the triangular part of the slice (chord as the straight edge, arc as the crust, no lines reaching the centre)
2. **The spoke model for radius/diameter**: think of a bicycle wheel — every spoke is a radius (centre to rim); the diameter is the spoke that goes all the way across, passing through the hub, equal to two spokes placed tip-to-tip
3. **The clock-hand arc model**: the boundary traced by a clock's minute hand from one position to another is an arc; the two positions (on the boundary) define the endpoints; the region between that arc and the two clock hands (radii) is a sector

## Why Students Fail
1. **Confusing sector and segment**: both involve an arc and a chord, but a sector replaces the chord with two radii; without clear vocabulary discipline, students label either shape as the other
2. **Applying radius formula where diameter is given (or vice versa)**: C = 2πr requires radius; when the problem provides diameter, students plug in d directly, computing 2πd — the most common circle-calculation error globally
3. **Treating "chord" as a synonym for "radius" or "diameter"**: a chord is any segment joining two boundary points, but students often hear "chord" and picture only the diameter; they do not readily accept that a chord can be shorter than the diameter
4. **Calling the arc length = the arc label**: students read "arc AB" and think it names a length rather than a set of points; they confuse the name of the arc with its measure (degree measure or length)

## Misconceptions
**MC-1: SECTOR-SEGMENT-SWAP (Type 1, overgeneralization)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "The shaded region between the chord and the arc is a sector" (when it lacks radii — it is a segment)
- **Mechanism**: both sector and segment involve an arc; students overgeneralize "arc + enclosed region = sector" without registering the critical distinction (two radii vs. one chord)
- **Evidence signature**: consistently swaps labels on diagrams; cannot produce the defining feature of each on demand
- **Repair path**: "A sector has two radii meeting at the centre — count the straight sides: two straight sides (both reaching the centre) → sector; one straight side (a chord, not reaching the centre) → segment. Draw both side by side and label 'radii to centre' vs. 'chord, not at centre.'"

**MC-2: DIAMETER-RADIUS-CONFUSION-IN-FORMULA (Type 5, instruction-induced)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "The circumference is 2π × 14 cm" when the problem gives diameter = 14 cm (answer should use r = 7)
- **Mechanism**: C = 2πr and C = πd both exist; students learn 2πr first, then see a diameter value, and plug it in directly without halving it — or they confuse which symbol appears in which formula variant
- **Evidence signature**: circumference answers consistently double what they should be; the student cannot spontaneously write both formula variants and identify which input each needs
- **Repair path**: "Two formula paths lead to the same answer: C = 2πr (use the radius) or C = πd (use the diameter). If you're given the diameter and use C = 2πr, you must halve it first: r = d/2. Never plug the diameter into the 2πr version without halving."

**MC-3: CHORD-MEANS-DIAMETER (Type 3, language contamination)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "A chord is a line through the centre" (defining diameter, not chord in general)
- **Mechanism**: the word "chord" appears most prominently in the context of the diameter (the longest chord); students attach the term to that prototype and do not extend it to shorter, off-centre segments
- **Evidence signature**: cannot identify a chord that is clearly shorter than the diameter; draws only diameters when asked to draw "a chord"
- **Repair path**: "A chord just needs to touch the boundary at both ends — it does NOT have to go through the centre. The diameter is a special chord that does go through the centre. Draw a short chord nowhere near the centre — that is also a chord."

**MC-4: ARC-MEASURE-CONFUSION (Type 4, notation-induced)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "Arc AB is 60°" — student believes this means the arc has length 60° rather than that the arc subtends a 60° central angle
- **Mechanism**: arc notation uses degree labels (central angle measure) interchangeably with length labels in different contexts; students do not distinguish between degree measure of an arc and its actual length in centimetres
- **Evidence signature**: uses the degree value directly in a length calculation without multiplying by (θ/360)×2πr
- **Repair path**: "An arc can be measured two ways: by the central angle (in degrees) or by the arc length (in cm or m). When you see '60°', that is the central angle measure — to get the actual length you use arc length = (60/360) × 2πr. Label which one you have before computing."

## Analogies
1. **The bicycle wheel**: radius = every spoke from hub to rim; diameter = an imaginary spoke that crosses the entire wheel through the hub; chord = a string stretched directly across the wheel (not through the hub); arc = the rim section between two valve positions
2. **The pie/pizza**: the full pie is the circle; one slice is a sector (straight cuts from centre); cutting off just the crust piece (between a chord and the arc) is a segment

## Demonstrations
1. **Labelling drill on a blank circle**: draw a circle with centre O marked; students add and label five things in sequence: a radius (OA), the diameter (OB through O), a chord that does NOT go through O, a minor arc, a major arc, a sector, a segment — forcing use of every term in one diagram
2. **Sector vs. segment cut-out**: cut a paper circle into sectors; then on a different circle, cut along a chord (not through centre); hold up each piece and ask "sector or segment? how do you know?" — tactile disambiguation
3. **Measurement verification**: have students measure radius and diameter with a ruler on a printed circle and confirm diameter = 2 × radius for several circles of different sizes — cementing the doubling relationship

## Discovery Questions
1. If the radius of a circle is 5 cm, what is the diameter? If someone tells you the diameter is 18 cm, what is the radius?
2. Draw a circle and mark two points on the boundary. How many different arcs connect those two points? Which one is the minor arc?
3. A chord passes through the centre of a circle. What special name does this chord have? Why is it the longest chord the circle can have?
4. What is the difference between a sector and a segment? Draw both on the same circle and label the boundaries of each.
5. If you cut a pizza into 8 equal slices, what is the central angle of each slice? What would you call each slice using proper geometry vocabulary?

## Teaching Sequence
1. **Activation (3 min)**: Show a photograph of a bicycle wheel and a pizza. Ask "what parts can you name? What would you call the spokes? What about one pizza slice?" — surface informal language before introducing formal terms
2. **Centre and radius (4 min)**: Draw a circle with centre O; define radius as "every spoke length." Measure several radii to confirm they are all equal — establishing the fundamental defining property
3. **Diameter and chord (5 min)**: Introduce diameter as "the longest chord, passing through the centre." Demonstrate a shorter chord (not through O) to establish "chord" in the general sense. Contrast: "Diameter = special chord through centre; chord = any segment joining two boundary points." Address MC-3 explicitly
4. **Arc, sector, segment (8 min)**: On the same circle, shade a sector (pizza slice) and a segment (crust piece). Label radii on the sector; label the chord on the segment. Establish the defining feature of each. Drill MC-1 with a side-by-side pair of diagrams
5. **Formula preview (3 min)**: Write C = 2πr and C = πd side by side. Establish which uses radius and which uses diameter. Plant the MC-2 repair rule before students encounter the formulas in later lessons
6. **Labelling practice (7 min)**: Students label a blank circle with all six terms; then solve 2–3 problems identifying parts from a described figure

## Tutor Actions
- **Use both formula variants explicitly**: whenever circumference arises, write both C = 2πr and C = πd and ask which the problem's given information fits
- **Always ask "does it go through the centre?"** before accepting a chord label — the answer determines whether it is a diameter or just a chord
- **Draw sector and segment side by side**: any time either term is used, briefly sketch both to reinforce the contrast
- **Ask for defining features, not just names**: "What makes this a sector?" not just "What is this shape called?"

## Voice Teaching Notes
- **Emphasis markers**: stress "NOT through the centre" when defining a general chord; stress "Two radii and an arc — that's the sector; one chord and an arc — that's the segment"
- **Hesitation-recovery moves**: if the student hesitates on sector vs. segment, ask "how many straight edges does this region have? Two reaching the centre → sector; one chord → segment"
- **Load-bearing sentences**:
  - "Every point on the circle is exactly one radius from the centre — that's the definition"
  - "Diameter = 2 × radius, always — and the diameter is the longest chord"
  - "Sector has two radii; segment has one chord — count the straight edges"
- **Register notes**: "radius/diameter/chord/arc/sector/segment" are all formal terms that should be used precisely from the start — no informal substitutes like "the line across" or "the slice"

## Assessment Signals
- **Labels all six parts correctly on a blank circle diagram** = AUTOMATIC
- **Swaps sector and segment labels** = MC-1 active (count-the-straight-sides diagnostic; sector/segment side-by-side contrast)
- **Plugs diameter directly into C = 2πr without halving** = MC-2 active (dual-formula card: write both forms and ask which the given information fits)
- **Draws only diameters when asked for a chord** = MC-3 active (draw an off-centre chord explicitly; confirm it still qualifies)
- **Treats degree arc label as arc length** = MC-4 active (two-measure distinction: central angle in degrees vs. length in cm)

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
**Immediate tier (right-now, same turn)**:
1. If MC-1 (sector/segment swap): "Count the straight edges on this region. Two straight lines meeting at the centre → sector. One straight line (a chord, not at the centre) → segment."
2. If MC-2 (diameter in radius formula): "You used C = 2πr but you have the diameter, not the radius. You need to halve first: r = d/2. Or switch to C = πd directly — same answer, less work."
3. If MC-3 (chord = diameter only): "Draw a short line connecting two boundary points well away from the centre. That is also a chord. A chord only needs two boundary endpoints — it does NOT have to cross the centre."
4. If MC-4 (degree = length confusion): "Sixty degrees tells you the central angle of the arc — not its length. To get the length you need: (angle/360) × 2πr."

**Follow-up tier (consolidation)**:
- Labelling sprint: five different circles, each with one part shaded/marked — student identifies and defines each; repeat until 5/5 with definitions, not just names
- For MC-2: give three problems alternating radius and diameter inputs to build the halving habit

## Memory Hooks
- **"Sector = Slices (two radii like knife cuts); Segment = Crust piece (one chord, no centre)"**: the pizza mnemonic locks the sector/segment distinction
- **"d = 2r — diameter is double the radius"**: the single most critical numerical relationship
- **"Chord: any two boundary points; Diameter: chord through the centre"**: the generalisation hierarchy in one line

## Transfer Connections
1. **Circle circumference** (`math.geom.circle-circumference`): the circumference formula C = 2πr = πd applies directly to the radius/diameter distinction established here; every circumference problem requires knowing which part name maps to the input value
2. **Circle area** (`math.geom.circle-area`): A = πr² uses the radius exclusively; students who confuse radius and diameter propagate their error directly into area calculations
3. **Circle theorems** (`math.geom.circle-theorems`): the inscribed-angle theorem, chord-equidistance theorem, and tangent properties all reference radius, chord, arc, and sector — fluent part-naming is prerequisite vocabulary for every theorem

## Cross-Subject Connections
- **Engineering and wheel design**: spoke length (radius), axle-to-rim distance (radius), tyre size measured by diameter — circle-parts vocabulary is the standard engineering description of any rotating disc
- **Music and strings**: a string vibrates in segments; the term "node" (a point on the boundary where amplitude is zero) maps loosely to chord endpoints; acoustic resonance involves arc-length-to-wavelength ratios
