## Learning Objective

The learner can: state that a ratio a:b is a MULTIPLICATIVE comparison
("for every a of one quantity, there are b of the other"), write
ratios in all three forms (a:b, a/b, "a to b"), and correctly preserve
ORDER (a:b≠b:a in general); generate and verify equivalent ratios via
multiplication/division (never addition), and simplify a ratio via its
GCF without believing the underlying quantities themselves change;
distinguish PART-TO-PART from PART-TO-WHOLE comparisons (the second
term is another category versus the sum of all categories); and
interpret a RATE as a ratio comparing quantities with different units,
computing a UNIT RATE by dividing both terms by the second term's
value.

## Core Understanding

`math.arith.fractions` already establishes fraction notation,
equivalent fractions, and GCF-based simplification. A **ratio** a:b
compares two quantities MULTIPLICATIVELY — "for every a of one
quantity, there are b of the other" — written in three equivalent
forms: a:b (colon), a/b (fraction), "a to b" (word). Critically, **ORDER
MATTERS**: 3:5 (red:blue) is a genuinely different comparison from 5:3
(blue:red would be 3:5's own reverse) — the quantity named FIRST in a
question occupies the FIRST position. **Equivalent ratios** are
generated by multiplying (or dividing) both terms by the same nonzero
number — 2:3=4:6=6:9=8:12 — directly reusing `math.arith.
fraction-equivalence`'s own multiplicative-scaling logic. **Simplifying**
a ratio (dividing both terms by their GCF) does NOT change the
underlying quantities — 12:18 simplified to 2:3 still describes 12 and
18 real items; 2:3 is a smaller-number NAME for the identical
relationship, not a claim that the quantities themselves shrank.
**Part-to-part** ratios compare one category to ANOTHER category
(boys:girls=12:18); **part-to-whole** ratios compare one category to
the SUM OF ALL categories (boys:total=12:30, where total=boys+girls) —
the diagnostic test is simply "what does the second term represent: a
specific other category, or everything combined?" Finally, a **rate**
is a ratio comparing quantities with DIFFERENT units (60 km per 1 hr);
a **unit rate** is a rate where the second term equals 1, found by
dividing both terms by the second term's original value (72 cookies
in 3 hours → 24 cookies/hour).

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "a ratio is just two numbers side by side; the
  order doesn't really matter, like listing ingredients"**: the
  learner treats a:b and b:a as interchangeable, losing the reference
  direction the ratio encodes. Shelf-life warning: this model produces
  a genuine practical error the moment a ratio must be applied
  directionally (e.g., mixing paint in the wrong proportion).
- **Intermediate model — "ratio order matters, and I can generate
  equivalent ratios and simplify via GCF, but I sometimes mix up
  part-to-part with part-to-whole"**: the learner correctly preserves
  order and executes equivalence/simplification, but may compute a
  "ratio to total" using only some categories rather than all of them.
  Upgrade trigger: being asked for a part-to-whole ratio in a
  three-or-more-category context.
- **Advanced model — "ratios are precise multiplicative comparisons
  where order encodes reference direction, simplification never
  changes the underlying quantities (only their expression), and
  part-to-whole always sums ALL categories — rates extend this to
  different-unit comparisons via unit rates"**: the learner fluently
  distinguishes part-to-part from part-to-whole, correctly computes
  unit rates for comparison, and connects ratios to linear
  relationships (rate as a constant of proportionality). Upgrade
  trigger: being asked whether a specific ratio (like a ramp's
  rise:run) is best understood as a part-to-part comparison or a rate.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner still treating a:b and b:a as
  interchangeable (beginner model, directly triggering MC-1) should
  not be pushed toward part-to-whole distinctions or rate computation
  (advanced model) before order-preservation is fully secure — MC-1 is
  FOUNDATIONAL per the Blueprint's own MAMR.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant, foundational failure treats a ratio as an unordered pair
— believing 3:5 and 5:3 describe the same comparison — missing that the
FIRST term always corresponds to whichever quantity was named first in
the question, and reversing the order genuinely reverses the meaning
(3 red to 5 blue is not the same mixture as 5 red to 3 blue). A second
failure, once order is respected, confuses PART-TO-PART with
PART-TO-WHOLE comparisons — asked for "red to total," writes
red:another-category instead of red:(sum of all categories), missing
that "total" specifically means every category summed together. A
third, independent failure believes simplifying a ratio (e.g., 6:10 to
3:5) literally changes the underlying quantities, as though the
original 6 and 10 items became 3 and 5 items, missing that
simplification only changes the NAME of the relationship, never the
actual counts.

## Misconceptions

Reusing the Blueprint's Misconception Registry (Educational Brain v1.0
format), birth-type classification added per this program's diagnostic
procedure — not re-derived:

### MC-1: Ratio is commutative (Foundational; Type 6 — analogy overextension)
**Trigger**: treats a:b as equivalent to b:a — "3 apples to 5 oranges"
is treated the same as "5 oranges to 3 apples."
**Diagnostic note**: classified Type 6 — the learner overextends the
commutativity of ordinary addition/multiplication (where order
genuinely doesn't matter, a+b=b+a) onto ratio notation, where order IS
load-bearing.
**Repair**: cats:dogs=6:4=3:2 means "for every 3 cats, 2 dogs" (cats is
the reference, named first); dogs:cats=4:6=2:3 means "for every 2
dogs, 3 cats" (dogs is now the reference) — 3:2≠2:3, a genuinely
different comparison. Real consequence: mixing paint as "red:blue=3:2"
versus accidentally "2:3" produces the wrong color balance.
**MAMR**: FOUNDATIONAL — ratio order is the foundation for both
part-to-part analysis (which name is the reference) and simplification
(which preserves the relative comparison); must clear before MC-2 or
MC-3.

### MC-2: Part-to-part vs. part-to-whole confusion (Moderate; Type 1 — overgeneralization)
**Trigger**: asked for the ratio of red to TOTAL, writes red:(one other
category) rather than red:(sum of ALL categories).
**Repair**: "total" means the SUM OF ALL PARTS — for 10 boys and 14
girls, boys:total=10:(10+14)=10:24=5:12, NOT 10:14 (which is
boys:girls, a part-to-part comparison). The diagnostic test: what does
the second term represent — a specific other category, or everything
combined?

### MC-3: Ratio simplification changes the quantities (Moderate; Type 1 — overgeneralization)
**Trigger**: believes simplifying 6:10 to 3:5 means the original items
(6 and 10) literally became 3 and 5.
**Repair**: 12:18 and 2:3 are EQUIVALENT ratios describing the SAME
relationship — grouping 12 red and 18 blue tiles into 6 groups of (2
red, 3 blue) shows 2:3 describes ONE group, while 12 and 18 remain the
real, unchanged totals. Simplifying changes only the notation, never
the actual quantities.

## Analogies

**Primary — the mixing recipe (Blueprint's own core context)**: a
ratio a:b is like a recipe ratio — "2 cups flour for every 3 cups
sugar" — the recipe scales up or down (4:6, 6:9, 8:12) but the
UNDERLYING PROPORTION (2 flour for every 3 sugar) never changes;
simplification just finds the smallest equivalent recipe describing
the identical taste.

**Secondary — the reversed recipe disaster (targets MC-1)**: mixing
"red:blue=3:2" (3 parts red for every 2 parts blue) versus
accidentally mixing "2:3" (2 parts red for every 3 parts blue)
produces a visibly different, wrong-colored paint — concretely
demonstrating that reversing ratio order is not a harmless notational
choice.

**Anti-analogy to retire**: "A ratio is just two related numbers,
listed in whichever order is convenient." This directly invites MC-1
by suggesting ratio notation carries no directional information,
missing that the order encodes exactly which quantity is being used as
the reference point.

## Demonstrations

**Order-reversal contrast (breaks MC-1)**: 18 girls, 12 boys.
girls:boys=18:12=3:2 ("for every 3 girls, 2 boys"); boys:girls=
12:18=2:3 ("for every 2 boys, 3 girls") — genuinely different ratios,
confirmed via 3÷2=1.5≠2÷3≈0.67.

**Part-to-part vs. part-to-whole (breaks MC-2)**: class of 12 boys, 18
girls. Part-to-part: boys:girls=12:18=2:3. Part-to-whole:
boys:total=12:30=2:5, girls:total=18:30=3:5 (and 2/5+3/5=1, confirming
the parts sum to the whole).

**Simplification preserves quantities (breaks MC-3)**: 24 apples, 36
oranges, simplified 24:36=2:3 (GCF=12). Scale factor 12 recovers the
originals: 2×12=24, 3×12=36 — the apples are STILL 24, unchanged by
simplification.

**Unit rate for comparison**: Car A: 250 km in 5 hrs=50 km/hr; Car B:
210 km in 3 hrs=70 km/hr — B is faster, directly comparable via unit
rates even though the raw ratios (250:5 vs. 210:3) aren't immediately
comparable.

## Discovery Questions

Present two groups of physical counters (e.g., 4 red, 7 green tiles)
and ask the learner to write the ratio of red to green, then green to
red — the learner discovers these produce genuinely different numeric
comparisons (4:7 vs. 7:4) largely unaided, directly experiencing the
order-sensitivity of ratio notation before it's stated as an abstract
rule. Recommendation: guided discovery for the order-sensitivity
insight (directly experiential from the counter-comparison); direct
instruction for the part-to-whole computation rule (MC-2's repair) and
the unit-rate procedure, since both require explicit scaffolding (the
"sum all categories" rule; the "divide by the second term" rule) not
independently rediscoverable.

## Teaching Sequence

MC-1 (ratio is commutative) is addressed first and is FOUNDATIONAL per
the Blueprint's own MAMR — it must be cleared before MC-2 or MC-3,
since order-preservation underlies both correctly naming the reference
in part-to-part/part-to-whole analysis and correctly interpreting what
a simplified ratio preserves. MC-2 (part-to-part vs. part-to-whole
confusion) and MC-3 (simplification changes quantities) are addressed
FIFO once MC-1 clears, per the Blueprint's own protocol structure.

## Voice Teaching Notes

**Register**: Say "which quantity was named FIRST?" explicitly before
writing any ratio, since this is exactly the check that prevents MC-1's
order confusion.

**Wait-time**: After posing a part-to-whole question in a
three-category context, give extended wait-time before revealing
whether all categories were summed — let the learner attempt the
"total" computation themselves.

**Load-bearing sentences**:
- "The order of a ratio always matches the order the quantities were
  named — reversing it is a genuinely different comparison."
- "Simplifying a ratio changes only the notation — the real quantities
  never change."

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Likeliest utterance: "does it matter which order I write the ratio
in — isn't 3:5 the same as 5:3?" — the concept-specific smaller
question: "if a recipe says 'mix 3 parts red paint to 2 parts blue,'
and you instead mix 2 parts red to 3 parts blue, do you get the same
color?" reframes the confusion from "ratio order is a stylistic
choice" (MC-1's commutativity-overextension root) to "ratio order
encodes a genuinely different physical outcome," directly isolating
MC-1 with a consequence the learner can concretely picture.
