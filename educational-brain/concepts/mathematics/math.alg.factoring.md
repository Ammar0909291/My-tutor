## Learning Objective
- The learner can factor a polynomial completely by applying, in strict order, a decision procedure — extract the GCF first, then classify the residual by term count and structure, then verify every remaining factor is either degree 1 or an irreducible degree-2 factor — rather than stopping the moment any single factoring step succeeds.
- The learner can select the correct technique for a residual based on its term count and coefficient structure (2 terms → difference/sum of squares or cubes; 3 terms → perfect-square trinomial or the ac-method, explicitly accounting for a non-1 leading coefficient; 4 terms → grouping) rather than applying one memorised pattern to every problem regardless of fit.
- The learner can use the discriminant (b²−4ac) as a verification tool to confirm a quadratic factor is genuinely irreducible over the reals, rather than assuming every quadratic-looking expression (including a sum of squares) can be forced into real linear factors.

## Core Understanding
Factoring a polynomial means rewriting it as a product of lower-degree polynomials that, when multiplied back out, reproduce the original exactly — it is the reverse operation of the expansion the learner already performs fluently (`math.alg.polynomial-operations`), and every factoring strategy is really a lookup table of "what expansion pattern produced this shape." The procedure is not a single technique but a decision tree applied in a fixed order: first, extract the Greatest Common Factor from every term (this step is never optional and never skippable, because any GCF left inside a "fully factored" answer means the answer is not actually fully factored); second, classify what remains by its number of terms — two terms suggest a difference of squares (a²−b² = (a−b)(a+b)) or a sum/difference of cubes, three terms suggest either a perfect-square trinomial or the general ac-method (splitting the middle term using two numbers that multiply to a·c and add to b, which handles both monic and non-monic trinomials identically), four terms suggest grouping (pairing terms to expose a common binomial factor); third, and critically, this classification-and-factor step is not a one-shot action — the resulting factors must themselves be checked for further factorability (a residual factor might itself have an extractable structure), so the procedure repeats until every remaining piece is either a linear (degree-1) factor or a genuinely irreducible quadratic. The discriminant test (b²−4ac < 0 implies no real linear factors exist) is the tool that makes "genuinely irreducible" a checkable fact rather than a guess — it is what separates a difference of squares (a²−b², always factorable, discriminant of x²−b² is 4b²>0) from a sum of squares (a²+b², never factorable over the reals, discriminant of x²+b² is −4b²<0), two forms that look almost identical on the page but behave oppositely.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — factoring is un-multiplying: find what two things were multiplied to make this.** For 6x²+11x+3, the goal is finding two binomials whose product gives back exactly this trinomial. *Upgrade trigger*: a polynomial with more than one applicable technique (a GCF hiding inside a factorable trinomial), where "just find the two things" has no starting point without a system. *Shelf life*: one session.
2. **Intermediate — factoring is a decision tree: GCF first, then classify by term count, then apply the matching pattern.** Extract GCF → count residual terms → 2 terms (difference of squares/cubes), 3 terms (perfect square or ac-method), 4 terms (grouping) → apply. *Upgrade trigger*: a factor produced by this process that is itself still factorable (e.g. x⁴−81 factoring first into (x²−9)(x²+9), where (x²−9) is a second difference of squares waiting to be split again) — revealing that the tree must be applied recursively, not once. *Shelf life*: durable but incomplete until the recursion habit is installed.
3. **Advanced — factoring terminates only when every remaining factor is linear or a verified-irreducible quadratic, checked by the discriminant.** After each factoring step, ask: can any resulting factor be factored again? Use b²−4ac<0 to certify a quadratic factor as genuinely finished rather than merely "not obviously factorable." *Upgrade trigger*: a non-monic trinomial (leading coefficient ≠ 1), where a memorised monic-only shortcut (guess-and-check binomials) breaks down and the general ac-method (which handles both cases identically) becomes necessary.
4. **Expert — factoring, the Factor Theorem, and polynomial roots are one system: every linear factor (x−a) corresponds to a root a, and complete factorisation over the reals is equivalent to finding every real root and every irreducible quadratic factor.** *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the exact bridge this concept's own unlock (`math.alg.polynomial-roots`) is designed to formalise — a root of multiplicity k corresponds to a repeated linear factor, and Vieta's formulas relate the factored form directly back to the original coefficients.

## Why Students Fail
The single most frequent failure, and the Blueprint's own foundational finding, is stopping after the GCF extraction without checking whether the residual itself can be factored further — the learner correctly pulls out 3x from 6x³−21x²−12x to get 3x(2x²−7x−4) and reports that as the final answer, never noticing the quadratic factor (2x²−7x−4) still splits into (2x+1)(x−4). This is structurally the same premature-termination failure this concept's own prerequisite, `math.alg.factor-theorem`, already names in its MC-2 (finding one factor via root-testing and treating that as complete) — the shared root cause across both concepts is treating "I successfully performed one factoring step" as equivalent to "I have finished factoring," rather than treating every factoring step as one that must be followed by a fresh check on its own output. The second major failure appears specifically with non-monic trinomials (leading coefficient a ≠ 1): a learner taught only the guess-and-check binomial method for monic trinomials (where a=1) has no systematic way to handle a≠1, and either abandons factoring for such trinomials, guesses without a organizing principle, or silently drops the leading coefficient's role in the search — the ac-method (find two numbers multiplying to a·c and summing to b, then split and group) is the general procedure that handles both cases with one identical rule, but only if it is taught as the default method rather than as "what you do when guessing fails." The third failure is a genuine structural confusion between two nearly-identical-looking binomial forms: a difference of squares (a²−b²) factors into real linear factors every time, while a sum of squares (a²+b²) never does over the reals — a learner who has only ever practiced difference-of-squares problems, and pattern-matches on "two squared terms" rather than checking the sign between them, will attempt to factor x²+9 the same way as x²−9 and either produce an incorrect factorisation or force complex-looking factors where none were asked for; the discriminant test is the precise tool that resolves this ambiguity by computation rather than by memorised sign-watching alone.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 2 registry (MC-1..MC-3), with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — GCF-THEN-DONE** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation — extracting the GCF is the first, most memorable, and most procedurally clean step in the factoring process, so it is generalised to be the *entire* process; the closely related mechanism appears one concept earlier as `math.alg.factor-theorem`'s MC-2 (finding one root-tested factor and stopping), confirming this is the same premature-termination pattern recurring at a different stage of the pipeline, not a coincidence.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: factoring 6x³−21x²−12x as "3x(2x²−7x−4)" and stopping, leaving the factorable quadratic (2x²−7x−4) = (2x+1)(x−4) unresolved.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint): given a polynomial whose GCF-extracted residual is itself a factorable trinomial, ask whether the factorisation shown is complete — answering yes confirms MC-1.
  - **Repair**: restate factoring as a two-part discipline attached to every step: (1) extract the GCF, (2) explicitly test whether what remains has a term count and structure matching a further pattern, and only declare "done" once every resulting factor is linear or verified irreducible.
  - **Verification of death**: given a polynomial whose GCF-extracted residual is factorable, the learner extracts the GCF, then continues unprompted to factor the residual completely.

- **MC-2 — AC-METHOD-ONLY-FOR-MONIC** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 5, instruction-induced — many introductions teach a guess-and-check binomial method that only works cleanly when the leading coefficient is 1, leaving no general procedure once a ≠ 1 is encountered; the ac-method is frequently presented as a fallback for "when guessing fails" rather than as the single general rule that subsumes the monic case (where a=1 makes the two-numbers-multiply-to-c-add-to-b search a special case of multiply-to-ac-add-to-b).
  - **Characteristic phrase**: for a non-monic trinomial like 6x²+11x+3, attempting to guess factor pairs of the constant term alone (ignoring the leading coefficient 6) and either failing or arriving at an incorrect factorisation.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint): given a non-monic trinomial, ask what two numbers should be found and what they must multiply to — an answer omitting the leading coefficient from the product confirms MC-2.
  - **Repair**: teach the ac-method as the single default procedure for every trinomial, monic or not: find two numbers whose product is a·c and whose sum is b, split the middle term into those two pieces, then factor by grouping — demonstrate explicitly that setting a=1 reduces this to the familiar monic case, so nothing new is needed when a=1, only more when a≠1.
  - **Verification of death**: given a non-monic trinomial, the learner correctly computes a·c first, searches for the two numbers against that product (not against c alone), and completes the factorisation via grouping.

- **MC-3 — IRREDUCIBLE-QUADRATIC-FACTORABLE** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 2, perceptual intuition — a sum of squares (x²+9) and a difference of squares (x²−9) are visually almost identical (two squared terms, one operator differing by a single sign), so pattern-matching on "two perfect squares present" overrides checking which operator is actually there.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: attempting to factor x²+9 as if it were a difference of squares, producing an incorrect result such as (x+3)(x−3) — which expands back to x²−9, not x²+9.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint): given x²+9, ask whether it factors into real linear factors — answering yes, or producing (x+3)(x−3), confirms MC-3.
  - **Repair**: install the discriminant as a checkable, computation-based test rather than relying on sign-spotting alone: for ax²+bx+c, compute b²−4ac; a negative value proves no real linear factors exist, regardless of how the expression visually resembles a familiar factorable pattern. For x²+9: a=1, b=0, c=9, discriminant = 0−36 = −36 < 0, confirming irreducibility by computation.
  - **Verification of death**: given a mixed set of sum-of-squares and difference-of-squares expressions, the learner correctly identifies which factor and which do not, and can justify a "does not factor" answer using the discriminant rather than a memorised visual rule alone.

## Analogies
- **Un-baking a cake into its ingredients, in a fixed order.** Just as separating a finished cake back into flour, sugar, and eggs follows a known reverse-recipe, factoring reverses a known expansion recipe — and just as you would not stop half-way through un-baking and declare the job done, GCF extraction is only the first ingredient pulled out, not the whole job. *Where it holds*: the reversal framing and the "don't stop halfway" discipline. *Where it breaks* (state explicitly, per Universal Principle on anti-analogy labelling): a cake, once separated, cannot be separated further — but a factored polynomial's own factors frequently need re-checking for further structure, which the baking analogy has no equivalent step for; this must be stated directly rather than left for the learner to discover the analogy's limit unassisted.
- **A locked box with a combination that depends on what's inside.** Extracting the GCF is like noticing the box is inside a bigger crate first (remove the crate before opening the box); classifying the residual by term count is like reading how many dials the lock has (2, 3, or 4) before choosing which combination strategy to try. *Where it holds*: the ordered, structure-dependent decision process. *Where it breaks*: a lock's combination strategy does not change if the box, once opened, contains a smaller locked box needing the exact same procedure again — the recursion in factoring (a factor that is itself still factorable) has no natural analogue here and must be taught as an explicit additional check, not implied by the box metaphor.

## Demonstrations
1. **GCF extraction followed by a factorable quadratic residual, directly confronting MC-1.** Factor 6x³−21x²−12x completely. Step 1: GCF of 6, −21, −12 is 3; GCF of x³, x², x is x; extract 3x → 3x(2x²−7x−4). Step 2 (the step MC-1 skips): is (2x²−7x−4) itself factorable? It is a non-monic trinomial — apply the ac-method: a·c = 2·(−4) = −8, need two numbers multiplying to −8 and summing to −7: those are −8 and 1. Split: 2x²−8x+x−4 = 2x(x−4)+1(x−4) = (2x+1)(x−4). Final answer: 3x(2x+1)(x−4). Verify by expanding back.
2. **The ac-method on a non-monic trinomial, directly confronting MC-2.** Factor 6x²+11x+3. a·c = 6·3 = 18, need two numbers multiplying to 18 and summing to 11: 9 and 2. Split: 6x²+9x+2x+3 = 3x(2x+3)+1(2x+3) = (3x+1)(2x+3). Note the leading coefficient 6 was essential to finding the correct pair (9 and 2), not merely a bystander — this is the exact step MC-2 omits.
3. **Sum of squares versus difference of squares, side by side, directly confronting MC-3.** Factor x²−9 and attempt to factor x²+9. x²−9: discriminant of x²−9 (as ax²+bx+c with a=1,b=0,c=−9) is 0−4(1)(−9)=36>0, factors as (x−3)(x+3) — verify by expanding. x²+9: discriminant is 0−4(1)(9)=−36<0, confirming no real linear factors exist; forcing (x+3)(x−3) as an answer is checked by expansion to give x²−9, not x²+9, making the error concrete and self-detected rather than asserted.
4. **A nested difference of squares, demonstrating the recursion MC-1 misses at a higher degree.** Factor x⁴−81 completely. First pass: this is a difference of squares, (x²)²−9² = (x²−9)(x²+9). Second pass (the recursive check): is (x²−9) itself factorable? Yes — another difference of squares: (x−3)(x+3). Is (x²+9) factorable? No — discriminant −36<0, a genuine sum of squares, irreducible. Final answer: (x−3)(x+3)(x²+9) — three factors, only two of them linear, one genuinely irreducible.

## Discovery Questions
- "You just extracted the GCF from a polynomial and got a quadratic left over. Is the job finished — or is there one more question you should ask about that leftover piece?" — surfaces MC-1 directly, inviting the learner to state the missing check rather than being told it.
- "For 6x²+11x+3, if you only look at the 3 (the constant term) when guessing factor pairs, ignoring the 6, do you think you'll find the right pair? What role might the 6 need to play?" — surfaces MC-2 by making the omission concrete before the ac-method is named.
- "Here are x²−9 and x²+9, side by side. They look almost the same. Multiply out (x−3)(x+3) — which of the two originals does it actually match? What does that tell you about trying the same trick on the other one?" — surfaces MC-3 through self-checking computation rather than a stated rule.
- "You factored x⁴−81 once and got (x²−9)(x²+9). Take a close look at each of those two pieces separately — does either of them look like something you already know how to factor further?" — surfaces the recursion requirement using the exact worked demonstration above, before it is named as a general rule.

## Teaching Sequence
1. **Anchor**: connect to the already-secure `math.alg.polynomial-operations` — factoring is un-multiplying; if you can expand (x+3)(x−4) to x²−x−12, you already understand what factoring is asking for in reverse.
2. **Establish the GCF-first rule as non-negotiable**: demonstrate a polynomial where skipping the GCF makes every later step harder or impossible (e.g. attempting the ac-method directly on 6x²+14x+4 without first extracting 2, versus extracting 2 first to get the far simpler 2(3x²+7x+2)).
3. **Teach the term-count classification**: 2 terms → check difference/sum of squares or cubes; 3 terms → check perfect-square trinomial, else apply the ac-method; 4 terms → try grouping. Use Demonstration 1-3 above as the worked instances for each branch.
4. **Explicitly install the discriminant as the irreducibility test**: not a special extra topic, but the tool that finishes the "is this residual factorable?" question with certainty (Demonstration 3).
5. **Install the recursion habit**: after every factoring step, the explicit question "can any resulting factor be factored again?" — practiced directly against Demonstration 4's nested example.
6. **Practice mixed problems** deliberately combining GCF + trinomial, GCF + difference of squares, and a genuine non-monic ac-method case, always requiring the full recursive check before an answer is accepted as final.
7. **Bridge forward**: state explicitly that a fully factored linear factor (x−a) corresponds to a root a of the polynomial — the direct preview of `math.alg.polynomial-roots`.

## Tutor Actions
- After any submitted "final" factored answer, ask "is every piece of your answer either a single x-term (linear) or a quadratic you've checked can't be split further?" before accepting it — this single question, asked every time, is the direct structural defence against MC-1.
- When a learner is stuck on a non-monic trinomial, ask "what do you get when you multiply the leading coefficient and the constant term together?" before offering the ac-method by name — this makes the learner discover that a·c (not c alone) is the number that matters, pre-empting MC-2.
- When a learner produces a sum-of-squares "factorisation," ask them to expand their answer back out and compare it, term by term, to the original — the mismatch is self-evident and more durable than being told the rule.
- Never accept "it doesn't factor" as a final answer without the discriminant computation shown — an unverified "doesn't factor" claim can hide an unfound GCF or an unattempted classification, not genuine irreducibility.

## Voice Teaching Notes
- The phrase "keep asking" should be spoken with genuine rhythm on multi-step problems — "GCF... then what's left... can THAT be split... keep asking" — so the recursive habit is heard as a repeated refrain, not read once as a rule.
- When demonstrating the ac-method, say the product "a times c" aloud explicitly before naming any numbers — "six times three is eighteen; what two numbers multiply to eighteen and add to eleven?" — so the leading coefficient is heard entering the calculation, directly countering MC-2's tendency to drop it silently.
- When contrasting x²−9 and x²+9 aloud, pause audibly on the operator: "x squared, MINUS nine" versus "x squared, PLUS nine" — spoken emphasis on the single differing symbol matches the Blueprint's own diagnosis that the error is perceptual (Type 2), so the repair should also be perceptual (audible), not only written.

## Assessment Signals
- **Correct + fast + explains the recursive check unprompted** → MASTERED; ready for `math.alg.polynomial-roots`.
- **Correct but stops after one factoring step without checking the residual** → MC-1 active; needs the two-part discipline repair (extract, then explicitly test the residual) before advancing.
- **Correct on monic trinomials only, fails or freezes on non-monic ones** → MC-2 active; needs the ac-method taught as the default, not the fallback.
- **Attempts to factor a sum of squares into real linear factors** → MC-3 active; needs the discriminant test installed as a computational habit, not a memorised visual rule.
- **Cannot identify GCF at all** → prerequisite gap in `math.alg.polynomial` (term/coefficient vocabulary), not a factoring-specific misconception; route back to that concept rather than re-teaching factoring technique.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If a learner has just been told a factoring answer is "incomplete" (MC-1) and shows frustration, do not immediately supply the missing factor — instead re-ask the exact discriminant-free version of the discovery question ("is there one more question you should ask about that leftover piece?") to keep ownership of the discovery with the learner, and only supply the ac-method mechanics if a second attempt still fails. If a non-monic trinomial produces repeated failed guesses (MC-2), stop the guessing immediately (guessing without the ac-method's structure wastes turns and builds frustration with no learning payoff) and pivot directly to "let's compute a times c together" — this is one of the few points in this concept where direct instruction of the mechanical step, not further discovery prompting, is the right recovery move, because the ac-method is a genuinely non-obvious algorithm rather than a re-derivable insight.

## Memory Hooks
- "GCF first, always" — a short, repeatable rule stated before every factoring problem, installing the non-negotiable first step as a reflex.
- "a times c, not c alone" — the specific phrase that directly targets MC-2's omission.
- "Minus splits, plus doesn't" — a compact, checkable-by-discriminant memory hook distinguishing difference of squares (factors) from sum of squares (does not), explicitly flagged to the learner as a starting intuition to be VERIFIED by the discriminant, not trusted blindly (since the hook itself is a simplification of the general b²−4ac test).
- "Keep asking: can this be split again?" — the recursion habit, stated as a repeatable question rather than a one-time instruction.

- **Chemistry** (`chem.` stoichiometric/equilibrium expressions): factoring a quadratic arising from an equilibrium-constant expression (e.g. solving for [X] in an ICE table) uses the identical ac-method and discriminant-based irreducibility check taught here — the algebra is not chemistry-specific, and a learner who has internalised the decision tree transfers it without re-learning.
