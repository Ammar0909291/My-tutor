## Learning Objective
- The learner can correctly identify the outer function $f$ and inner function $g$ of a composition $(f\circ g)(x)$, and apply the Chain Rule $(f\circ g)'(x)=f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$ — differentiating the outer function, evaluated at the INNER function (not at bare $x$), and multiplying by the inner function's own derivative.
- The learner never omits the inner-function-derivative factor $g'(x)$, recognizing that stopping after $f'(g(x))$ alone is an incomplete application of the rule, not a shortcut.
- The learner can distinguish a composition (chain rule needed) from a product (product rule needed) — recognizing that "the variable is in the exponent/argument of another function" signals composition, while "two separate multiplied factors" signals a product.

## Core Understanding
The Chain Rule differentiates a COMPOSITION $(f\circ g)(x)=f(g(x))$ — one function nested inside another — via $(f\circ g)'(x)=f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$: differentiate the OUTER function $f$ as usual, but evaluate that derivative AT the inner function $g(x)$ (not at bare $x$), then multiply by the inner function's OWN derivative $g'(x)$. This second factor, $g'(x)$, is the single most commonly OMITTED piece — a student who correctly computes $f'(g(x))$ and stops has captured only how the outer function responds to a change in its input, while the inner function's own rate of change (how fast that input itself is changing) must ALSO be multiplied in. Whether to apply the Chain Rule at all hinges on correctly diagnosing the expression's STRUCTURE first: a composition (the variable sits inside another function's argument or exponent, e.g. $\sin(x^2)$ or $e^{3x}$) needs the Chain Rule, while a PRODUCT (two separately multiplied factors, e.g. $x\cdot\sin x$) needs the Product Rule instead — these are structurally different operations, and applying the wrong rule to the wrong structure produces a meaningless computation, not merely an inefficient one.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — "differentiate the outside, then differentiate the inside, and multiply."** A two-step recipe followed mechanically, often without correctly identifying WHICH function is outside and which is inside. *Upgrade trigger*: getting an answer that's missing a factor, discovered by checking the derivative numerically at a specific point.
2. **Intermediate — "identify $f$ (outer) and $g$ (inner) explicitly first; compute $f'(g(x))$, then multiply by $g'(x)$ — never skip the second factor."** The two-factor structure is now explicit and checked deliberately. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to distinguish a genuine composition from a product that merely LOOKS similar (e.g. $\sin(x)\cdot x^2$ versus $\sin(x^2)$).
3. **Advanced — "is the variable inside another function's argument (composition, chain rule) or is it one of several separately multiplied factors (product, product rule)? Diagnose the structure BEFORE choosing a rule."** The rule-selection decision now precedes the rule's mechanical application. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to apply the Chain Rule TWICE or more, for a triple (or deeper) composition.
4. **Expert — the Chain Rule composes smoothly: for $h(x)=f(g(u(x)))$, differentiate outermost-to-innermost, multiplying a NEW derivative factor at each layer, exactly following the composition's own nested structure.** Implicit differentiation, related rates, and integration by substitution are all recognized as direct applications or inversions of this same multiplicative-factor idea. *Shelf life*: permanent.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant and single highest-frequency failure — named explicitly by the Blueprint as the concept's foundational misconception, propagating forward into implicit differentiation, related rates, and integration by substitution — is a straightforward overgeneralization of the simpler differentiation rules already mastered: having practiced rules where "differentiate the function" is the WHOLE task (power rule, sum rule), a learner correctly computes $f'(g(x))$ (the outer derivative, evaluated at the inner function) and stops, never multiplying by the inner function's own derivative $g'(x)$ — treating a composition as if it required only ONE differentiation step, the way a simple function does (MC-1, INNER-DERIVATIVE-MISSING). A second, related failure is a habitual carryover of the ordinary "plug in $x$" evaluation pattern: rather than evaluating the outer function's derivative AT the inner function $g(x)$ (as the Chain Rule requires), a learner evaluates it at bare $x$ instead, as if $g(x)$ were not genuinely nested inside — an overgeneralization of the standard evaluate-at-$x$ habit into a setting where the correct evaluation point is itself a function, not the variable (MC-2, OUTER-EVALUATED-AT-X). A third failure is a structural misclassification: mistaking a PRODUCT of two functions for a COMPOSITION, and applying the Chain Rule where the Product Rule is actually required — an overgeneralization of "this looks like two functions combined, use the Chain Rule" without first checking whether the combination is genuinely nested (composition) or merely multiplied (product) (MC-3, CHAIN-RULE-FOR-PRODUCTS).

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Misconception Registry (MC-1, MC-2, MC-3) and its own repair guidance embedded in the Scaffolding Protocol. **The Blueprint's Misconception Registry carries a Trigger column but no explicit birth-type column**; all three classifications below are independently assigned here.

- **MC-1 — INNER-DERIVATIVE-MISSING** (the Blueprint's own explicitly declared "Foundational Misconception" — named as propagating forward into implicit differentiation, related rates, and integration by substitution)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization of the simpler differentiation rules (power rule, sum rule) already mastered, where "differentiate the function" is the complete task — a composition genuinely requires a SECOND multiplicative factor that those simpler rules never needed.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: writing $f'(g(x))$ as the complete final answer, with no $g'(x)$ factor multiplied in.
  - **Detection probe** (parallels `math.calc.derivative-rules`' own MC-2, COEFFICIENT-MULTIPLICATION-OMITTED — the identical "a required second factor is dropped" mechanism, recurring here at the composition level): evaluate the derivative of $(4x^3+1)^7$; a learner who writes only $7(4x^3+1)^6$, omitting the inner derivative $12x^2$, demonstrates MC-1.
  - **Repair**: the Blueprint's own numerical-comparison repair technique — compute the derivative BOTH with and without the inner-derivative factor at a specific test point, showing the two answers are NOT equal, giving the learner "a concrete failure" (the Blueprint's own phrase) rather than an abstract rule reminder.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh composition, the learner explicitly writes BOTH factors — $f'(g(x))$ and $g'(x)$ — as separate pieces before multiplying, rather than writing a single combined expression from memory.

- **MC-2 — OUTER-EVALUATED-AT-X** (the Blueprint's own second registered misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization of the ordinary "evaluate the derivative at $x$" habit from simple (non-composed) differentiation, carried over into a setting where the correct evaluation point is the INNER function $g(x)$, not bare $x$.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: writing $f'(x)$ instead of $f'(g(x))$ — differentiating the outer function correctly in form, but evaluating it at the wrong argument.
  - **Detection probe**: evaluate the derivative of $e^{\sin x}$; a learner who writes $e^x\cdot\cos x$ (evaluating the exponential's derivative at bare $x$ rather than at $\sin x$) demonstrates MC-2, versus the correct $e^{\sin x}\cdot\cos x$.
  - **Repair**: the 5-step identification checklist's own explicit ordering — differentiate $f$ to get $f'(u)$ WITHOUT substituting $x$ back in yet, THEN evaluate at $u=g(x)$ as a deliberately separate step — preventing the premature $x$-substitution that MC-2 represents.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh composition, the learner writes the outer derivative in terms of a placeholder (e.g. "$u$") before substituting $g(x)$ for $u$, rather than substituting $x$ directly into the outer derivative's formula.

- **MC-3 — CHAIN-RULE-FOR-PRODUCTS** (the Blueprint's own third registered misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization — misclassifying a PRODUCT structure as a COMPOSITION structure, and applying the Chain Rule where the Product Rule is required. This is the mirror-image confusion of `math.calc.derivative-rules`' own MC-3 (DISTRIBUTING-DERIVATIVE-OVER-PRODUCTS, applying an additive/simple rule to a product) and `math.calc.product-rule`'s own MC-1 (DERIVATIVE-DISTRIBUTED-OVER-PRODUCT) — all three are instances of applying the WRONG rule to a product's structure, this time by reaching for the Chain Rule instead of simplifying incorrectly or omitting a factor.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: attempting to apply the Chain Rule's outer-inner decomposition to an expression like $x\cdot\sin x$, which is a PRODUCT of two separately multiplied factors, not a composition.
  - **Detection probe** (the Blueprint's own A03 diagnostic question): "is the variable in the exponent/argument of another function, or is it a separate multiplicative factor?" — a learner who cannot correctly answer this for a given expression, or who applies outer-inner decomposition to a genuine product, demonstrates MC-3.
  - **Repair**: the Blueprint's own chain-rule-vs-product-rule diagnostic table, applied explicitly to the expression in question BEFORE any differentiation rule is chosen — structure diagnosis always precedes rule selection.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh expression, the learner states "composition, Chain Rule" or "product, Product Rule" (with a brief justification referencing the expression's structure) before writing any derivative.

## Analogies
- **Best — nested Russian dolls, versus two separate toys sitting side by side.** A composition ($f(g(x))$) is one doll nested INSIDE another — you must account for both the outer doll's own size-change AND how the inner doll's size-change affects it. Two separate toys side by side ($f(x)\cdot g(x)$) is a product — each toy's own size matters independently, combined by the Product Rule instead.
- **Alternative — a car's speed depends on the engine's RPM, and the engine's RPM depends on the accelerator pedal position.** The car's speed's rate of change with respect to PEDAL POSITION is the rate-of-change-with-RPM times the rate-of-change-of-RPM-with-pedal — two multiplicative "gear ratios" chained together, exactly like $f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "the Chain Rule is just differentiating twice."** This vague phrasing licenses MC-1 directly, since it suggests two SEPARATE differentiation results rather than one MULTIPLIED together. Say "the Chain Rule multiplies two rates of change chained together — the outer rate at the inner value, TIMES the inner rate" instead.

## Demonstrations
- **The missing-factor numerical check.** Compute the derivative of $(4x^3+1)^7$ at $x=1$ both with the inner-derivative factor included and without it. *Predict whether omitting the factor will change the numerical answer, and by how much, before computing both.* Getting a value that is off by EXACTLY the missing factor of $g'(1)$ is the demonstration for MC-1 — "a concrete failure," per the Blueprint's own phrasing.
- **The wrong-evaluation-point contrast.** Compute the derivative of $e^{\sin x}$ evaluating the exponential's derivative at $\sin x$ (correct) versus at bare $x$ (MC-2). *Predict whether the two will agree at $x=0$ before computing.* At $x=0$, $\sin(0)=0$, so the two computations coincidentally AGREE there — deliberately choose a SECOND test point (e.g. $x=\pi/2$) where they visibly diverge, to make the error unmistakable.
- **The structure-diagnosis sort.** Present a mixed list of expressions ($\sin(x^2)$, $x\cdot\sin x$, $e^{3x}$, $x^2\cdot e^x$) and sort each as "composition, Chain Rule" or "product, Product Rule" BEFORE differentiating any of them. *Predict the sort before differentiating.* Correctly separating compositions from products is the demonstration for MC-3.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction is the argued call for the Chain Rule's formula itself (it is a specific consequence of the limit definition of the derivative applied to compositions, not independently rediscoverable at this level via guided exploration alone), but the missing-inner-derivative-factor's numerical consequence (MC-1) and the composition-versus-product structural distinction (MC-3) are both genuinely discoverable by direct comparison.
1. **Need** — "Compute the derivative of $(2x)^3$ two ways: first by expanding $(2x)^3=8x^3$ directly, then via the Chain Rule pattern $3(2x)^2\cdot(\text{something})$. What does 'something' need to be for the two answers to match?" The learner discovers the missing factor must be $2$ (the inner derivative).
2. **Playground** — try the same expand-versus-chain-rule comparison on a couple more simple compositions where direct expansion is feasible.
3. **Invention** — "Why does the Chain Rule need this extra multiplied factor, when the simpler differentiation rules didn't?" Let the learner connect it to the inner function's OWN rate of change mattering, not just the outer function's.
4. **Collision** — confront a learner who omitted the inner-derivative factor with the direct numerical mismatch from the missing-factor demonstration.
5. **Formalisation** — state the Chain Rule explicitly, $(f\circ g)'(x)=f'(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$, with the 5-step identification checklist.
6. **Compression** — "Outer's derivative, AT the inner — TIMES the inner's own derivative. Never stop after the first part."

## Teaching Sequence
Per the Blueprint's own explicit "Leibniz first, then Lagrange" sequencing rationale, the concrete substitution derivation ($u=x^2$, $y=\sin u$) precedes the abstract formula, since the concrete substitution is structurally more transparent for first exposure. The inner-derivative-factor discipline (MC-1) and the correct-evaluation-point discipline (MC-2) are established TOGETHER in the initial worked-example pair, since both are consequences of the SAME formula and separating them risks a learner fixing one error while the other persists unnoticed. The composition-versus-product structural diagnosis (MC-3) is introduced LAST, once the mechanical application is fluent, per the Blueprint's own A03 — since a learner who cannot yet reliably APPLY the Chain Rule is not ready to additionally judge WHETHER it applies. The double/triple chain rule extension and the implicit-differentiation preview both follow the single-composition case, as direct extensions rather than new conceptual ground. Turn-level scripts for A01–A04 are owned by the Blueprint's Component 5 (Scaffolding Protocol) and are not restated here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Worked Example** — the missing-factor numerical check ($(4x^3+1)^7$ at $x=1$, with and without the inner-derivative factor), with the learner predicting whether omission changes the answer BEFORE either version is computed. First action; anchors the two-factor requirement concretely.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "Is this expression a composition (variable inside another function) or a product (two separate multiplied factors)?" asked on a fresh mixed set, BEFORE any differentiation rule is applied. Surfaces MC-3 in one turn.
- **DO: Demonstration** — the wrong-evaluation-point contrast ($e^{\sin x}$ evaluated at $\sin x$ versus at bare $x$), run at a test point deliberately chosen to make the disagreement visible (not $x=0$, where the two coincidentally agree).

## Assessment Signals
- **Writes $f'(g(x))$ as the complete final answer, with no $g'(x)$ factor multiplied in** — MC-1. Route to the missing-factor numerical check, on the exact composition in question.
- **Evaluates the outer function's derivative at bare $x$ rather than at the inner function $g(x)$** — MC-2. Route to the wrong-evaluation-point contrast, on the exact composition in question.
- **Applies outer-inner decomposition to a genuine product, rather than the Product Rule** — MC-3. Route to the structure-diagnosis sort, on the exact expression in question.
- **Correctly identifies composition versus product, applies the two-factor Chain Rule with the correct evaluation point** — the intended target state.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance here is "I got $f'(g(x))$, isn't that the derivative?" — a reasonable question given that $f'(g(x))$ genuinely IS a correctly-computed piece of the answer, just not the complete one. The concept-specific smaller question returns to a concrete numerical check: **"Let's test your answer at $x=1$. Compute it, then compute the derivative a SECOND way — by expanding the expression directly if possible, or checking with a slightly different $x$-value nearby. Do the two agree?"** The learner discovers the numerical mismatch themselves. Then return: "the outer derivative $f'(g(x))$ is genuinely part of the answer — but the inner function is ALSO changing as $x$ changes, and that rate, $g'(x)$, has to be multiplied in too." If the frustration is instead about identifying outer versus inner, shrink to the bare check: **"What operation happens LAST if you were computing this by hand, step by step, for a specific number? That last operation is the outer function."** Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Memory Hooks
- Concept type: **procedure with two embedded precision requirements** (correct outer/inner identification and the complete two-factor multiplication are both procedural precision points, and the composition-versus-product structural diagnosis is a conceptual judgment, all three needed simultaneously for a correct application). Review by *requiring the learner to write BOTH factors — $f'(g(x))$ and $g'(x)$ — as visibly separate pieces before combining them*, never accepting a bare final derivative, since skipping the visible two-factor step lets MC-1 pass undetected on problems where a learner has memorized the FORM of a specific answer without genuinely deriving it.
- Concept-specific deviation: keep at least one expression in the review rotation that LOOKS like a composition but is genuinely a product (e.g. $x\cdot e^x$, easily mistaken for $e^{x\cdot(\text{something})}$-style composition at a glance), so the structure-diagnosis habit (MC-3) doesn't atrophy into pattern-matching on surface appearance alone.
