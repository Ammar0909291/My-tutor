## Learning Objective
- The learner can execute the full four-step substitution procedure — isolate, substitute into the other equation, solve, back-substitute — treating back-substitution as a mandatory final step, never optional cleanup.
- The learner can correctly interpret a FALSE numerical statement reached during substitution (e.g. 2 = −4) as the definitive signal that the system is inconsistent and has no solution.
- The learner can correctly interpret a TRUE numerical statement independent of the variable (e.g. 6 = 6) as the definitive signal that the system is dependent and has infinitely many solutions — and, critically, never confuse this outcome with the opposite (no-solution) case.

## Core Understanding
Substitution is a complete, four-step procedure, and each step depends on the one before it in a way that makes skipping any step produce an incomplete or wrong answer: isolate one variable in one equation, substitute that expression into the *other* equation (never the one it came from), solve the resulting single-variable equation, then back-substitute the found value to recover the second variable — a solution to a two-variable system is always a pair, and a procedure that stops after finding only the first value has not actually finished. The two special outcomes this concept exists to sharpen are the moments substitution produces no variable at all, leaving a bare numerical statement — and the entire content of this concept is the discipline of reading that bare statement correctly rather than treating both cases as interchangeable "something went wrong" signals. A FALSE statement (2 = −4) is not an error to fix; it is the algebra correctly reporting that the two original equations, substituted into each other, are mutually incompatible — geometrically, two parallel lines that never meet, so no pair of values can satisfy both simultaneously. A TRUE statement (6 = 6) is the opposite kind of correct report: the algebra has discovered that the two equations were never actually independent constraints at all — one was a disguised restatement of the other, describing the identical line, so *every* point that satisfies one automatically satisfies both, giving infinitely many solutions rather than zero. These two outcomes look structurally similar (the variable has vanished in both cases) but mean opposite things, which is exactly why they are taught side by side here rather than in isolation.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — isolate, swap, solve, then go back for the other letter.** y = 2x+1 and 3x+y=11: swap the first equation's expression into the second, solve for x, then plug x back into the first equation to get y. *Upgrade trigger*: a system where the variable cancels out entirely during substitution, leaving no equation to solve at all — "solve, then go back" has no next step when there's nothing left to solve for. *Shelf life*: one session.
2. **Intermediate — when the variable disappears, read what's left: true or false decides everything.** A false leftover (2 = −4) means no solution exists; a true leftover (6 = 6) means every point on the line is a solution. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to justify *why* these two outcomes are opposite rather than simply memorising which is which. *Shelf life*: durable and remains correct permanently.
3. **Advanced — a false statement proves the two equations describe non-intersecting (parallel) lines; a true statement proves they describe the identical line.** The bare arithmetic outcome is a direct report on the geometric relationship between the two original equations, not an arbitrary rule to apply. *Upgrade trigger*: encountering the elimination method's identical 0=k / 0=0 outcomes, recognising the same underlying geometric fact expressed through a different algebraic route.
4. **Expert — substitution is a special case of variable elimination, and the "variable vanishes" outcome is not a failure mode of the method but a genuinely informative third answer, exactly as meaningful as finding a unique solution.** *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the model that treats "no solution" and "infinitely many solutions" as first-class answers a solving method can correctly report, not exceptions requiring a different tool.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant failure, and the one this concept is specifically built to correct, is misreading which of the two disappearing-variable outcomes signals which conclusion: reaching a false statement like 2 = −4, the learner either misinterprets the numbers (attempting, nonsensically, to solve "2 = −4" for a variable that is no longer present) or simply becomes confused and abandons the problem, rather than recognising the false statement itself as the complete and definitive answer — no solution exists, full stop, nothing further needs to be computed. The mirror-image failure occurs at the true-statement outcome: reaching 6 = 6, the learner concludes "no solution," directly inverting the correct reading, because both outcomes share the superficial feature of "the variable vanished" and, without a deliberate side-by-side contrast, that shared feature is treated as the meaningful signal rather than the shared feature being a coincidence of *how* the variable vanished (into a falsehood versus into a truth). A third, more mundane failure occurs even in the ordinary case where a genuine unique solution exists: having correctly solved for the first variable, the learner reports that single value as the complete answer, omitting the back-substitution step needed to find the second coordinate — treating the harder, more interesting part of the procedure (isolate and substitute) as the whole task and the final step as unnecessary cleanup.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1..MC-3) and its repair actions B01–B03, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — INCONSISTENT-SYSTEM-FALSE-STATEMENT-MISINTERPRETED** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation — every prior equation this learner has solved was solvable, so a bare false numerical statement has no established meaning in their experience and is treated as an error rather than a conclusive result.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: reaching 2 = −4 during substitution and either misreading it as an equation to solve or abandoning the problem without a stated conclusion.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): present Example 2 and check whether "no solution" is correctly concluded from 2 = −4.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — restate explicitly: a false statement with no variable remaining means the system has NO solution; connect this directly to the parallel-lines geometric picture.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh system that reduces to a false statement, the learner states "no solution" immediately and confidently, without hesitation or attempted further algebra.

- **MC-2 — DEPENDENT-SYSTEM-TRUE-STATEMENT-CONFUSED-WITH-NO-SOLUTION** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of MC-1's own (correct) association "variable vanished → no solution," extended past its actual scope to cover the structurally similar but logically opposite true-statement case.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: reaching 6 = 6 and concluding "no solution" rather than "infinitely many solutions."
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): present Example 3 and check whether "no solution" is (incorrectly) concluded from 6 = 6.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — re-derive by explicitly checking that the second equation is a scalar multiple of the first, confirming both describe the identical line with every point shared.
  - **Verification of death**: given both a false-statement and a true-statement system in the same session, the learner distinguishes them correctly and confidently, without cross-contamination between the two.

- **MC-3 — BACK-SUBSTITUTION-STEP-OMITTED** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of "I solved the equation" (true for the single-variable equation reached mid-procedure) into "I solved the system" (false, since a system's solution is a pair, and only one coordinate has been found).
  - **Characteristic phrase**: solving for x and reporting "x = 2" as the complete solution, with no value for y.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): review a submitted solution for a missing second coordinate.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — re-walk the back-substitution step explicitly as a required final step, not optional cleanup, immediately after any single-variable equation is solved.
  - **Verification of death**: given a new system, the learner reports the final answer as an ordered pair unprompted, with both coordinates present.

## Analogies
- **Best — a question with a real, complete answer, even when that answer is "impossible" or "always."** Asking "for what x does 2 equal −4" is not a broken question needing repair — it has a genuine, complete answer ("no such x, ever"), exactly as asking "for what x does 6 equal 6" has the genuine, complete answer "every x, always." This directly grounds both MC-1's and MC-2's repairs in a single reframing.
- **Alternative — two rulers, compared.** A false leftover statement is like discovering two supposedly identical rulers actually measure differently at every point (they can never agree — no solution); a true leftover is like discovering they are actually the same physical ruler, relabeled (they agree everywhere — infinite solutions).
- **Story analogy** — two people claiming contradictory facts about the same date (a false leftover, meaning no scenario satisfies both claims) versus two people restating the identical fact in different words (a true leftover, meaning every scenario consistent with the fact satisfies both claims automatically).
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "if the variable disappears, something went wrong — start over."** This is exactly MC-1's mechanism, treating a genuinely informative outcome as an error state; the correct frame is "the variable disappearing is itself the answer — read what remains."
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "no variable left means no solution, always."** This is precisely MC-2's overgeneralised rule; the correct, more precise frame distinguishes the truth value of what remains, not merely whether a variable remains.

## Demonstrations
- **The false-versus-true side-by-side.** Work Example 2 (false, 2=−4) and Example 3 (true, 6=6) in direct succession, pausing explicitly at each disappearing-variable moment to ask "true or false — and what does that mean?" before revealing the answer. *Predict, before evaluating, whether each outcome will be true or false* — the paired, immediate contrast is the demonstration, operationalising Blueprint A02's design directly.
- **The parallel-versus-identical-line check.** For a false-statement system, compute and compare the two lines' slopes and intercepts, showing same slope, different intercept (parallel); for a true-statement system, show the second equation is literally a scalar multiple of the first (identical line). This grounds both misconceptions' repairs in a visual, checkable geometric fact rather than an abstract rule.
- **The back-substitution completeness check.** After solving for the first variable, pause explicitly and ask "is this the complete answer, or only half of it?" before allowing the learner to move on — a standing checkpoint that directly operationalises Blueprint B03's repair.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for the four-step procedure itself — isolate, substitute into the OTHER equation, solve, back-substitute is a direct, already-secured extension of `math.alg.system-linear-equations`' own content, not new material to discover. What is genuinely discoverable, and is this concept's entire distinguishing purpose, is the true-versus-false interpretation: (1) **Need** — "Solve y=3x+2 and y=3x−4 by substitution. What happens to x?" (2) **Playground** — work through several systems where the variable cancels, some to false statements and some to true, recording each outcome and, separately, each pair of lines' actual geometric relationship (parallel or identical). (3) **Invention** — "What's the rule connecting what's left over (true or false) to how many solutions the system has?" (4) **Collision** — offer both a false-statement and a true-statement system back to back, and test a learner-proposed rule ("variable disappearing always means no solution") against the true-statement case, which it gets wrong. (5) **Formalisation** — a false leftover statement means no solution (the lines are parallel); a true leftover statement means infinitely many solutions (the lines are identical). (6) **Compression** — "Variable gone? Check what's left. Lie → nothing works. Truth → everything works."

## Teaching Sequence
The complete four-step procedure (TA-A01) is established first, on a system with a genuine unique solution, so the back-substitution habit is secure before either disappearing-variable case is introduced — a learner who has not yet internalised "the answer is always a pair" has no reliable framework for correctly handling a case where the procedure produces something other than a pair at all. The false-versus-true contrast (TA-A02, targeting both MC-1 and MC-2 simultaneously) is deliberately taught as one combined lesson rather than two separate ones, per the Blueprint's own explicit design, because MC-1 and MC-2 are a single confusion viewed from two directions, and separating their instruction would remove the direct juxtaposition that most reliably prevents the confusion from forming in the first place. The Blueprint's own Component 5 sequence (TA-A01 full four-step procedure via conceptual shift, TA-A02 false-versus-true contrast pair, TA-A03 mastery gate) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Worked example, the full four-step procedure** — narrated with each step explicitly labelled, back-substitution treated with the same weight as the earlier steps, not glossed over.
- **TEST-THINKING: Contrast pair, false-versus-true** — the Blueprint's own paired examples, run as a direct side-by-side comparison in one sitting rather than presented across separate lessons.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "will this be true or false, and what will that mean?" asked at every disappearing-variable moment, before the arithmetic is evaluated.
- **Does NOT fit: teaching the false-statement case and the true-statement case in separate sessions, days apart.** Per the Blueprint's own Teaching Notes, this is exactly the structure most likely to produce MC-2, since the direct contrast that prevents the confusion depends on temporal proximity.
- **Does NOT fit: presenting only systems with a unique solution during initial procedure instruction.** This never exercises the back-substitution-omission risk in a context where the variable might otherwise vanish, and never gives the learner practice distinguishing the ordinary case from the two special ones.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "true or false — that's the whole answer." Slow down on "whole," emphasising that no further algebra is needed once the truth value is determined. Listen for a learner who, upon reaching a bare numerical statement, continues attempting further algebraic manipulation (trying to "solve" 2=−4) rather than stopping and stating a conclusion — the continued manipulation predicts MC-1's confusion. For MC-2 specifically, listen for whether the learner's stated conclusion matches the geometric picture they can independently verify (are these lines actually the same, or actually parallel?) — a mismatch between the stated conclusion and an easily-checkable geometric fact is the clearest late-stage signal. A confident, unprompted report of BOTH coordinates as an ordered pair, without being asked "and y?", is the strongest positive signal for MC-3's repair. Channel reality is owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals
- **A false leftover statement (e.g. 2=−4) met with continued algebraic manipulation or an abandoned problem** — MC-1; route to the false-versus-true side-by-side, never to a bare restatement of "that means no solution."
- **A true leftover statement (e.g. 6=6) concluded as "no solution"** — MC-2, cleanly diagnostic and the mirror image of MC-1; route to the parallel-versus-identical-line check specifically.
- **A single coordinate reported as the complete solution to an ordinary (unique-solution) system** — MC-3; route to the back-substitution completeness check.
- **Both a false-statement and a true-statement outcome correctly and confidently distinguished within the same session** — the strongest positive signal available, indicating the two misconceptions' shared confusion has genuinely been resolved rather than one direction being memorised while the other remains fragile.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A03 gate, MAMR ⌈0.85×5⌉ = 5/5, including its P76 phone-plan transfer probe, whose part (b) requires interpreting a no-solution result in a genuine practical context — a gate passed without that applied-interpretation component does not certify that the false/true distinction transfers beyond drilled symbolic examples.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance is "the x disappeared and I don't know what to do" — a precise, honest description of exactly the moment this concept addresses. The concept-specific smaller question redirects to the truth-value check directly: **"Forget solving for anything. Just look at what's left — is it a true statement, or a false one?"** — isolating the one judgment (true or false) from any expectation that further algebra is needed. If the freeze is specifically about which conclusion follows from which truth value, shrink to the geometric picture: **"If two equations describe two different lines that never cross, could a single point satisfy both? What if they're actually the exact same line?"** — grounding the answer in an already-intuitive physical picture rather than a memorised pairing. Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.
