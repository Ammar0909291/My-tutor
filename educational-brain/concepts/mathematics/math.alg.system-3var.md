## Learning Objective
- The learner can solve a system of three equations in three unknowns via an elimination cascade — eliminating the same variable from two different pairs of the original equations to produce a genuine two-variable system, then solving that reduced system with already-mastered technique.
- The learner can correctly extend the two-variable case's three solution outcomes to three variables, recognising that "infinitely many solutions" is now richer than before: it can describe either a line (one redundant equation, one free parameter) or an entire plane (two redundant equations, two free parameters).
- The learner can recognise that a genuine contradiction found from any two of the three equations proves the whole system has no solution immediately, regardless of the third equation or which elimination order was chosen.

## Core Understanding
Solving three equations in three unknowns is not a new method but the two-variable elimination technique applied twice, in a specific, necessary sequence: eliminating one variable from a pair of the original three equations produces only a *system* of two equations in two unknowns, not yet a single number — a second elimination round, using a different pair of the original equations to eliminate the same variable again, is required to produce a second equation in those same two unknowns, and only once both reduced equations exist can the already-mastered two-variable technique finish the job. The three solution outcomes from the two-variable case all still apply, but the "infinitely many solutions" case gains genuine new richness with the extra dimension: because three variables allow for either one or two independent directions of freedom (rather than only one, as in the two-variable case), a system where exactly one of the three original equations turns out to be a redundant combination of the other two collapses to two independent equations and one free parameter — a *line* of solutions — while a system where two of the three equations are redundant collapses to only one independent equation and two free parameters — an entire *plane* of solutions, a possibility that simply had no room to exist with only two unknowns. The contradiction case extends most simply of all: if combining any two of the three equations produces a false numerical statement (0 = k, k ≠ 0), that alone proves the entire system has no solution, and this conclusion is completely independent of the third equation and of which variable-elimination order was chosen — a genuine contradiction, once found, cannot be undone or avoided by trying a different path through the algebra.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — eliminate a variable twice, then solve the leftover two-variable system like before.** Three equations become two (after one elimination round on two different pairs), then one number (after solving that reduced system). *Upgrade trigger*: stopping after only one elimination round, with a single reduced equation still carrying two unknowns — "eliminate, then solve like before" needs a second reduced equation before "like before" actually applies. *Shelf life*: one to two sessions.
2. **Intermediate — the same variable, eliminated from two different pairs, produces the two-variable system you already know how to finish.** Deliberately choosing which pairs to combine, and confirming both reductions target the identical variable, is what produces a genuinely solvable reduced system. *Upgrade trigger*: a system where two or all three equations turn out to be redundant, where "reduce to two, then solve" produces a degenerate (not fully independent) reduced system requiring the richer line-versus-plane interpretation. *Shelf life*: durable and remains correct permanently, once the two-round structure is secure.
3. **Advanced — count how many of the three original equations are actually redundant to know whether "infinitely many" means a line or a plane.** One redundant equation leaves one free parameter (a line); two redundant equations leave two free parameters (a plane). *Upgrade trigger*: needing to determine, efficiently and without a full elimination, whether a given system is likely degenerate at all — motivating a more systematic check.
4. **Expert — a 3×3 linear system's solution set (a point, a line, a plane, or empty) is fully determined by the rank of its coefficient structure relative to the number of unknowns, and a contradiction found anywhere is conclusive precisely because it reflects a structural property of the whole system, not an artefact of one particular elimination path.** *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the model this concept's own unlock (`math.linalg.row-reduction`) formalises completely, replacing ad hoc pair-selection with a systematic, column-by-column procedure.

## Why Students Fail
The most structurally damaging failure is stopping after a single elimination round: having eliminated one variable from one pair of the original three equations, the learner treats the single resulting equation (still carrying two unknowns) as already solvable, attempting to extract both remaining variables' values from one equation alone — a task that is simply impossible, since one linear equation in two unknowns has infinitely many solutions on its own and pins down nothing until paired with a second, independent equation in the same two unknowns. The second major failure is a direct, uncritical carry-over from the two-variable case: having correctly reached an "infinitely many solutions" conclusion, the learner assumes the solution set must be a line, exactly as it always was with two variables, without checking how many of the three original equations are actually redundant — missing that two redundant equations (rather than one) leave an entire second dimension of freedom, producing a plane rather than a line, a genuinely different geometric object the two-variable case never had room to produce. The third failure is one of trust rather than technique: having correctly found a contradiction (0 = k, k ≠ 0) from combining two of the three equations, the learner suspects a computational error was made — perhaps the wrong variable was eliminated first, or the wrong pair of equations was combined — and attempts to "double check" by retrying with a different elimination order, when in fact a genuine contradiction found anywhere is conclusive and cannot be escaped by choosing a different algebraic path.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1..MC-3) and its repair actions B01–B03, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — SINGLE-ELIMINATION-ROUND-ASSUMED-SUFFICIENT** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation — the two-variable case's elimination procedure (one round, then solved) is applied directly to the three-variable case without recognising that "reduce by one variable" and "reduce to a solvable form" are no longer the same event once a third unknown is present.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: given one reduced equation like 3x+2z=9, attempting to solve it directly for both x and z.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): ask a learner whether one reduced equation like 3x+2z=9 can already be solved for x and z — a "yes" answer confirms MC-1.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — re-walk a full two-round cascade explicitly, re-anchoring on "eliminate the SAME variable using TWO different pairs of the original equations — only then do you have a genuine two-variable system to solve."
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh 3×3 system, the learner performs two full elimination rounds, producing two reduced equations, before attempting to solve either.

- **MC-2 — INFINITE-SOLUTIONS-ASSUMED-ALWAYS-A-LINE** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation — the two-variable case's own single finding ("infinitely many" always means a line) is carried over unchanged into a context with an extra degree of freedom where a second possibility genuinely exists.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: describing any infinite-solution 3×3 system's solution set as "a line," without checking how many equations are actually redundant.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): ask whether the infinite-solutions case for a 3-variable system always means the solution set is a line, as in the 2-variable case — a "yes" answer confirms MC-2.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — re-walk the fully-redundant case (two equations redundant, two free parameters, a genuine plane), re-anchoring on "count how many equations are actually redundant — one gives a line, two gives a plane."
  - **Verification of death**: given both a one-redundant-equation and a two-redundant-equation system, the learner correctly identifies the line versus plane outcome for each, unprompted.

- **MC-3 — CONTRADICTION-TREATED-AS-METHOD-ERROR** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of a healthy general habit ("double-check unexpected results") applied to a result that is not actually uncertain — a genuine algebraic contradiction is treated as evidence of a mistake rather than as the intended, informative output of a correctly-executed step.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: reaching 0=2 from two equations and proposing to retry with a different variable-elimination order to confirm.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): ask a learner who has found 0=k (k≠0) from two equations whether they should retry with a different elimination order — a "yes" answer confirms MC-3.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — re-walk the direct contradiction, re-anchoring on "a genuine contradiction from ANY two equations is conclusive — the third equation and the choice of elimination order cannot change a 'no solution' conclusion."
  - **Verification of death**: given a contradiction found from one pair of equations, the learner states the system has no solution immediately and confidently, with no unprompted attempt to re-verify via a different pairing.

## Analogies
- **Best — narrowing a search in two independent steps.** Finding a specific book on a specific shelf in a specific room requires first narrowing to the room, then the shelf — one narrowing step alone (the room) still leaves a whole shelf's worth of books; both steps together pin down the exact book. This directly targets MC-1 by making "one reduction isn't enough" a familiar, everyday pattern.
- **Alternative — how many independent directions can you still move in?** A single point has zero freedom; a line has one direction you can freely move along; a plane has two independent directions. "Infinitely many solutions" only tells you freedom exists — counting the redundant equations tells you how many directions. This targets MC-2 directly.
- **Story analogy** — a jury reaching a definitive, case-closing verdict from just two pieces of decisive evidence: no amount of re-examining a third, irrelevant piece of evidence, or re-ordering the presentation, changes an already-conclusive verdict — directly modelling MC-3's repair.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "eliminate a variable and you've solved the system."** This licenses MC-1 by treating the entire multi-round cascade as a single step; the correct frame explicitly separates "reduce the equation count" from "reach a fully solved system."
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "infinitely many solutions just means a line, same as before."** This is exactly MC-2's error stated as a rule; the correct, extra-dimension-aware frame requires checking the count of redundant equations before naming the solution set's shape.

## Demonstrations
- **The two-round cascade, staged explicitly.** Work Example 1 live, visibly pausing after the first elimination round to ask "do I have a solvable system yet?" before performing the second round. *Predict, after one round, whether the remaining equation can already be solved* — the pause and the "not yet" answer is the demonstration, operationalising Blueprint B01's repair.
- **The line-versus-plane side-by-side.** Work a one-redundant-equation system (a line) directly beside a two-redundant-equation system (a plane), counting free parameters explicitly for each. *Predict, before counting, how many independent directions of freedom each system will have* — this operationalises Blueprint B02's repair.
- **The contradiction-is-final demonstration.** Find a contradiction from one pair of equations, then deliberately attempt the elimination again using a *different* pair and a different variable, showing the conclusion (no solution) is unchanged either way. This directly operationalises Blueprint B03's repair by proving, rather than merely asserting, that retrying does not help.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for the two-round cascade mechanics themselves, since they are a direct, structural extension of already-secured two-variable elimination, not new material to re-derive. What is genuinely discoverable, and central to this concept's own distinguishing content, is the line-versus-plane extension: (1) **Need** — "This system has infinitely many solutions. Is the solution set always a line, like it was with two variables?" (2) **Playground** — work through several 3×3 systems with different numbers of redundant equations (zero, one, two), recording how many free parameters each produces. (3) **Invention** — "What determines whether the solution set is a line or a plane?" (4) **Collision** — offer a two-redundant-equation system to a learner who has proposed "always a line," directly contradicting the rule. (5) **Formalisation** — one redundant equation leaves one free parameter (a line); two redundant equations leave two free parameters (a plane); the count of redundant equations determines the dimension of freedom. (6) **Compression** — "Count what's redundant. One extra freedom is a line. Two is a plane."

## Teaching Sequence
The two-round elimination cascade (TA-A01, targeting MC-1) is secured first, on a system with a genuine unique solution, before either degenerate outcome (line-versus-plane at TA-A02, or the contradiction case at TA-A03) is introduced — a learner who has not yet internalised that two full rounds are required cannot reliably distinguish a genuinely degenerate system from one where a simple procedural shortcut (stopping early) was mistaken for a degenerate outcome. The line-versus-plane extension is taught before the contradiction-is-final content, following the Blueprint's own worked-example ordering, because both degenerate cases (infinite solutions and no solution) share the surface feature of the target variable's coefficient vanishing during elimination, and establishing the *positive* degenerate case (infinite solutions, with its own richer sub-structure) first gives the contradiction case a clean contrast to be taught against. The Blueprint's own Component 5 sequence (TA-A01 two-round cascade via representation shift, TA-A02 line-versus-plane contrast pair, TA-A03 contradiction-is-conclusive via conflict evidence, TA-A04 mastery gate) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Demonstration** — the two-round cascade staged explicitly, with a visible pause-and-check after the first round, run on the learner's own system rather than a fixed example.
- **ORGANIZE: Free-parameter counting** — the line-versus-plane side-by-side, framed as literally counting independent directions of freedom, this concept's highest-value action for preventing MC-2.
- **TEST-THINKING: Conflict evidence** — the contradiction-is-final demonstration, run as a genuine repeated attempt with a different pairing rather than a stated assurance, per the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence design.
- **Does NOT fit: introducing the line-versus-plane distinction before the two-round cascade is independently fluent.** A learner still uncertain about basic cascade mechanics cannot reliably distinguish "I stopped too early" from "this system is genuinely degenerate."
- **Does NOT fit: presenting only systems with a unique solution before either degenerate case is introduced.** This never gives MC-2 or MC-3 a chance to surface during instruction, producing false confidence that every system reduces cleanly to a single point.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "one round gets you to two unknowns, not to an answer — you need a second round." Slow down on "second," every time the cascade is narrated. Listen for a learner who, after one elimination round, moves directly toward solving the single reduced equation for both variables at once, rather than pausing to note that a second equation in those same two unknowns is still needed — the unpaused move predicts MC-1 reliably. For the line-versus-plane distinction, listen for whether the learner counts redundant equations explicitly ("two of these three collapsed to the same thing") before naming the solution set's shape, versus reaching for "a line" reflexively. A confident, unprompted "let me just double-check this doesn't change with a different pair" followed by trying anyway (rather than accepting the contradiction outright) is the clearest live signal of MC-3 still active. Channel reality is owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals
- **A single reduced equation (still carrying two unknowns) treated as immediately solvable** — MC-1; route to the two-round cascade staged explicitly, never to a bare restatement of "you need two equations."
- **An infinite-solutions outcome automatically labelled "a line" without checking how many equations are redundant** — MC-2, cleanly diagnostic; route to the line-versus-plane side-by-side.
- **A found contradiction followed by an unprompted retry with a different elimination order or variable choice** — MC-3; route to the contradiction-is-final demonstration so the futility of retrying is directly experienced.
- **A correctly identified line-versus-plane distinction stated with an explicit free-parameter count as justification** — the strongest positive signal available, indicating MC-2's repair has genuinely taken hold rather than the correct label being guessed.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A04 gate, MAMR ⌈0.8×5⌉ = 4/5, including its P76 chemistry-conservation-equations transfer probe, whose part (c) specifically asks why row reduction becomes strongly preferred for larger systems — a gate passed without that scalability-reasoning component does not certify the forward motivation this concept is meant to build toward `math.linalg.row-reduction`.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance is "I eliminated one and now I'm stuck with two letters left" — a precise, honest description of exactly the MC-1 boundary. The concept-specific smaller question redirects to the missing second round: **"Right — that's one equation down, but you've still got two unknowns in it. Can you eliminate the SAME variable again, using a different pair of your original three equations?"** — making the missing step explicit and actionable rather than leaving the learner to guess what comes next. If the freeze is specifically about interpreting an infinite-solutions outcome, shrink to a direct count: **"How many of your three original equations turned out to say the same thing as another one? One, or two?"** — isolating the redundancy count from the broader geometric vocabulary. Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Memory Hooks
- Concept type: **procedure**, the two-variable elimination technique applied twice in a structured cascade, plus an embedded classification judgment (how many equations are redundant?) for the degenerate outcomes. Review by *application on mixed input* every cycle, including at least one unique-solution system, one line-outcome system, and one plane-outcome system.
- Concept-specific deviation: keep a plane-outcome (two-redundant-equation) system in permanent rotation specifically — it is the rarer, newer case this concept introduces, and ordinary practice sets skew toward unique-solution or simple line-outcome systems, letting the plane case decay fastest.

## Transfer Connections
- **Near**: `math.linalg.row-reduction` (this concept's own unlock — the systematic, matrix-based generalisation that removes the need to hand-plan which pairs of equations to combine).
- **Far**: any problem requiring three simultaneous linear constraints on three unknown quantities — mixture problems with three components, three-way resource allocation, or any scenario with three interacting linear relationships.
- **Real-world**: the Blueprint's own chemistry-conservation-equations transfer probe (balancing a reaction with three unknown reactant quantities governed by three conservation laws) is a genuine, standard application of exactly this technique.
- **Expert transfer**: the general linear-algebra principle that a system's solution structure (point, line, plane, or empty set) is determined by the relationship between the number of independent equations and the number of unknowns — the direct conceptual seed of the rank concept in full linear algebra.
