# math.alg.linear-equation-1var

## Identity

## Learning Objective
- The learner can solve ax + b = c by applying the SAME operation to both sides at every step, preserving equality throughout, never operating on one side alone.
- The learner can select an efficient isolation order — eliminating the constant term before dividing by the coefficient — to avoid unnecessary fraction arithmetic.
- The learner can correctly negate a moved term (the sign travels with the operation, never merely "with the move"), including under a negative coefficient.
- The learner can verify any candidate solution by substitution back into the original equation, as a compulsory closing step rather than an optional check.

## Core Understanding
A linear equation ax + b = c (a ≠ 0) is a statement that two expressions are equal for exactly one value of x, and solving it means finding that value by transforming the equation into progressively simpler equations that remain equally true at every step. The single invariant underneath every valid transformation is the balance principle: the equals sign asserts the two sides carry the same value right now, so any operation applied to only one side breaks that assertion, while an operation applied identically to both sides preserves it — this is not a rule imposed on top of equations, it is what "equals" already means, made procedural. The canonical isolation sequence — simplify each side, eliminate constant terms, isolate the variable term, divide by the coefficient, verify by substitution — is not five independent skills but one skill (apply the same operation to both sides) exercised in an order chosen for arithmetic convenience: subtracting the constant before dividing by the coefficient keeps intermediate values as whole numbers whenever possible, while the reverse order routinely manufactures fractions that carry no advantage and only add error surface. "Moving a term to the other side and flipping its sign" is a true *description* of what subtracting-from-both-sides looks like on paper, but taught as the primitive rule itself, it invites a physical, non-algebraic reading — a term does not travel and flip; a term is eliminated from one side by an inverse operation that is, by the balance principle, mirrored on the other side, and the "flip" is nothing but the visible trace of that mirrored operation.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — the balance scale.** Two pans in balance; removing (or adding) the same weight from both pans keeps them level. 3x + 5 = 20 is three boxes plus five loose weights, balanced against twenty loose weights; remove five from each pan, then split the remaining fifteen evenly across the three boxes. *Upgrade trigger*: a negative coefficient, where "remove weight" has no obvious physical reading for "divide by a negative number." *Shelf life*: one to two sessions, and it never becomes wrong, only insufficient on its own.
2. **Intermediate — every step is an operation on the WHOLE equation, not on one side.** Subtracting 5 from "the equation" 3x + 5 = 20 means subtracting 5 from both the left expression and the right expression simultaneously — it is one action with two visible effects, not two separate choices. *Upgrade trigger*: an equation requiring simplification (brackets, scattered like terms) before the balance operations can even begin, where "operate on the equation" gives no guidance about what must happen first, inside each side, before anything crosses the equals sign.
3. **Advanced — the isolation sequence is a strategy for arithmetic convenience, not a rigid law.** Eliminating constants before dividing by the coefficient is *usually* better because it tends to keep numbers as integers longer, but both valid orders reach the same correct answer; the sequence is optimization, not necessity. *Upgrade trigger*: an equation where the "usual" order is not actually the cleanest — e.g. 4(x + 2) = 20, where dividing by 4 first is strictly better because it also simplifies the bracket in one move.
4. **Expert — solving is inverse-function composition.** ax + b = c is the statement f(x) = c for f(x) = ax + b; solving is applying f⁻¹ to both sides: x = f⁻¹(c) = (c − b)/a. Every isolation step is literally undoing one layer of the function that was applied to x, in reverse order of how those layers were built up. *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the model that makes function composition and inverse functions feel like a restatement of something already deeply known, not a new topic.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant and most damaging failure is the balance principle's own literal violation: the learner performs a step on only one side, most often subtracting a constant from the left while leaving the right untouched — from 3x + 5 = 20, writing 3x = 20 rather than 3x = 15 — because the equation is being read and manipulated as a string of symbols to rearrange, not as a live claim of equality that any one-sided change would falsify. This is compounded by a related, more subtle failure once "moving terms" is taught as a shortcut without the balance principle underneath it: the learner treats crossing the equals sign as physical relocation and forgets that the operation performed (not merely the term's position) is what determines the sign — 3x + 5 = 20 becomes 3x = 20 + 5 rather than 3x = 20 − 5, because "moving" a term feels like it should not, on its own, change anything about the term itself. A third failure is a strategy error rather than a validity error: dividing by the coefficient before eliminating the constant — from 3x + 5 = 20, dividing by 3 first to get x + 5/3 = 20/3 — produces a technically valid but needlessly fraction-heavy path that increases the chance of a later arithmetic slip, without ever producing a wrong final answer by itself. All three failures share a root the Blueprint names directly: presenting "move it and flip the sign" as the primary taught rule, ahead of or instead of the balance principle, reliably manufactures the second failure even in learners who never make the first.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 2 registry (MC-1..MC-3) and its Component 5 repair actions B01–B02, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — BALANCE-NOT-MAINTAINED** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation — treating an equation as a symbol string to rearrange (a habit workable for pure expression simplification, where there is only one side) rather than as a claim of equality that a one-sided change would falsify.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: from 3x + 5 = 20, "3x = 20" (the constant removed from the left only).
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P49): "Solve: 2x + 3 = 11." A wrong answer traceable to a one-sided operation confirms MC-1.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — substitute the learner's own candidate answer into both the original and the one-sided-modified equation, showing the modified version is no longer true; reframe every step as "subtract 5 from the entire equation," never "remove 5 from the left."
  - **Verification of death**: given 5x + 10 = 30, the learner writes both sides of every step explicitly (5x + 10 − 10 = 30 − 10) without being asked to, unprompted.

- **MC-2 — WRONG-OPERATION-ORDER** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of PEMDAS read in reverse without understanding why the isolation sequence's order is a convenience choice, not an application of the order-of-operations rule itself.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: from 3x + 5 = 20, dividing by 3 first to reach x + 5/3 = 20/3, then struggling with the resulting fractions.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P49): "Which first step produces the cleanest solution to 4x − 12 = 8: divide by 4, or add 12 to both sides?" — choosing "divide by 4" with no recognition of the fraction cost is diagnostic.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02's isolation-sequence framing — demonstrate both valid orders side by side on the same equation, making the fraction-avoidance benefit of eliminating constants first directly visible rather than asserted as a rule.
  - **Verification of death**: given 6x + 9 = 27, the learner subtracts 9 before dividing by 6, unprompted, and can state why.

- **MC-3 — SIGN-ERROR-TRANSPOSING** (foundational)
  - **Birth type**: Type 4, notation-induced — "moving a term across the equals sign" is read as physical relocation of the term itself, with the sign change treated as an arbitrary extra step rather than as the visible trace of the actual operation (subtracting from both sides) that was performed.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: from 3x + 5 = 20, "3x = 20 + 5 = 25" (the term crossed without its sign changing).
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): substitute the learner's resulting candidate into the original equation; 3(25/3) + 5 = 30 ≠ 20 exposes the error directly.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — reframe explicitly as inverse operations applied to both sides ("to undo +5, subtract 5 — from both sides"), never as movement; a two-column both-sides layout makes the mirrored subtraction visible on every line.
  - **Verification of death**: given 7 − 2x = 1, the learner subtracts 7 from both sides to reach −2x = −6 (not −2x = 6), unprompted.

## Analogies
- **Best — the balance scale.** Two pans holding equal weight; any change applied to only one pan tips the scale, so every change must be mirrored on the other pan to preserve balance. This is the Blueprint's own P03 analogy and it is the direct, load-bearing repair for MC-1 — the physical intuition that removing weight from only one side is obviously wrong transfers cleanly to the algebraic case.
- **Alternative — the taxi fare.** A flat fee plus a per-kilometre charge totals a fixed fare; recovering the distance travelled from the total fare requires undoing the flat fee first, then dividing out the per-kilometre rate — this is the Blueprint's own P76 transfer context and it grounds the isolation sequence in a scenario where "the total is fixed, every rearrangement must preserve it" is self-evident.
- **Story analogy** — a locked box with a known combination built from two operations (add 5, then multiply by 3); to recover what went in, undo the operations in reverse order (divide by 3 first, then subtract 5) — this anticipates the expert-level inverse-function model without naming it.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "move the term and flip the sign."** This is exactly MC-3's parent framing; taught as the primary rule (rather than as an observed consequence of the balance principle), it manufactures the sign-error misconception in learners who would otherwise never make it.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "solve for x means get x alone, however you can."** This licenses any sequence of moves, including invalid one-sided operations, as long as x ends up isolated; the correct frame is "every move preserves balance, and getting x alone is what that sequence of balance-preserving moves happens to achieve."

## Demonstrations
- **The substitution collision for a one-sided operation.** Take the learner's own one-sided step (3x + 5 = 20 → "3x = 20"), substitute their resulting answer into both the original equation and the modified one, and show the modified equation is now false even though it was derived from a true one. *Predict, before substituting, whether the modified equation will check out* — the mismatch is the demonstration.
- **The both-sides two-column trace.** For 7 − 2x = 1, write every step in two explicit columns (left side | right side), performing the identical operation on each line. *Predict the number of lines before starting* — this operationalises Blueprint B01/B02's repair as a standing habit for equations where sign or balance errors are most likely.
- **The fraction-cost race.** Solve 6x + 9 = 27 two ways, side by side: subtract-then-divide versus divide-then-subtract. Both reach x = 3, but one path stays in whole numbers throughout and the other passes through 9/6. *Predict which path will feel faster before starting either* — this is the Blueprint's own TA-A03 contrast pair, restaged as a race rather than a static comparison.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for the balance principle itself — it is a definitional consequence of what "equals" means, not a pattern a learner is likely to re-derive faster than it can simply be stated and then immediately grounded in the balance-scale concrete anchor, and treating it as discoverable risks a learner "discovering" the physically-plausible-but-wrong shortcut (move-and-flip without the balance reasoning underneath). What genuinely earns a discovery framing is the isolation-order strategy question: (1) **Need** — "Solve 6x + 9 = 27 by dividing by 6 first. Now solve it by subtracting 9 first. Which felt easier?" (2) **Playground** — try both orders on several equations of the form ax + b = c, noting which order stays in whole numbers and which introduces fractions. (3) **Invention** — "Can you state a rule for which order to try first?" (4) **Collision** — offer 4(x + 2) = 20, where the "usual" subtract-first order does not apply cleanly (there is no bare constant term to subtract before the bracket is resolved) and dividing by 4 first is strictly better; a rule stated as "always subtract before dividing" fails here. (5) **Formalisation** — eliminate constants before dividing by the coefficient WHEN a bare constant term is present; when the equation opens with a multiplied bracket and nothing else, consider dividing first. (6) **Compression** — "Choose whichever first step keeps the numbers simplest — usually that means constants before coefficients, but check the shape of the equation first."

## Teaching Sequence
The balance principle (TA-A01, via the concrete balance-scale anchor) must be secure, including on one-step equations with no constant-elimination decision to make, before any two-step equation is attempted — this concept's own difficulty tag (developing) and required CPA entry stage (Concrete) reflect that skipping the concrete anchor reliably produces MC-1, per the Blueprint's own Teaching Notes. Sign handling under subtraction (embedded in TA-A02's worked examples) must be practiced explicitly, including the negative-coefficient case, before the isolation-order strategy question (TA-A03) is posed — a learner who is still shaky on MC-3's sign-transposition error cannot meaningfully evaluate which isolation order is "cleaner," since both orders are corrupted equally by an unrelated sign error. Simplification-required equations (brackets, scattered like terms) are deliberately sequenced after single-step and two-step bare-variable equations are fluent, so that a `math.alg.simplification` error is never confused with a balance-principle error during diagnosis. The Blueprint's own Component 4 sequence (TA-A01 balance principle → TA-A02 two-step and simplification-required equations → TA-A03 isolation-order contrast → TA-A04 pattern induction over the full isolation sequence → TA-A05 mastery gate) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Demonstration** — the substitution collision, run on the learner's own one-sided step rather than a hypothetical one, so the "this is now false" result is felt as a consequence of their own move.
- **DO: Worked example, two-column trace** — every early two-step equation solved with both sides written out explicitly on parallel lines, making the balance principle visually unavoidable rather than merely asserted.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "will this path stay in whole numbers, or will it need fractions?" asked before choosing an isolation order, until the strategy becomes automatic.
- **TEST-THINKING: Contrast pair** — the Blueprint's own TA-A03 correct-versus-fraction-heavy comparison, run as a genuine race rather than an inert side-by-side table.
- **Does NOT fit: teaching "move it, flip the sign" as the opening rule.** This is the direct manufacturer of MC-3, per the Blueprint's own explicit Teaching Notes warning; the balance principle must be established first, with the shortcut (if named at all) introduced afterward as an observed consequence, never as the primitive.
- **Does NOT fit: extended one-step-equation drilling before any two-step equation is introduced.** MC-2's isolation-order judgment cannot be tested or taught on equations with only one operation to perform.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "whatever you do, do it to the *whole* equation — both sides." Slow down on "whole" and "both." Listen for a learner narrating a step as "I'll move the 5 over" versus "I'll subtract 5 from both sides" — the first phrasing is the audible signature of the movement metaphor that predicts MC-3, and a tutor hearing it should redirect to the balance framing before the sign error actually occurs, not after. For the isolation-order decision, listen for whether the learner pauses to consider the equation's shape (bracket present? bare constant present?) before committing to a first step, versus reaching reflexively for "always subtract first" — the pause is the strategic-thinking signal this concept is building toward. A confident, unprompted "let me check that" followed by substitution back into the *original* equation is the strongest positive signal this concept produces. Channel reality is owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals
- **A one-sided operation traceable in the work (e.g. 3x + 5 = 20 → 3x = 20)** — MC-1; route to the substitution collision, never to a bare restatement of "do it to both sides."
- **A sign-preserved (not flipped) transposition (3x = 20 + 5)** — MC-3, cleanly diagnostic once MC-1 is ruled out; route to the both-sides two-column trace.
- **A valid but fraction-heavy path chosen where a whole-number path was available** — MC-2; this is a strategy gap, not a correctness gap, and should be routed to the fraction-cost race rather than marked as a wrong answer if the final solution is otherwise correct.
- **Distribution skipped on a bracketed equation before isolation begins** — a `math.alg.simplification` prerequisite gap resurfacing here, not a new misconception of this concept; diagnose and repair via that concept's own machinery, not this one's.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A05 gate, MAMR ⌈0.9×5⌉ = 5/5, including its P76 taxi-fare transfer probe — a gate passed without that word-problem modelling item does not certify the ability to apply the equation-solving procedure to a genuinely novel, unscaffolded context.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance is "I don't know what to do first" facing a two-step equation, or a frozen stare at a negative coefficient. The concept-specific smaller question returns to the one-step case embedded inside the harder one: **"Forget the +5 for a second. If the equation were just 3x = 15, what would you do?"** — isolating the coefficient-division step from the constant-elimination step lets the learner demonstrate a piece of the procedure they already own, then the recovery re-attaches the missing first step. If the freeze is specifically at a negative coefficient, shrink further to a bare numeric fact: **"What is 9 divided by negative 3?"** — confirming the arithmetic fact is secure before re-embedding it in the equation. Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.
