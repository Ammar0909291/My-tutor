## Learning Objective
- The learner can derive $\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$ directly from the Product Rule $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$ by integrating both sides — recognizing it as "the Product Rule run in reverse," exactly parallel to how `math.calc.u-substitution` reverses the Chain Rule.
- The learner can apply the LIATE heuristic (Logarithmic, Inverse trig, Algebraic, Trigonometric, Exponential) to choose $u$ and $dv$ deliberately, and can recognize that a poor choice produces a MORE complicated new integral rather than a simpler one.
- The learner can apply Integration by Parts repeatedly when a single application leaves behind a new integral that itself requires the technique again.

## Core Understanding
Integration by Parts is the Product Rule run backward, in exactly the same structural sense that `math.calc.u-substitution` is the Chain Rule run backward. Starting from $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$ and integrating both sides gives $uv=\int u'v\,dx+\int uv'\,dx$, which rearranges to $\int uv'\,dx=uv-\int u'v\,dx$ — or, in differential notation, $\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du$. The formula does not simplify an integral automatically; it TRADES one integral, $\int u\,dv$, for a different one, $\int v\,du$, and whether that trade is a good one depends entirely on the choice of which factor is $u$ and which is $dv$. The LIATE heuristic orders factor types by how good a candidate they are for $u$: logarithms and inverse trig functions are hard to integrate directly but differentiate into something simpler, making them good choices for $u$ (since $u$ gets differentiated); exponentials are easy to integrate repeatedly without changing form, making them good choices for $dv$ (since $dv$ gets integrated). Choosing the factors in the wrong roles can produce a NEW integral that is more complicated than the original, not less — the technique does not guarantee progress on its own; the choice does. Some integrals require applying the technique more than once, when the new integral $\int v\,du$ still contains a product that itself needs the same treatment — each application typically reduces a polynomial factor's degree by one, so a degree-$n$ polynomial factor requires $n$ applications to fully resolve.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — "integration by parts is a formula: uv minus the integral of v du."** The formula is applied by trying $u$ and $dv$ somewhat arbitrarily, often defaulting to whichever factor is written first, with no sense that the choice matters. *Upgrade trigger*: trying the technique on a product and finding the resulting integral is HARDER than the one started with.
2. **Intermediate — "the choice of u and dv matters — u should be the factor that gets simpler when differentiated (like ln x or a polynomial), and dv should be the factor that's easy to integrate repeatedly (like e^x)."** The LIATE-style reasoning is now present, though perhaps not yet fully systematic. *Upgrade trigger*: encountering an integral (like $x^2e^x$) where one application leaves a new integral that still contains a product.
3. **Advanced — "the formula is the Product Rule integrated and rearranged; a poor choice of u and dv doesn't just fail to help, it actively produces a WORSE integral, because the technique trades one integral for another rather than solving it outright; and some integrals genuinely need the trade made more than once."** The derivation, the choice-matters discipline, and the repeated-application pattern are all now connected as one coherent piece of reasoning rather than three separate rules. *Upgrade trigger*: encountering a case where the resulting integral, after one or more applications, matches the ORIGINAL integral (up to a constant), requiring an algebraic rather than further-integration resolution — a case this concept does not itself cover but the learner's reasoning should not be surprised by.
4. **Expert — Integration by Parts is one member of a family of "reverse a differentiation rule, then manage the resulting trade-off" integration techniques, alongside u-substitution's own reversal of the Chain Rule, generalizing further into systematic reduction formulas for repeated structural patterns.** The learner recognizes the meta-strategy itself as reusable, actively predicting which differentiation rule a new integral technique might be reversing before being told. *Shelf life*: permanent.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant failure treats the choice of $u$ and $dv$ as an arbitrary labeling with no consequence, rather than a deliberate decision with real structural stakes: since the formula "works" in the sense of being algebraically valid regardless of which factor is called $u$ and which $dv$, a learner assumes it does not matter which is chosen — missing that a poor choice produces a genuinely MORE complicated integral, not merely a less efficient path to the same answer (MC-1, U-DV-CHOICE-TREATED-AS-ARBITRARY). This is an instruction-induced gap: without an explicit demonstration that swapping the SAME two factors' roles produces a worse outcome, nothing in the formula's own algebra signals that the choice carries a consequence, since both choices are equally "valid" applications of the rule. A second, distinct failure is overgeneralizing from the typical simple worked example (most first introductions use a single-application integral like $\int xe^x\,dx$) to assume the technique always resolves an integral in one pass: a learner who has only ever seen one-step examples applies the formula once, arrives at a new integral that STILL contains a product, and either stops there (believing the problem is finished) or does not recognize that the SAME technique should be applied again (MC-2, SINGLE-APPLICATION-ASSUMED-ALWAYS-SUFFICIENT) — a Type 1 overgeneralization of "the formula produces the answer" from a narrow set of introductory examples that all happened to resolve in one step. A third failure imports a habit from ordinary indefinite integration: because every OTHER indefinite integral computed so far ends with "+C," a learner adds a constant of integration when computing $v$ from $dv$ midway through the formula — an intermediate step, not the final answer — which is unnecessary (any such constant would cancel out algebraically in the final formula) and a source of confusion and arithmetic error (MC-3, DV-INTEGRATED-WITH-AN-UNNECESSARY-CONSTANT-OF-INTEGRATION) — the identical misapplied-"+C" mechanism already documented for `math.calc.ftc-part2`'s own MC-2 (C-IN-DEFINITE-INTEGRAL), here recurring in the different context of an intermediate step within a larger formula rather than a definite integral's own evaluation.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1, MC-2, MC-3) and its own repair actions B01–B03. **The Blueprint's Misconception Registry carries a Severity column (Foundational, Foundational, Moderate) but no explicit birth-type column**; all three classifications below are independently assigned here.

- **MC-1 — U-DV-CHOICE-TREATED-AS-ARBITRARY** (the Blueprint's own "Foundational" misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 5, instruction-induced. The formula is algebraically valid for either choice of $u$ and $dv$, and nothing in a single, uncontrasted worked example signals that one choice is structurally better than the other — the consequence only becomes visible when the SAME integral is attempted both ways side by side.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: answering "no" or "it doesn't matter" when asked whether swapping which factor is called $u$ and which is $dv$ changes how easy the resulting integral is to solve.
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's A02 hook): "does it matter which factor you call $u$ and which $dv$, as long as the formula is applied correctly?" — an answer of "no, it doesn't matter" reveals MC-1.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — re-walk Example 2's backfire demonstration (the identical integral $\int xe^x\,dx$ attempted with the roles swapped, producing a harder integral), then check: "does your new integral look simpler than the one you started with? If not, swap and try the other way."
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh product integral, the learner states which factor should be $u$ and why (appealing to which factor simplifies under differentiation, or which is easy to integrate repeatedly) BEFORE writing out the formula.

- **MC-2 — SINGLE-APPLICATION-ASSUMED-ALWAYS-SUFFICIENT** (the Blueprint's own "Foundational" misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization. Early worked examples are deliberately simple, single-application cases; a learner who has only encountered these overgeneralizes "the formula, applied once, gives the final answer" to every case, including ones where the resulting integral still contains a product requiring the same treatment again.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: stopping after one application with an unresolved integral still present, or answering "yes" when asked whether Integration by Parts always fully solves an integral after just one application.
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's A03 hook): "does Integration by Parts always fully solve an integral after just one application?" — a "yes" answer reveals MC-2.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — re-walk Example 3's full two-step derivation ($\int x^2e^x\,dx$, where the first application leaves $2\int xe^x\,dx$, itself requiring a second application), checking after each application: "does the leftover integral look like a BASIC form yet, or does it still contain a product needing the same treatment?"
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh multi-application integral (a polynomial factor of degree 2 or higher times an exponential or trig function), the learner applies the technique, checks the resulting integral for a remaining product, and continues applying the technique until a basic form is reached, without prompting.

- **MC-3 — DV-INTEGRATED-WITH-AN-UNNECESSARY-CONSTANT-OF-INTEGRATION** (the Blueprint's own "Moderate" severity misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization — the identical misapplied-"+C" mechanism already documented for `math.calc.ftc-part2`'s own MC-2, here recurring in a different structural location: an intermediate computation of $v$ from $dv$ within the larger Integration by Parts formula, rather than a definite integral's final evaluation.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: writing $v=e^x+C$ (or similar) when computing $v$ from $dv=e^x\,dx$ midway through applying the formula.
  - **Detection probe**: ask the learner to compute $v$ from a given $dv$ as an isolated step, and check whether a "+C" appears.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — explain that only the FINAL integral in the whole computation gets a constant of integration; $v$ itself is just one specific antiderivative, chosen without its own arbitrary constant, since any constant added here would cancel out algebraically in the final formula regardless (a genuinely different role from a stand-alone indefinite integral's own "+C").
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh application, the learner computes $v$ from $dv$ without appending a constant, reserving the single "+C" for the very end of the entire computation.

## Analogies
- **Best — trading one puzzle for a different, hopefully easier, puzzle.** Integration by Parts does not solve the original integral outright; it exchanges it for a new integral, and just like trading one unsolved puzzle for another, the trade is only worth making if the new puzzle is genuinely easier — which depends entirely on which piece you designate as $u$ versus $dv$.
- **Alternative — u-substitution's own sibling: reversing a known rule, deliberately.** Just as u-substitution deliberately reverses the Chain Rule (rather than being a brand-new independent technique), Integration by Parts deliberately reverses the Product Rule — recognizing the SAME meta-strategy ("run a differentiation rule backward") in a new context, rather than treating each as an unrelated formula to memorize separately.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "u and dv are just labels for the two factors, in whichever order they're written."** This phrasing licenses MC-1 directly, implying the assignment is a bookkeeping formality rather than a strategic decision with real consequences for how hard the resulting integral is. Say "$u$ and $dv$ are ROLES you assign deliberately, based on which factor should be differentiated and which should be integrated" instead.

## Demonstrations
- **The backfire comparison.** For $\int xe^x\,dx$, work it with $u=x$, $dv=e^x\,dx$ (giving $xe^x-e^x+C$), then work the SAME integral with the roles swapped, $u=e^x$, $dv=x\,dx$ (giving $\frac{x^2}{2}e^x-\int\frac{x^2}{2}e^x\,dx$ — a harder integral). *Predict whether swapping the roles will make the problem easier, harder, or the same before trying it.* Getting a visibly harder new integral from the swapped choice is the demonstration for MC-1.
- **The repeated-application chain.** For $\int x^2e^x\,dx$, apply the formula once ($u=x^2$, $dv=e^x\,dx$) to get $x^2e^x-\int 2xe^x\,dx$, then recognize the remaining integral $2\int xe^x\,dx$ is exactly the FIRST demonstration's own integral, already solved. *Predict, immediately after the first application, whether the leftover integral is already a basic form or still needs more work.* Recognizing the leftover integral still contains a product is the demonstration for MC-2.
- **The unnecessary-constant check.** Computing $v$ from $dv=e^x\,dx$, ask whether "+C" belongs on $v=e^x$ at this intermediate step. *Predict whether adding a constant here would change the final answer before checking algebraically.* Seeing that any such constant cancels out in the final rearranged formula is the demonstration for MC-3.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction is the argued call for the formula's own derivation from the Product Rule (a specific algebraic rearrangement that benefits from an explicit walkthrough), but the choice-matters comparison (MC-1) and the repeated-application recognition (MC-2) are both genuinely discoverable by direct trial.
1. **Need** — "Compute $\int xe^x\,dx$ with $u=x$, $dv=e^x\,dx$. Now try the SAME integral with $u=e^x$, $dv=x\,dx$. Do you get the same level of difficulty both times?" It does not.
2. **Playground** — try a couple more products (e.g. $\int x\cos x\,dx$) with both role assignments.
3. **Invention** — "Why does one assignment simplify the problem while the other one complicates it?" Let the learner connect it to which factor becomes SIMPLER when differentiated versus which stays manageable when integrated.
4. **Collision** — confront a learner who claims the assignment doesn't matter with the direct side-by-side difficulty gap from the backfire comparison.
5. **Formalisation** — name the LIATE ordering explicitly as a heuristic for which factor tends to make the better $u$.
6. **Compression** — "Choose u to get SIMPLER when differentiated; choose dv to stay EASY when integrated — and check if you need to do it again."

## Teaching Sequence
The Product Rule derivation must be established FIRST, since both the choice-matters discipline (MC-1) and the repeated-application pattern (MC-2) depend on the learner understanding the formula as a genuine algebraic TRADE (one integral for another), not an automatic solver — a learner who sees the formula only as "the answer, computed directly" has no framework for why a choice could make the trade worse. The choice-matters comparison (MC-1) follows directly, per the Blueprint's own A02, using Example 2's identical-integral, swapped-roles demonstration as direct, undeniable evidence. The repeated-application pattern (MC-2) is introduced LAST, per the Blueprint's own A03, once a single correct application is fluent, deliberately reusing Example 1's own already-solved integral as a building block inside Example 3's second step — so the learner experiences firsthand that recognizing an already-solved sub-integral is itself a useful skill, not just repeating the whole procedure blindly. The unnecessary-constant check (MC-3) is folded in as a smaller, procedural correction alongside whichever example is being worked, since it is a computational habit rather than a separate conceptual stage. Turn-level scripts for A01–A04 are owned by the Blueprint's Component 5 and are not restated here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Worked Example** — derive the formula live from $(uv)'=u'v+uv'$, then work Example 1 ($\int xe^x\,dx$) with a well-chosen $u$ and $dv$. First action; anchors the derivation and a correct baseline application.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "Will choosing $u=e^x$, $dv=x\,dx$ for the SAME integral give an equally easy result?" asked BEFORE showing the swapped computation, using the trading-puzzles analogy as the point of contrast. Surfaces MC-1 in one turn.
- **DO: Demonstration** — the repeated-application chain for $\int x^2e^x\,dx$, with the learner checking after the first application whether the leftover integral is already basic.
- **TEST-THINKING: Error Analysis** — "A student computed $\int x^2e^x\,dx$ using Integration by Parts once and reported $x^2e^x-\int 2xe^x\,dx$ as their final answer. What's incomplete?" targets MC-2 directly.
- **Does NOT fit: tabular/DI-method shortcuts for repeated application, cyclic integrals requiring algebraic self-reference (like $\int e^x\sin x\,dx$), or systematic reduction formulas, here.** Those extensions belong to `math.calc.reduction-formulas`, which this concept unlocks.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "the formula trades one integral for another — pick $u$ and $dv$ so the trade actually helps." Say it every time a new product integral is set up, not just the first. Listen for a learner who, after one application, states the answer while an integral sign is still visibly present in their own work — that is the tell for MC-2, and is best caught by asking "is there still an integral sign anywhere in what you just wrote?" rather than checking the final algebra alone. Listen for a learner who writes "+C" while computing $v$ from $dv$, mid-formula — that specific placement is the tell for MC-3. ## Assessment Signals
- **States or implies the choice of $u$ and $dv$ doesn't affect how easy the resulting integral is** — MC-1. Route to the backfire comparison, on the exact integral in question.
- **Stops after one application while a product still remains inside the resulting integral, treating it as finished** — MC-2. Route to the repeated-application chain, on the exact integral in question.
- **Writes a constant of integration when computing $v$ from $dv$ as an intermediate step** — MC-3. Route to the unnecessary-constant check, on the exact computation in question.
- **Correctly chooses $u$ and $dv$ deliberately, applies the formula as many times as needed, and reserves the constant of integration for the final answer only** — the intended target state.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's A04 gate, MAMR 4/5 (⌈0.8×5⌉=4). The 4-item P77 set plus the P76 transfer probe (the $\int x^3e^x\,dx$ signal-processing problem, explicitly reusing Example 3's own already-solved result as a building block) must include at least one item requiring the learner to justify their choice of $u$ and $dv$ before computing, not merely execute a given assignment correctly — a gate made only of correct-execution items risks certifying mechanics without certifying the strategic-choice discrimination against MC-1.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance here is "I don't get why it matters which one is u — the formula works either way, doesn't it?" — a reasonable question given that the formula IS algebraically valid regardless of the assignment. The concept-specific smaller question returns to the SAME integral tried both ways: **"Try $\int xe^x\,dx$ with $u=e^x$ this time, instead of $u=x$. What does the new integral, $\int v\,du$, look like now?"** The learner computes it themselves and sees a $\frac{x^2}{2}e^x$ term appear — visibly messier than the original. Then return: "the formula didn't break — it's still algebraically correct either way — but this choice traded your original integral for a WORSE one. That's the part that matters: not whether the formula applies, but whether the trade helps." If the frustration is instead about not recognizing a leftover integral needs a second application, shrink to the bare check: **"Look at what you just wrote down. Is there still an integral sign anywhere in it?"** Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Memory Hooks
- Concept type: **procedure with an embedded strategic-choice requirement** (which factor is $u$ and which is $dv$ is a deliberate decision with real consequences, not a fixed labeling step, and must be justified, not just executed). Review by *requiring the learner to state their reasoning for the $u$/$dv$ assignment BEFORE writing the formula*, never accepting a correct final answer alone as evidence of understanding, since a learner can occasionally guess the better assignment without understanding why it was better, letting MC-1 pass undetected.

## Cross-Subject Connections
