## Learning Objective
- The learner can simplify ⁿ√a by extracting the *largest* perfect n-th-power factor from the radicand, using the product rule ⁿ√(xy) = ⁿ√x·ⁿ√y.
- The learner can rationalize a denominator containing a single radical term (multiply by that same radical over itself) and a binomial denominator containing a radical (multiply by the conjugate, using the difference-of-squares identity).
- The learner can combine like radicals — same index AND same radicand — and can correctly refuse to combine radicals that share only one of those two properties.

## Core Understanding
A radical expression ⁿ√a is, underneath its own notation, the fractional exponent a^(1/n) — everything this concept teaches is a direct consequence of that identity plus the exponent rules the learner already owns. Simplifying by extracting perfect powers is the product rule for exponents read through radical notation: ⁿ√(xy) = ⁿ√x · ⁿ√y, so factoring the radicand to isolate the largest perfect n-th-power piece and pulling its root out front is not a new trick, it is exponent-splitting wearing a radical sign. Rationalizing a denominator exists because a radical in the denominator is considered an unfinished form, not because radicals are somehow forbidden there — for a single radical term, multiplying by "itself over itself" uses ⁿ√a · ⁿ√a = a to erase the radical; for a binomial denominator, the same goal is reached by multiplying by the *conjugate*, which triggers the difference-of-squares identity (a+b)(a−b) = a² − b² specifically because that identity is the one algebraic tool that turns a sum containing a square root into a radical-free expression. Combining like radicals is, once again, exactly `math.alg.like-terms`'s rule, transplanted: a√r + b√r = (a+b)√r requires both the index and the radicand to match, in precisely the same way combining ax + bx required the variable part to match — √2 and √3 are as unlike as x and y, and √2 and ³√2 are unlike for the same structural reason x and x² are unlike (different "exponent," here expressed as a different root index, on the same base).

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — pull the perfect-square piece out.** For √72, spot that 72 = 36 × 2 and that 36 is a perfect square, so √72 = √36 · √2 = 6√2. *Upgrade trigger*: a denominator containing a radical, where "pull something out" gives no instruction for what to do with a radical that resists extraction entirely. *Shelf life*: one to two sessions.
2. **Intermediate — clear the denominator by multiplying top and bottom by whatever erases the radical below.** For a single radical, that's the radical itself; for a sum or difference containing one, it's the conjugate, because (a+b)(a−b) = a²−b² has no cross term left to carry a radical. *Upgrade trigger*: deciding whether two radical terms can be added — multiplying-to-clear gives no guidance about when *addition* of radicals is legal. *Shelf life*: durable through the whole of this concept and its immediate unlocks.
3. **Advanced — a radical is a fractional exponent, and every rule here is an exponent rule in disguise.** ⁿ√(xy) = ⁿ√x·ⁿ√y is (xy)^(1/n) = x^(1/n)y^(1/n); combining like radicals is combining like terms where the "variable part" is the pair (index, radicand). *Upgrade trigger*: `math.alg.fractional-exponent`, where the notation itself switches from radical signs to explicit rational exponents and every rule must survive the switch unchanged.
4. **Expert — radicals are one notational convention among several for the same underlying operation, and fluency means moving between conventions without re-deriving anything.** ⁿ√a, a^(1/n), and (in later mathematics) the principal n-th root as a specific complex number are three views of one object; the "rules" of this concept are properties of exponentiation, not properties invented specifically for radical notation. *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the model that prevents radicals from ever again feeling like a separate topic from exponents.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant and most damaging failure is treating any two radical expressions as automatically combinable the way like algebraic terms are — 3√2 + 5√3 gets "simplified" into a single merged term, or the coefficients and radicands get cross-combined in some ad hoc way, because addition-of-radicals notation superficially resembles addition-of-algebraic-like-terms notation (a√r + b√r visually echoes ax + bx) and the learner has not yet installed the stricter matching condition (both index and radicand, not just "there's a radical present") that licenses combination. This is compounded by a related but separate error at the "matching" boundary itself: √2 and ³√2 share a radicand but not an index, and a learner checking only the radicand will wrongly judge them combinable, missing that they represent genuinely different numerical values. The second major failure is specific to rationalization: faced with a binomial denominator like 3 + √2, the learner applies the single-radical-term technique (multiply by the same expression) rather than recognising the case has changed and a conjugate is required — multiplying (3+√2) by itself does not eliminate the radical (it produces 9 + 6√2 + 2, still carrying √2), so the method silently fails to achieve its own stated goal, and a learner who does not check "is the radical actually gone?" can complete the algebra without noticing the rationalization never happened. A third, quieter failure is stopping the extraction step early — simplifying √72 to 2√18 (18 = 2×9, so a further perfect square remains) instead of continuing to 6√2 — which produces a technically-equivalent but incompletely reduced answer that most mastery checks will mark wrong.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1..MC-3) and its repair actions B01–B03, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — UNLIKE-RADICALS-COMBINED-INCORRECTLY** (Foundational)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of `math.alg.like-terms`'s own combination notation — a√r + b√r visually echoes ax + bx closely enough that the stricter "both index and radicand must match" condition is skipped in favour of "there's a radical, so combine it."
  - **Characteristic phrase**: shown 3√2 + 5√3, an attempt to merge into a single radical term (e.g. adding radicands, or otherwise producing one combined term where none is possible).
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): present 3√2 + 5√3 and ask the learner to "simplify further" — an attempted merge confirms MC-1.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — re-anchor explicitly on the algebraic-like-terms analogy: √2 and √3 are as different from each other as two different variables x and y, and are never combinable by addition for exactly the same structural reason.
  - **Verification of death**: given 2√5 + 3√20 − √5 (which requires simplifying 3√20 to 6√5 *before* the combination decision can even be made), the learner correctly simplifies first, then combines only the genuinely like terms.

- **MC-2 — RATIONALIZATION-SKIPPED-FOR-BINOMIAL-DENOMINATOR** (Foundational)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of the single-radical-term rationalization method to the binomial case, where it does not achieve its own goal.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: given a denominator like 4 + √2, multiplying top and bottom by 4 + √2 itself (the single-radical method) rather than by its conjugate 4 − √2, leaving a radical in the result.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): give a binomial-radical-denominator problem and check whether the multiplier chosen is the same expression or its conjugate — choosing the same expression confirms MC-2.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — let the learner attempt the "same expression" multiplication, show explicitly that the radical survives (it does not cancel), then apply the conjugate and connect the success directly to the difference-of-squares identity already known from `math.alg.polynomial-operations`-adjacent factoring work.
  - **Verification of death**: given 3/(4+√2), the learner selects the conjugate 4−√2 unprompted and correctly produces a radical-free denominator.

- **MC-3 — PERFECT-POWER-FACTOR-NOT-FULLY-EXTRACTED** (Moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of "found *a* perfect-power factor, stop" — the procedure is treated as complete after one successful extraction rather than as a search for the *largest* available factor.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: simplifying √72 to 2√18, rather than continuing to the fully reduced 6√2.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): check whether a learner's simplified radical still contains an extractable perfect-power factor — 2√18 (18 = 9×2, and 9 is a perfect square) confirms MC-3.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — continue the extraction process explicitly on the remaining radicand, checking after every extraction whether a further perfect-power factor remains, until none does.
  - **Verification of death**: given √200, the learner produces 10√2 directly (not 2√50 or 5√8), and can state, unprompted, the check ("does 2 have any remaining perfect-square factor? no — done").

## Analogies
- **Best — the variable-parts-must-match rule from like terms, restated.** √2 and √3 are as different as two different algebraic variables; a√r + b√r combines only when r and the index match exactly, exactly as ax + bx combines only when the variable part matches exactly. This is the Blueprint's own primary framing and it is the direct repair for MC-1 because the learner already trusts the source rule.
- **Alternative — clearing a fraction's denominator of an unwanted factor.** Multiplying top and bottom of a fraction by the same nonzero quantity never changes its value, only its appearance; rationalization is this familiar fraction-equivalence move, aimed specifically at a radical rather than at reducing to lowest terms.
- **Story analogy** — two rulers, one marked in the "√2 unit" and one in the "√3 unit": a length of 3 √2-units plus 5 √3-units cannot be reported as a single measurement in either unit alone, because the units themselves are incommensurable — this targets MC-1 from a physical-measurement angle rather than a purely algebraic one.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "just multiply by the denominator to clear it."** This is exactly MC-2 stated as an instruction; the correct frame is "multiply by whatever makes the radical vanish," which is the same expression only in the single-radical-term case and the conjugate in the binomial case — the goal (radical gone), not the mechanical habit (multiply by the denominator), must drive the choice.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "simplifying a radical means pulling *a* number out front."** This licenses MC-3 directly; the correct frame is "pulling the *largest possible* perfect-power factor out front, and checking that none remains."

## Demonstrations
- **The failed-rationalization collision, run live.** Attempt 4/(2−√5) by multiplying top and bottom by (2−√5) itself, show the radical survives in the expanded denominator, then repeat correctly with the conjugate (2+√5) and show the radical vanishes via difference-of-squares. *Predict, before multiplying, whether the radical will disappear* — the mismatch between the prediction and the failed attempt's actual result is the demonstration; the conjugate rule is only the label applied afterward.
- **The extraction-continuation check.** Simplify √72 by stopping deliberately at 2√18, then ask "is this fully simplified?" and let the learner discover 18 still hides a perfect-square factor, continuing to 6√2. *Predict the final coefficient before continuing* — this directly operationalises Blueprint B03's repair as a standing habit.
- **The index-versus-radicand sort.** Lay out √2, √3, ³√2, 2√2, 5√3, −√2 as cards and sort into "combinable groups" before performing any arithmetic. This isolates the matching judgment (index AND radicand) from the addition itself, the same separation-of-judgment-from-arithmetic principle `math.alg.like-terms`'s own sorting-tray demonstration uses.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for the extraction procedure and the rationalization techniques themselves — both are applications of already-secured rules (the product rule for exponents, the difference-of-squares identity), not principles a learner could plausibly re-derive unaided within this lesson, and framing them as discoverable risks manufacturing an incomplete or incorrect "discovered" version (most obviously, discovering *a* factor rather than the *largest* one, directly seeding MC-3). What is genuinely discoverable, and should be surfaced as a question rather than told, is the matching condition for combining radicals: (1) **Need** — "Can you write 3√2 + 5√3 as a single term? Try a few ways." (2) **Playground** — numerically estimate √2 ≈ 1.41 and √3 ≈ 1.73, computing 3(1.41)+5(1.73) versus various candidate single-radical answers, and observe none match. (3) **Invention** — "What has to be true about two radical terms before they can be added into one?" (4) **Collision** — offer √2 and ³√2 (same radicand, different index): a rule based on "same number under the root" alone fails, since √2 ≈ 1.41 and ³√2 ≈ 1.26 are genuinely different values. (5) **Formalisation** — both the index and the radicand must match exactly. (6) **Compression** — "Same root, same number underneath — only then do the coefficients add."

## Teaching Sequence
Extraction of perfect-power factors (TA-A01) must be secure, including the "keep going until none remain" discipline that prevents MC-3, before the combining-like-radicals judgment (TA-A02) is introduced — the Blueprint's own P77 mastery item (2√5 + 3√20 − √5) is deliberately constructed so that an unresolved MC-3 (incomplete extraction on the 3√20 term) makes the combining decision unanswerable regardless of whether MC-1 is otherwise cleared. Rationalization (also within TA-A01) is sequenced with the single-radical case taught and secured before the binomial/conjugate case, because MC-2 is specifically the failure of applying the single-radical method unmodified to the binomial case — introducing the binomial case first would remove the very contrast (same goal, different required method) the repair depends on. The like-radicals contrast (TA-A02, targeting MC-1) is placed last among the three, deliberately after both extraction and rationalization are fluent, so that the P77/P76 mastery items can require genuine multi-step application (simplify, *then* decide combinability) rather than testing the matching rule in isolation on already-simplified radicands. The Blueprint's own Component 4/5 sequence (TA-A01 → TA-A02 → TA-A03 mastery gate, with B01–B03 repairs threaded through) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Demonstration** — the failed-rationalization collision, run on the learner's own attempted same-expression multiplication rather than a hypothetical one, so the "it didn't work" result is felt as a consequence of their own choice.
- **ORGANIZE: Sorting / Categorisation** — the index-versus-radicand card sort, this concept's highest-value action for MC-1 because it separates the matching judgment from any arithmetic, exactly as `math.alg.like-terms`'s sorting tray does for algebraic terms.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "will this fully simplify in one extraction, or is there more hiding?" asked before every simplification, until the largest-factor habit is automatic.
- **TEST-THINKING: Error Analysis** — "a student simplified √72 to 2√18 and stopped. What's still wrong?" — stronger than direct correction because it requires the learner to re-apply the extraction check to someone else's (plausible-looking) stopping point.
- **Does NOT fit: drilling extraction on radicands that are already-fully-reduced primes (e.g. √7, √11) before the incomplete-extraction case is covered.** It never gives MC-3 a chance to surface, producing false confidence.
- **Does NOT fit: teaching rationalization as "always multiply by the denominator."** This directly seeds MC-2; the method must be taught as "multiply by whatever clears the radical," verified case by case.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence for combining radicals is "same index, same radicand — both, exactly, or they don't combine." Slow down on "both" and "exactly." Listen for a learner reading 3√2 + 5√3 aloud and immediately reaching for a merged answer without pausing on "these are different numbers under the root" — the absence of that pause is the audible signature of MC-1 about to fire. For rationalization, listen for whether the learner names "conjugate" only after checking that the same-expression attempt failed, versus reaching for "conjugate" reflexively on every problem including single-radical-term denominators (where it is unnecessary, though not wrong, and suggests the *why* of the choice has not landed). A learner who states, unprompted, "is the radical actually gone?" after a rationalization step has crossed the real threshold of this concept — that self-check is the goal, not merely the correct final answer. Channel reality is owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals
- **Merged single-term answer on 3√2 + 5√3** — MC-1; route to the index-versus-radicand sort, never to a bare restatement of the matching rule.
- **Same-expression (not conjugate) multiplier chosen on a binomial radical denominator** — MC-2, cleanly diagnostic; route to the failed-rationalization collision so the learner sees the method's own failure before the correction.
- **A simplified radical that still contains an extractable perfect-power factor (e.g. 2√18)** — MC-3; route to the extraction-continuation check, asking explicitly whether a further factor remains.
- **Correct simplification of both terms before attempting to combine, even when the combination is ultimately impossible** — the intended, well-formed response pattern; do not rush past it toward a "there must be a combinable answer" expectation.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A03 gate, MAMR ⌈0.8×5⌉ = 4/5, including its P76 transfer probe (an architect's rationalization problem paired with a material-length combination requiring extraction-before-combining) — a gate passed without that combined-skill transfer item does not certify the ability to sequence extraction and combination correctly under an unscaffolded, multi-step problem.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance is "these both have radicals, why can't I just add them?" — a reasonable-sounding question given the surface resemblance to like-term addition, not confusion about the material's difficulty. The concept-specific smaller question returns to already-secured ground: **"What is 3x + 5y? Can you write that as one term?"** — the learner already knows the answer is no, and the return move is simply: "√2 is like x here, and √3 is like y — different, unrelated quantities." If the freeze is specifically at rationalization, shrink to the goal rather than the method: **"What are we actually trying to get rid of? Does multiplying by this expression get rid of it, or not?"** — checking the goal directly, rather than reciting "multiply by the conjugate," is what prevents MC-2 from recurring under pressure. Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Memory Hooks
- Concept type: **procedure**, with an embedded judgment (is this fully extracted? are these combinable?) at its head — closer to `math.alg.like-terms`'s procedure-plus-judgment shape than to a pure mechanical skill. Review by *application on mixed input*, including at least one item requiring extraction before a combination decision and one binomial-denominator rationalization item every cycle.
- Concept-specific deviation: keep at least one already-fully-simplified radical (nothing left to extract) and one genuinely non-combinable pair (matching neither index nor radicand fully) permanently in the review rotation — both "already done" and "cannot combine" responses decay faster than the active procedures, because ordinary practice sets skew toward items requiring visible work.

## Transfer Connections
- **Near**: `math.alg.fractional-exponent` (the direct notational switch to a^(1/n), where every rule taught here must survive re-expression), `math.alg.radical-equations` (solving equations containing radicals depends on simplification fluency established here), `math.alg.simplifying-radicals` and `math.alg.rationalizing-denominators` as this concept's own KG children, isolating each half of this concept's scope for deeper practice.
- **Far**: complex number arithmetic, where rationalizing a complex denominator by multiplying by the complex conjugate is the identical difference-of-squares-style move (a+bi)(a−bi) = a²+b², performed for structurally the same reason.
- **Real-world**: any physical-measurement calculation that produces an irrational length or magnitude requiring a "cleaned up" final form — the Blueprint's own architect's-brace-length transfer probe is not a contrived setting; engineering and construction calculations routinely arrive at radical expressions needing simplification before a usable numeric answer is read off.
- **Expert transfer**: the general habit of recognising when an expression is in a "standard," communicable final form versus merely an equivalent but unreduced one — the same discipline that governs reducing fractions, simplifying rational expressions, and normalising results in any symbolic system.

## Cross-Subject Connections
- **Physics**, genuine: quantities computed via the Pythagorean theorem or similar (resultant vector magnitudes, diagonal distances) routinely produce radicals that must be simplified and, when they appear in a denominator (e.g. in certain formula rearrangements), rationalized before further use.
- **Computer science**, weak but real: numerical libraries and symbolic computation systems perform radical simplification and rationalization as an explicit normalisation step so that equivalent expressions compare as syntactically identical.
