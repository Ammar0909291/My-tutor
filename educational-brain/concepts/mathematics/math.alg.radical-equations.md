## Learning Objective
- The learner can isolate a radical — get it alone on one side of the equation — before squaring, every time, never squaring a sum or difference that still contains an un-isolated radical.
- The learner can square both sides of an equation with an isolated radical, and correctly expand any resulting binomial square rather than mis-simplifying it.
- The learner can check every candidate solution against the ORIGINAL (unsquared) equation, correctly identifying and discarding any extraneous solution the squaring step introduced.

## Core Understanding
A radical equation has the variable trapped under a radical sign, and the standard solving method — isolate the radical, then square both sides — works because squaring a variable's radical of matching index cancels it, converting the equation into an ordinary polynomial equation that already-known techniques can solve. The isolation step must happen *before* squaring, not as an optional cleanup afterward, because squaring an expression that still contains an un-isolated radical (like √(2x−1) + 3) does not eliminate the radical at all — it produces (√(2x−1))² + 2·3·√(2x−1) + 3², a messier expression that still carries the radical inside its middle term, entirely defeating the purpose of squaring. The one genuinely new and structurally important fact this concept introduces is that squaring is not a reversible operation: it discards sign information, because both a = b and a = −b square to the identical a² = b², so squaring both sides of an equation can manufacture a solution that satisfies the *squared* equation without satisfying the *original* one — an extraneous solution. This is not a rare edge case to watch for occasionally; it is a structural consequence of the method itself, which is why the check against the original equation is not optional verification but a required, final stage of the solving procedure — a radical equation is not considered solved until every candidate has been individually confirmed against the equation as it was originally written, before any squaring occurred.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — get the radical alone, then square it away.** √(x+3) = 4: already isolated, so square both sides directly to reach x + 3 = 16, then solve normally. *Upgrade trigger*: an equation where the radical is not already alone (something is added or subtracted alongside it), where "just square it" applied directly produces a mess rather than a clean elimination. *Shelf life*: one session.
2. **Intermediate — isolate first, always, even when it takes an extra step.** √(2x−1) + 3 = x becomes √(2x−1) = x − 3 (subtracting 3 from both sides) *before* any squaring happens. *Upgrade trigger*: a solved-and-checked equation that produces two candidate answers, only one of which actually satisfies the original equation — "isolate first" alone gives no account of why a seemingly correctly-solved equation could still yield a wrong answer. *Shelf life*: durable and remains correct forever, but insufficient alone once extraneous solutions appear.
3. **Advanced — squaring loses sign information, so every candidate must be individually re-verified against the original equation.** a = b and a = −b both square to a² = b²; solving the squared equation necessarily finds solutions to *both* of these, and only re-checking against the true original equation (not the squared one) can tell which candidates are genuine. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to explain *why* extraneous solutions are less likely to arise in certain applied contexts (e.g. physical quantities constrained to be positive), where the abstract sign-loss explanation must be connected to a concrete domain restriction.
4. **Expert — solving a radical equation is finding the intersection of the original equation's solution set with the (larger) solution set of its squared version.** The squaring step is a *necessary but not sufficient* transformation: every true solution of the original survives squaring, but the squared equation may also admit extra solutions the original never had; the check is not a formality, it is the step that recovers the correct, smaller solution set. *Shelf life*: permanent, and it directly generalises to any equation-transforming step (raising to any even power, taking an absolute value) that is similarly non-injective.

## Why Students Fail
The most structurally important failure is skipping or only partially performing the final verification step: having correctly isolated the radical, squared, and solved the resulting polynomial equation to reach two or more candidate values, the learner accepts all of them as solutions without checking each one individually against the *original*, unsquared equation — missing that the squaring step can silently pull in solutions belonging to a different, sign-flipped equation that was never actually posed. This failure is dangerous precisely because every visible step up to that point can be executed flawlessly; the error is one of omission at the very end, not a computational mistake anywhere along the way, so a learner who "does everything right" mechanically can still report a wrong final answer set. The second major failure occurs earlier and is a sequencing error: the learner squares both sides before isolating the radical, attempting to square a sum like √(2x−1) + 3 directly as if it were (√(2x−1))² + 3² — this both fails to eliminate the radical (a genuine binomial square still carries a cross term containing the radical) and reflects a deeper gap in recognising that (a+b)² ≠ a² + b² in general, a misapplication that this concept exposes with unusual clarity because the un-eliminated radical is an immediate, visible signal that something went wrong. A third, narrower failure appears specifically once isolation is correctly performed and a binomial square must be expanded: (x−3)² is mis-expanded as x² − 9 (dropping the middle term) rather than the correct x² − 6x + 9, an error imported directly from incomplete mastery of `math.alg.factoring-special`'s own perfect-square-trinomial pattern rather than being specific to radical equations.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1..MC-3) and its repair actions B01–B03, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — RADICAL-EQUATION-CANDIDATES-NOT-CHECKED-AGAINST-ORIGINAL** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation — in every other equation-solving concept this learner has met, a correctly-executed sequence of valid steps guarantees a correct solution set, and there has been no prior structural reason to distrust that guarantee; squaring is the first genuinely non-reversible step this learner's equation-solving experience has encountered.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: given √(x+2) = x, solving the squared equation to reach x = 2 or x = −1, and reporting both without individually re-checking either against the original.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): present Example 2 and check whether both x = 2 and x = −1 are accepted without individual verification.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — substitute each candidate separately into the *original*, unsquared equation, showing one genuinely satisfies it (√4 = 2 ✓) while the other does not (√1 = 1 ≠ −1), making the discarding of the extraneous candidate a directly observed consequence rather than an asserted rule.
  - **Verification of death**: given a new radical equation yielding two candidates, the learner checks both individually against the original equation, unprompted, before reporting a final answer.

- **MC-2 — RADICAL-SQUARED-BEFORE-BEING-ISOLATED** (foundational)
  - **Birth type**: Type 4, notation-induced — squaring "the whole equation" is read as squaring whatever expression currently sits on each side, without recognising that a sum containing a radical must first be reduced to the radical *alone* before squaring can eliminate anything.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: given √(2x−1) + 3 = x, squaring the left side directly as (√(2x−1))² + 3² = 2x − 1 + 9, dropping the required cross term.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): present Example 3 and check whether the radical is isolated before squaring.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — attempt the un-isolated squaring live, showing the radical does *not* disappear (the correct expansion (√(2x−1)+3)² = (2x−1) + 6√(2x−1) + 9 still contains a radical), then re-derive correctly after isolating first.
  - **Verification of death**: given √(3x+4) − 2 = x, the learner isolates (adds 2 to both sides) before squaring, unprompted.

- **MC-3 — SQUARED-SUM-EXPANDED-INCORRECTLY** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of the "square each term separately" pattern that is valid for a *product* of like radicals but not for a squared *binomial* — imported from `math.alg.factoring-special`'s own perfect-square-trinomial gap rather than originating in this concept.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: expanding (x−3)² as x² − 9, dropping the middle term −6x.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): review a submitted binomial-squaring step for a missing middle term.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — re-expand explicitly using the full (a−b)² = a² − 2ab + b² pattern, including the middle term every time, cross-referencing `math.alg.factoring-special`'s own treatment of this identity.
  - **Verification of death**: given (x+5)², the learner produces x² + 10x + 25, unprompted, with the middle term included.

## Analogies
- **Best — a squared photograph loses information a plain photograph kept.** Once a=b and a=−b are both squared, the result (a²=b²) can no longer distinguish which original statement was true — squaring is a lossy transformation, and recovering the truth requires going back to check against the original. This directly targets MC-1 by giving the sign-loss idea a concrete, checkable image.
- **Alternative — clearing a stubborn wrapper before applying the tool that removes it.** Squaring "removes" a radical the way a specific tool removes a specific wrapper, but the tool only works on the wrapper itself, alone — applying it to the wrapper-plus-something-else does not work as intended. This targets MC-2's premature-squaring error directly.
- **Story analogy** — two suspects, one guilty and one innocent, who both happen to match a coarse initial description (the squared equation); only individually re-interviewing each against the full original evidence (the unsquared equation) reveals which is actually guilty (a genuine solution).
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "squaring both sides always keeps the equation equivalent."** This is exactly MC-1's false premise; the correct frame is "squaring preserves every true solution, but may also introduce false ones — the original equation, not the squared one, is the final authority."
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "square each piece separately, like distributing a multiplication."** This licenses MC-2 and MC-3 simultaneously; squaring a sum is never "square each term," it is the full (a+b)² = a² + 2ab + b² pattern, with a middle term that a term-by-term approach always drops.

## Demonstrations
- **The two-candidate individual-check trace.** Solve √(x+2) = x live, reaching x = 2 and x = −1, then substitute *each one separately* into the original equation in full view, showing 2 checks out and −1 does not. *Predict, before checking, whether both candidates will survive* — most learners will predict yes, and the mismatch for x = −1 is the demonstration.
- **The premature-squaring failure, attempted live.** Take √(2x−1) + 3 = x and square the left side directly as written, showing the radical genuinely survives inside the result — then isolate correctly and re-square, showing the radical vanishes this time. *Predict whether the radical will disappear before attempting the premature version* — the failed prediction is the demonstration, operationalising Blueprint B02's repair.
- **The binomial-square side-by-side.** Expand (x−3)² both the (incorrect) term-by-term way and the correct full-pattern way, side by side, then verify by substituting a specific value of x into both expansions to show only the full-pattern version agrees with direct computation.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for the core method (isolate, then square) — it is a direct, necessary consequence of what squaring can and cannot do to a radical, not a principle to be discovered from scratch. What is genuinely discoverable, and central to this concept's own distinguishing content, is the extraneous-solution phenomenon itself: (1) **Need** — "Solve √(x+2) = x. You'll get two candidate values. Check both in the original equation." (2) **Playground** — solve several radical equations that each produce two candidates, checking each candidate against its own original equation, recording which candidates survive and which don't. (3) **Invention** — "Why would a value satisfy the squared equation but not the original one?" (4) **Collision** — offer √(x+2) = x alongside a nearly identical equation, √(x+2) = −x, and show both square to the identical x + 2 = x², so solving the squared equation alone cannot distinguish which original equation was actually being solved. (5) **Formalisation** — squaring loses the sign, so a = b and a = −b become indistinguishable after squaring; only re-checking against the true original recovers which is which. (6) **Compression** — "Squaring can manufacture false solutions. The original equation, not the squared one, always gets the final word."

## Teaching Sequence
Isolation-before-squaring (TA-A01, targeting MC-2) is sequenced first, ahead of the extraneous-solution lesson (TA-A02, targeting MC-1), because a learner who squares prematurely produces an equation that is not merely differently-solved but *wrong* from the start — teaching the extraneous-solution check on top of a premature-squaring error would ask the learner to verify candidates derived from an invalid transformation, confusing two genuinely separate failure modes. Within the extraneous-solution lesson itself, both candidates from the squared equation must be derived correctly (i.e. after MC-2 is already cleared) before the individual-checking habit can be meaningfully taught, since a learner cannot practise "which of these genuine candidates is extraneous" on candidates that were never validly derived in the first place. MC-3 (binomial-square expansion errors) is addressed as it arises within either TA-A01 or TA-A02's worked examples rather than as a separate staged lesson, because it is fundamentally an imported `math.alg.factoring-special` gap rather than a radical-equation-specific misconception, and isolating it as a standalone stage here would misrepresent its true origin. The Blueprint's own Component 5 sequence (TA-A01 isolation-before-squaring via conceptual shift, TA-A02 extraneous-solution contrast pair, TA-A03 mastery gate) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Demonstration** — the two-candidate individual-check trace, run on the learner's own solved equation rather than a hypothetical one, so the discarding of the extraneous candidate is felt as a direct consequence of their own work.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "will squaring this eliminate the radical, or not?" asked before every squaring step, specifically to catch un-isolated attempts before they are executed.
- **TEST-THINKING: Error Analysis** — the premature-squaring failure demonstration, framed as "here's what happens if you square too early" rather than as an abstract warning, making MC-2's consequence directly visible.
- **Does NOT fit: teaching this concept before `math.alg.factoring-special`'s binomial-square pattern is independently secure.** MC-3's error is imported wholesale from that prerequisite gap; attempting to repair it here as if it were native to radical equations misdiagnoses its actual source.
- **Does NOT fit: presenting only equations with a single, non-extraneous candidate solution.** This never gives MC-1 a chance to surface, producing false confidence that "solve and report" is a sufficient procedure.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "check it in the *original* equation — not the squared one." Slow down on "original," every time a candidate is reported. Listen for a learner who states an answer confidently immediately after solving the squared equation, with no mention of checking, versus one who pauses and says "let me check this in the real equation" — the unprompted checking pause is the strongest positive signal this concept produces. For MC-2, listen for whether the learner narrates an isolation step explicitly ("first I'll get the root by itself") before reaching for squaring, or reaches directly for squaring on an equation that still has other terms alongside the radical — the absent isolation narration predicts a premature-squaring attempt. Channel reality is owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals
- **Both candidates from a squared equation reported without individual verification** — MC-1; route to the two-candidate individual-check trace, never to a bare restatement of "always check your answers."
- **A radical squared while still accompanied by another term on the same side (an un-eliminated radical surviving the "squaring" step)** — MC-2, and the surviving radical itself is the clearest possible diagnostic signal; route to the premature-squaring failure demonstration.
- **A missing middle term in a squared-binomial expansion, isolated correctly beforehand** — MC-3, and should be diagnosed and repaired as a `math.alg.factoring-special` gap resurfacing here, not treated as a radical-equation-specific error.
- **A candidate correctly discarded as extraneous, with the learner able to state why (squaring lost the sign)** — the intended, fully-formed response pattern; this is the concept's actual mastery signal, not merely reaching a numerically correct final answer.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A03 gate, MAMR ⌈0.75×5⌉ = 4/5, including its P76 pendulum-period transfer probe, which specifically asks the learner to reason about *why* extraneous solutions are less practically likely in a physically-constrained context while still upholding the general algebraic check — a gate passed without that applied-reasoning component does not certify the deeper understanding of *why* the check matters, only the mechanical habit of performing it.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance is "I got two answers, which one do I pick?" — a genuinely well-posed question at exactly the right moment, not confusion about the material's difficulty. The concept-specific smaller question routes directly to the check itself: **"Plug the first answer back into the very first equation you were given — before you squared anything. Does it work?"** — isolating the verification action from any further algebra. If the freeze is specifically at the isolation-before-squaring step, shrink to the goal: **"What are we trying to get rid of? Is it alone on one side yet, or is something else still next to it?"** — redirecting attention to the isolation check rather than reaching for squaring reflexively. Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Transfer Connections
- **Near**: no KG unlocks are recorded for this concept — it currently sits as a terminal leaf, but its extraneous-solution-checking discipline is the direct conceptual ancestor of similar checks required when solving rational equations (which can introduce solutions that make a denominator zero) or absolute-value equations (which share the identical sign-loss structure).
- **Far**: any equation-solving method involving a non-injective (many-to-one) transformation — squaring, taking an absolute value, applying a periodic function — shares this concept's exact structural lesson: the transformed equation's solution set can be a strict superset of the original's, and checking against the original is not optional.
- **Real-world**: the Blueprint's own pendulum-period transfer probe reflects a genuine physical-formula-rearrangement scenario, where a physical constraint (length and period must be positive) narrows which algebraic candidates are physically meaningful even before an algebraic check is performed.
- **Expert transfer**: the general principle, foundational to later mathematics, that not every algebraically valid transformation of an equation preserves its exact solution set — squaring here is the learner's first encounter with this idea, and it recurs throughout calculus (e.g. squaring in optimization or related-rates problems) and beyond.
