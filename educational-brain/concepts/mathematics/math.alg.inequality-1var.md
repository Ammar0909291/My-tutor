## Learning Objective
- The learner can solve a variable-on-both-sides inequality by first collecting variable terms onto one side, and only then checking whether the resulting isolating coefficient is negative and a sign flip is required.
- The learner can solve a compound (two-sided) inequality a < mx + b < c by applying the identical operation to all three parts simultaneously, including flipping both inequality directions and reversing the part order when multiplying or dividing by a negative.
- The learner can recognise and correctly interpret degenerate cases — a linear inequality whose variable terms cancel entirely reduces to an always-true statement (solution: all reals) or an always-false statement (solution: the empty set), genuinely different outcomes from the usual interval solution.

## Core Understanding
This concept extends the single-operation sign-flip rule `math.alg.inequality` already established (flip the direction when multiplying or dividing by a negative) into three genuinely new situations that a single isolating step does not cover. When the variable appears on both sides, the sign-flip decision cannot be evaluated against the *original* inequality at all — there is no single isolating coefficient yet, because the variable terms have not been consolidated — so collecting them onto one side is a mandatory first stage, not an optional simplification, and the flip decision only becomes answerable once that collection is complete. A compound inequality a < mx + b < c is not one inequality but two, glued together (a < mx+b AND mx+b < c), and because both halves must remain true simultaneously, any operation applied to solve it must be applied to *all three* parts — the left bound, the middle expression, and the right bound — at every single step, with a negative multiplication or division flipping both directions and, correspondingly, reversing which bound is written on which side. The degenerate cases arise from a structural fact about the collecting step itself: if the variable terms on both sides are literally identical, collecting them cancels the variable entirely, leaving a bare constant-versus-constant comparison that is either unconditionally true (every real number satisfies the original inequality) or unconditionally false (no real number does) — a genuinely different kind of answer from an interval, and one that a learner expecting every inequality to produce "some range of x" has no framework for recognising.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — collect the x's on one side, just like in equations, then finish as usual.** 3x + 5 < 7x − 11 becomes, after subtracting 7x from both sides, an ordinary two-step inequality to isolate x. *Upgrade trigger*: the moment isolation requires dividing by a negative coefficient, where "finish as usual" needs the sign-flip rule attached, and the rule can only be checked correctly once collection is already done. *Shelf life*: one session.
2. **Intermediate — a compound inequality is two inequalities sharing a middle expression, solved in lockstep.** a < mx+b < c means both a < mx+b and mx+b < c must hold; every operation is performed on the left bound, the middle, and the right bound simultaneously, never on just one piece. *Upgrade trigger*: a compound inequality requiring division by a negative, where "lockstep" alone doesn't yet specify that *both* directions flip and the bounds swap order. *Shelf life*: durable and remains correct permanently, once the negative-division extension is layered on.
3. **Advanced — recognise, before solving, when a problem's structure signals a degenerate outcome.** If the coefficient of x is identical on both sides of an inequality (after any needed expansion), the variable terms are guaranteed to cancel during collection, and the answer will be a bare true/false statement about the leftover constants, not an interval — this can be anticipated by inspection, before fully solving. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to distinguish, at a glance, a genuinely degenerate setup from one that merely *looks* similar but has different coefficients on each side.
4. **Expert — every linear inequality in one variable partitions the real line into exactly one of three possible outcome shapes: a ray, an empty set, or the whole line — determined entirely by whether the x-coefficients differ (a genuine ray-shaped interval results) or coincide (a degenerate true/false result, all-reals or empty, depending on the surviving constant comparison).** *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the model that makes solving *any* compound or one-sided linear inequality feel like an instance of one classification scheme rather than a collection of separate cases to memorise.

## Why Students Fail
The most structurally damaging failure is attempting to judge the sign-flip decision before the variable terms have been collected: facing 3x + 5 < 7x − 11, a learner reaches directly for "is the coefficient negative?" against the *original*, uncollected form, where no single isolating coefficient yet exists to check — the flip decision is simply not answerable at that stage, and any answer reached this way is coincidental at best. The second major failure occurs in compound inequalities: the learner manipulates only part of the expression — subtracting a constant from the middle term alone while leaving the two bounds unchanged, or dividing only the middle by a negative while forgetting to flip both surrounding inequality symbols — because the "three parts move together" requirement is not obvious from the notation the way it would be if the compound inequality were visibly written as two separate statements. The third failure is a failure of expectation rather than execution: having correctly collected variable terms and found they cancel entirely, the learner does not know what to do with the resulting bare constant statement (6 < 10, or 6 < 4) and either forces a nonsensical "interval" answer out of it or concludes an error must have occurred, because every prior inequality this learner has solved has produced a genuine range of values, and nothing in that experience prepares them for an answer that is simply "always true" or "never true."

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1..MC-3) and its repair actions B01–B03, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — SIGN-FLIP-CHECKED-BEFORE-COLLECTING-VARIABLE-TERMS** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of the single-operation sign-flip check from `math.alg.inequality`, applied prematurely to a form (variable on both sides) where no isolating coefficient yet exists to check.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: attempting to judge a flip directly from 3x + 5 < 7x − 11's original coefficients, before subtracting 7x from both sides.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint hook): "Can the sign-flip rule be applied directly to a variable-on-both-sides inequality, before collecting the variable terms onto one side?" — a "yes" answer confirms MC-1.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — re-walk the collect-then-isolate sequence explicitly, re-anchoring on "collect variable terms first; only then is there a coefficient sign to check."
  - **Verification of death**: given 5x − 3 > 2x + 9, the learner collects variable terms onto one side before making any statement about a sign flip, unprompted.

- **MC-2 — COMPOUND-INEQUALITY-PARTS-MANIPULATED-INDEPENDENTLY** (foundational)
  - **Birth type**: Type 4, notation-induced — a compound inequality's three-part notation (a < expression < c) does not visually signal that every operation must apply to all three simultaneously, inviting a learner to treat the two bounds as fixed labels rather than as parts requiring identical treatment.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: subtracting a constant from the middle expression while leaving one or both bounds unchanged, or dividing only the middle by a negative without flipping both surrounding directions.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint hook): "When solving a compound inequality like a < mx+b < c, can each 'side' be manipulated independently?" — a "yes" answer confirms MC-2.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — re-walk the all-three-parts-together sequence explicitly, re-anchoring on "every operation applies to all three parts simultaneously," including both direction flips under negative multiplication or division.
  - **Verification of death**: given −4 < −x + 2 < 6, the learner applies every operation to all three parts and correctly reorders after a negative-coefficient division, unprompted.

- **MC-3 — EVERY-LINEAR-INEQUALITY-ASSUMED-TO-YIELD-AN-INTERVAL** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation from an unbroken run of prior practice items, every one of which produced a genuine interval, leaving no prior experience of the degenerate outcome to draw on.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: facing 6 < 10 (having cancelled the variable terms), forcing an interval-shaped answer, or concluding an error must have occurred.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint hook): "Must every linear inequality's solution be some genuine interval of real numbers?" — a "yes" answer confirms MC-3.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — re-walk both degenerate cases (always-true and always-false) explicitly, re-anchoring on "canceling variable terms can leave an always-true or always-false statement instead of an interval."
  - **Verification of death**: given 3(x+2) < 3x + 7 alongside 3(x+2) < 3x + 5, the learner correctly identifies which (if either) is degenerate and of which type, unprompted.

## Analogies
- **Best — two glued statements moving as one unit.** A compound inequality is like two people holding opposite ends of a rigid rod: whatever happens to one end (an operation applied) must happen identically to the other end and to the rod itself (the middle expression), or the rigid connection is broken. This is the Blueprint's own P28 conflict-evidence framing and it directly targets MC-2 by making the "all three parts together" requirement a physical, checkable image.
- **Alternative — an unanswerable question asked too early.** "Is the coefficient negative?" asked of an equation whose variable terms haven't yet been collected is like asking "which team is winning?" before the game has started — the question has no answer yet, not a wrong one, because the thing being asked about doesn't exist yet. This targets MC-1's premature-judgment error directly.
- **Story analogy** — a tug-of-war where both teams pull equally hard in the same direction: the rope doesn't move, and the "winner" question (which team is ahead) becomes meaningless rather than merely undecided — mirroring the degenerate case's shift from "which interval" to "always or never."
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "solve each side of the inequality like you would solve two separate equations."** This licenses MC-2 directly by suggesting the two bounds can be treated as independent, self-contained problems rather than as parts of one connected constraint.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "check the sign of x's coefficient to know if you'll need to flip."** Without first specifying "after collecting all variable terms onto one side," this is exactly MC-1's premature check, stated as an instruction.

## Demonstrations
- **The premature-check collision.** Attempt to answer "will this need a flip?" for 3x + 5 < 7x − 11 directly from the original form, showing there is no single coefficient to point to yet — then collect variable terms and show the question becomes answerable only afterward. *Predict, before collecting, whether the question can even be answered yet* — the impossibility itself is the demonstration.
- **The three-parts-in-lockstep trace.** Solve −3 < −2x + 1 < 9 live, writing every operation applied to all three parts on parallel lines, with the negative-division step's double flip (both directions) and reordering made maximally visible. *Predict, before dividing, what will happen to both inequality symbols* — this operationalises Blueprint B02's repair as a standing habit.
- **The degenerate-pair contrast, run live.** Solve 2(x+3) < 2x + 10 and 2(x+3) < 2x + 4 side by side, both reducing to a bare constant comparison, one true and one false. *Predict, before expanding either, whether the variable terms will survive collection* — noticing matching coefficients in advance is the advanced mental model in action.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for the mechanical extension itself (collecting variable terms before checking a flip, operating on all three parts of a compound inequality) — both are procedural consequences of already-secured rules (`math.alg.linear-equation-1var`'s isolation technique, `math.alg.inequality`'s sign-flip rule), not new principles to re-derive. What is genuinely discoverable, and central to this concept's own distinguishing content, is the degenerate-outcome phenomenon: (1) **Need** — "Solve 2(x+3) < 2x + 10. What happens to the x terms?" (2) **Playground** — expand and collect several inequalities of this general shape, some with matching x-coefficients on both sides and some without, recording what happens to the variable in each case. (3) **Invention** — "What does it mean when the x's completely disappear? Is that always a mistake?" (4) **Collision** — offer the paired case 2(x+3) < 2x + 4 immediately after 2(x+3) < 2x + 10, showing the identical cancellation mechanism can produce either a true or a false leftover statement depending only on one constant. (5) **Formalisation** — when the x-coefficients on both sides are identical, the variable cancels entirely, and the answer is determined purely by comparing the remaining constants: always true (all reals) or always false (empty set). (6) **Compression** — "If the x's vanish, look at what's left. Is it a true statement or a false one? That answers the whole problem."

## Teaching Sequence
The collect-before-checking-the-flip rule (TA-A01, targeting MC-1) is sequenced first, ahead of compound inequalities (TA-A02, targeting MC-2), because the sign-flip check itself — already familiar from `math.alg.inequality` — must be re-anchored to occur *after* collection before any more complex inequality shape is introduced; teaching compound inequalities on top of an unresolved MC-1 would conflate "which part of a compound inequality got the operation" with "was the flip judged too early," making diagnosis unreliable. Compound inequalities are addressed before degenerate cases (TA-A03, targeting MC-3) because degenerate outcomes can arise from *either* a simple one-sided inequality or a compound one, and the compound-inequality mechanics (all three parts moving together) must already be secure so that a degenerate compound-inequality item, if one arises later, is not misdiagnosed as a compound-handling error. The Blueprint's own Component 5 sequence (TA-A01 collect-then-check via representation shift, TA-A02 compound-inequality lockstep via conflict evidence, TA-A03 degenerate-case contrast pair, TA-A04 mastery gate) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Demonstration** — the premature-check collision, run on the learner's own attempted early flip-judgment rather than a hypothetical one, so the "this question isn't answerable yet" realisation is felt directly.
- **DO: Worked example, three-parts-in-lockstep trace** — every early compound-inequality problem solved with all three parts written out explicitly on parallel lines, making the simultaneous-operation requirement visually unavoidable.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "will the x's survive collection, or cancel?" asked before fully solving any inequality with the variable on both sides, building the advanced mental model's by-inspection skill.
- **TEST-THINKING: Contrast pair** — the Blueprint's own paired degenerate examples (2(x+3) < 2x+10 versus 2(x+3) < 2x+4), run as a genuine side-by-side comparison rather than presented sequentially.
- **Does NOT fit: introducing compound inequalities before variable-on-both-sides inequalities are independently fluent.** MC-1's premature-flip error, if uncleared, will corrupt the middle-expression isolation step inside a compound inequality just as it would a simple one, making the compound-specific MC-2 harder to isolate diagnostically.
- **Does NOT fit: presenting only non-degenerate practice items.** This never gives MC-3 a chance to surface, and a learner who has never encountered a degenerate outcome has no reason to expect or recognise one on an assessment.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "collect first — THEN check the sign." Slow down on "then," making the sequencing itself audible. Listen for a learner attempting to state a flip decision immediately upon reading a variable-on-both-sides inequality, before any algebra has been performed — this premature verdict is the clearest predictor of MC-1. For compound inequalities, listen for whether the learner narrates all three parts on every step ("subtract one from the left, the middle, AND the right") versus narrating only the middle expression's transformation — the omission of the bounds from the narration predicts MC-2 even when the written work might still be correct by chance. A confident, unprompted "the x's cancel — so this is either always true or never true" is the strongest positive signal this concept produces. Channel reality is owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals
- **A flip judged from the original, uncollected inequality** — MC-1; route to the premature-check collision, never to a bare restatement of "collect first."
- **An operation applied to only some of the three parts of a compound inequality, or only one direction flipped under a negative division** — MC-2, cleanly diagnostic; route to the three-parts-in-lockstep trace.
- **A degenerate result (variable terms cancelled) reported as an error, or forced into an interval shape** — MC-3; route to the degenerate-pair contrast so both possible outcomes (all-real and empty) are seen side by side.
- **Correct collection performed, correct flip applied, and the learner explicitly narrates the sign check as happening "after" collection** — the intended fully-formed response pattern; this narration, not merely a correct final answer, is the strongest evidence MC-1 is genuinely cleared.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A04 gate, MAMR ⌈0.85×5⌉ = 5/5, including its P76 quality-control transfer probe, whose part (c) specifically asks the learner to explain why judging the flip from the *original* coefficient is wrong — a gate passed without that explanatory component does not certify the underlying reasoning behind MC-1's repair, only the mechanical habit of collecting first.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance is "wait, do I flip it or not?" asked while still looking at the original, uncollected inequality. The concept-specific smaller question redirects to the missing prerequisite step: **"Before we even think about flipping anything — are the x's all on one side yet?"** — making the collection step the explicit next action rather than leaving the flip question hanging unanswerably. If the freeze is specifically at a compound inequality, shrink to one bound at a time framed as a connected pair: **"Whatever we do to the middle, we do to this number too. What do we do first?"** — anchoring on the paired-motion principle rather than the full three-part complexity at once. Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.
