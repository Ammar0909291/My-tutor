## Learning Objective
The student will define a Pythagorean triple as an integer solution (a,b,c) to a²+b²=c², apply the primitive-triple parametrization a=m²−n², b=2mn, c=m²+n² (for m>n>0, gcd(m,n)=1) to generate triples, and correctly distinguish primitive triples from their non-primitive integer multiples.

## Core Understanding
A Pythagorean triple is a set of three positive integers (a,b,c) satisfying a²+b²=c² — the same equation `math.geom.pythagorean-theorem` establishes geometrically for a right triangle's sides, but here studied as a purely number-theoretic question: for which integer values does the relation hold exactly? The 3-4-5 triple is the smallest and most familiar example, but there are infinitely many. Every primitive triple (one where a, b, c share no common factor) can be generated by the parametrization a=m²−n², b=2mn, c=m²+n², for any integers m>n>0 with gcd(m,n)=1 and m,n of opposite parity — this is a complete classification, not merely a way to produce examples. Non-primitive triples are simply integer multiples of a primitive one (e.g. 6-8-10 = 2×(3-4-5)); scaling a triple by any positive integer k always produces another valid triple, since (ka)²+(kb)²=k²(a²+b²)=k²c²=(kc)².

## Mental Models
1. **The geometric-question-becomes-arithmetic model**: `math.geom.pythagorean-theorem`'s equation a²+b²=c² is a statement about any real-valued right-triangle sides; asking specifically which integer values satisfy it turns the same equation into a genuinely different, number-theoretic question.
2. **The complete-generator model**: the m,n parametrization is not just a source of examples — it generates every single primitive triple, and no primitive triple exists outside this family.
3. **The scaling-preserves-the-relation model**: multiplying every side of a valid triple by the same positive integer k always produces another valid triple, since squaring distributes over the scalar consistently on both sides of the equation.

## Why Students Fail
The foundational failure is believing any three integers that happen to look reasonably close to satisfying a²+b²=c² (or that are simply "nice round numbers") form a Pythagorean triple, without actually checking the equation holds exactly. A second failure is believing every scaled multiple of a known triple (like 6-8-10 from 3-4-5) is itself a genuinely new, independent triple, rather than recognizing it as structurally derivative of the primitive triple it scales. A third failure is misapplying the m,n parametrization by choosing m,n without the required conditions (m>n>0, opposite parity, gcd(m,n)=1), producing an integer triple that satisfies a²+b²=c² but is not primitive, and mistaking it for one.

## Misconceptions
Authored directly via the birth-taxonomy diagnostic procedure (no Blueprint exists for this concept):

- **APPROXIMATE-TRIPLE-ASSUMED-VALID** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Description**: believing three integers that are merely close to satisfying a²+b²=c² (or simply look like "typical" triangle side numbers) form a genuine Pythagorean triple, without verifying the equation holds exactly.
  - **Birth type**: Type 2, perceptual intuition — numbers that are "roughly the right size" for a right triangle (e.g. 5-6-8) feel plausible without the exact arithmetic check.
  - **Repair approach**: require explicit computation of a²+b² versus c² for any proposed triple before accepting it, contrasting a genuine triple (3-4-5: 9+16=25) against a close-but-invalid one (5-6-8: 25+36=61≠64).

- **SCALED-TRIPLE-TREATED-AS-INDEPENDENT** (Foundational)
  - **Description**: believing every integer multiple of a known triple (e.g. 6-8-10, 9-12-15 from 3-4-5) is a genuinely new, independent triple rather than a scaled copy of the same primitive triple.
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization — since each scaled version does technically satisfy a²+b²=c², the habit of treating distinct-looking numeric triples as distinct discoveries is overextended without checking for a common factor.
  - **Repair approach**: for any given triple, explicitly compute gcd(a,b,c); if greater than 1, divide through to recover the primitive triple it derives from, demonstrating that 6-8-10, 9-12-15, and 3-4-5 are really "the same" triple at different scales.

- **PARAMETRIZATION-CONDITIONS-IGNORED** (Moderate)
  - **Description**: applying the formulas a=m²−n², b=2mn, c=m²+n² with arbitrarily chosen m,n, without the required m>n>0, opposite parity, and gcd(m,n)=1 conditions, producing a valid but non-primitive triple while believing it primitive.
  - **Birth type**: Type 4, notation-induced — the formulas themselves contain no visible reminder of their side conditions, so a learner who has memorized only the three equations can apply them to any m,n pair and get *a* valid triple, without it being the intended primitive one.
  - **Repair approach**: work an explicit case where the conditions are violated (e.g. m,n both even, or gcd(m,n)>1) and show the resulting triple, while satisfying a²+b²=c², shares a common factor and is therefore not primitive — then repeat with valid conditions to produce a genuinely primitive triple.

## Analogies
- **The family-tree framing**: every non-primitive triple is a "descendant" of exactly one primitive "ancestor" triple, related by a pure scaling factor — knowing all the primitive triples and the scaling rule is enough to describe every triple that will ever exist.

## Demonstrations
- Verifying 3-4-5 (9+16=25 ✓) against 5-6-8 (25+36=61≠64 ✗), targeting the approximate-triple misconception.
- Computing gcd(6,8,10)=2 and dividing through to recover 3-4-5, showing 6-8-10 and 9-12-15 are scaled copies of the same primitive triple, targeting the scaled-triple misconception.
- Generating a genuine primitive triple from m=2,n=1 (a=3,b=4,c=5) versus an m,n pair violating the conditions (e.g. m=4,n=2, both even), whose output shares a common factor, targeting the parametrization-conditions misconception.

## Discovery Questions
1. "Does 5-6-8 form a Pythagorean triple, just because the numbers look about the right size for a right triangle?"
2. "Is 9-12-15 a genuinely new triple, or something derived from 3-4-5?"
3. "Does the m,n formula always produce a primitive triple, no matter which m and n you pick?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Verify a candidate triple by direct computation of a²+b² versus c², contrasting a valid case against a near-miss.
2. Compute gcd(a,b,c) for a scaled triple and recover its primitive ancestor, distinguishing primitive from non-primitive.
3. Apply the m,n parametrization under its required conditions to generate a genuine primitive triple, then show a conditions-violating choice fails to produce one.
4. Mastery gate: verify a candidate triple, identify whether a given triple is primitive, and generate a new primitive triple from valid m,n values.

## Voice Teaching Notes
When a student proposes a candidate triple, ask "have you actually computed a²+b² and compared it to c², or does it just look about right?" as a standing check directly targeting the approximate-triple misconception.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If the approximate-triple misconception persists, forbid accepting any proposed triple without first writing out the explicit arithmetic a²+b² and c² side by side, comparing the two computed values digit by digit, until exact verification becomes the automatic first step.

## Memory Hooks
- "A candidate triple isn't valid until a²+b² actually equals c² — not just 'close enough.'"
- "Every scaled multiple of a triple is the same triple in disguise — check the gcd."
- "The m,n formula only gives a primitive triple under its stated conditions — m>n>0, opposite parity, no common factor."
