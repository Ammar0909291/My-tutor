## Learning Objective
- The learner can compute $\partial f/\partial x$ and $\partial f/\partial y$ for polynomial, exponential, and trigonometric two-variable functions by treating the OTHER variable as a constant and applying all single-variable differentiation rules.
- The learner can compute second-order partials $f_{xx}$, $f_{yy}$, $f_{xy}$, $f_{yx}$, and verify Clairaut's theorem ($f_{xy}=f_{yx}$ when both are continuous) — recognizing the order of differentiation genuinely does not matter for smooth functions.
- The learner can distinguish $\partial f/\partial x$ (the rate of change in the pure $x$-direction, $y$ held fixed) from the TOTAL rate of change of $f$ along an arbitrary path where both $x$ and $y$ vary simultaneously.

## Core Understanding
A partial derivative $\partial f/\partial x$ measures how $f(x,y)$ changes when $x$ moves and $y$ is deliberately FROZEN — treated exactly like a fixed numerical constant, not a variable. The computational rule follows directly: to find $\partial f/\partial x$, treat every occurrence of $y$ as a constant and apply the familiar single-variable differentiation rules to whatever remains as a function of $x$ alone; symmetrically for $\partial f/\partial y$, freezing $x$. Geometrically, $\partial f/\partial x$ at a point is the slope of the curve obtained by SLICING the surface $z=f(x,y)$ with the plane $y=y_0$ — one specific directional tangent line among many possible ones at that point, not "the" derivative in any single, unique sense. From the two first-order partials, FOUR second-order partials arise: $f_{xx}$, $f_{yy}$, and the two MIXED partials $f_{xy}$ (differentiate in $y$ first, then $x$) and $f_{yx}$ (differentiate in $x$ first, then $y$). Clairaut's theorem guarantees that for any function with continuous mixed partials — essentially every smooth function encountered in ordinary calculus — $f_{xy}=f_{yx}$: the ORDER of differentiation genuinely does not matter, a deep result rather than a coincidence, and it grants the practical freedom to choose whichever computation order produces simpler intermediate expressions. Finally, $\partial f/\partial x$ answers a narrow question — "how fast does $f$ change if ONLY $x$ moves, with $y$ held fixed?" — and must not be confused with the TOTAL rate of change along an arbitrary path where both $x$ and $y$ vary together; that total rate combines BOTH partials, weighted by how fast each variable moves along the path (the multivariable chain rule), and a partial derivative alone captures only one piece of it.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — "a partial derivative is differentiating with respect to one letter."** The freezing-the-other-variable discipline is not yet reliably applied, and terms mixing both variables are differentiated with respect to whichever variable happens to be salient. *Upgrade trigger*: computing $\partial f/\partial x$ for a function containing a $y^2$ term and mistakenly producing a nonzero $y$-dependent result from that term.
2. **Intermediate — "freeze the other variable as if it were a specific number, then differentiate normally."** The core computational discipline is now reliable for first-order partials. *Upgrade trigger*: being asked to compute a SECOND-order mixed partial, and needing to decide whether the order of differentiation matters.
3. **Advanced — "mixed partials $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ agree for smooth functions (Clairaut's theorem), so the order of differentiation is a free choice; and $\partial f/\partial x$ alone is only ONE piece of the total rate of change along a path where both variables move."** Both the order-independence of mixed partials and the partial-versus-total distinction are now explicit. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to combine both partial derivatives into a single vector (the gradient) that points in the direction of steepest increase.
4. **Expert — partial derivatives are the entry point into a broader multivariable differential-calculus toolkit (gradient, directional derivative, multivariable chain rule), each of which recombines the SAME underlying partial-derivative computations in different, purpose-specific ways.** The learner anticipates how partial derivatives will be reassembled into these more powerful tools before being taught each one individually. *Shelf life*: permanent.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant failure carries forward the single-variable habit of "differentiate everything in sight" into a setting where one variable must be deliberately frozen: when computing $\partial f/\partial x$, a learner differentiates $y$-containing terms WITH RESPECT TO $y$ (e.g. writing $d/dx(3y^2)=6y$ instead of the correct 0, since $y^2$ is a constant multiplier when $x$ is the variable of interest) — an overgeneralization of the reflexive "take the derivative of everything" instinct from single-variable calculus, extended into a context that specifically requires suppressing that instinct for one variable (MC-1, OTHER-VARIABLE-TREATED-AS-VARIABLE) — a Type 1 overgeneralization. A second, distinct failure treats the subscript ORDER in $f_{xy}$ versus $f_{yx}$ as inherently meaningful in the way order matters for many other mathematical operations (matrix multiplication, function composition), assuming the two mixed partials must generally differ and computing them expecting different results — missing that Clairaut's theorem guarantees equality for essentially every smooth function this concept encounters (MC-2, MIXED-PARTIALS-NONCOMMUTATIVE) — a Type 6 analogy overextension, borrowing the "order matters" pattern from other operations where it genuinely does apply and extending it to a setting where a specific theorem guarantees the opposite. A third failure carries forward the single-variable identification of "the derivative" as automatically THE rate of change of a function, extending that identification directly into the multivariable setting — treating $\partial f/\partial x$ as capturing the full rate of change of $f$ along ANY path, rather than recognizing it as only the $x$-directional piece with $y$ deliberately frozen (MC-3, PARTIAL-IS-TOTAL-DERIVATIVE) — again a Type 1 overgeneralization, this time of the 1D identification of "the derivative" with "the" rate of change, extended past the point where multiple, direction-dependent rates now genuinely coexist.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 2 registry (MC-1, MC-2, MC-3) and its own repair sequences B-MC1–B-MC3. **The Blueprint designates MC-1 as the single "Foundational" misconception but carries no explicit birth-type column**; all three classifications below are independently assigned here.

- **MC-1 — OTHER-VARIABLE-TREATED-AS-VARIABLE** (the Blueprint's own designated "Foundational" misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization. The reflexive single-variable habit of differentiating every term in sight is extended, unmodified, into a setting that specifically requires suppressing that habit for whichever variable is being held fixed.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: computing $\partial f/\partial x$ for $f(x,y)=3x^2y^2$ as $\partial f/\partial x=6xy\cdot2y=12xy^2$ — differentiating the $y^2$ factor as if $y$ were the variable of interest.
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's A01 P49 checkpoint): $f(x,y)=3x^2y^3-7y^2$; find $\partial f/\partial x$ — the correct answer $6xy^3$ has the $-7y^2$ term contribute exactly 0.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Sequence B-MC1 — re-anchor on treating the other variable as an unspecified numeric constant $c$, substituting it back in only after differentiating.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh two-variable function, the learner correctly identifies which terms are genuine constants (in the variable being differentiated) and produces zero contribution from them, without prompting.

- **MC-2 — MIXED-PARTIALS-NONCOMMUTATIVE** (unranked by the Blueprint beyond its registry entry)
  - **Birth type**: Type 6, analogy overextension. The "order matters" pattern, genuinely true for other mathematical operations like matrix multiplication or function composition, is over-applied to mixed partial differentiation, where Clairaut's theorem specifically guarantees the opposite for smooth functions.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: expecting $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ to differ and computing them as if verifying a genuinely open question, rather than confirming an already-guaranteed equality.
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's A03 P49 checkpoint): $f(x,y)=x^2y+e^{xy}$; find $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ and verify they agree.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Sequence B-MC2 — re-anchor on Clairaut's theorem as a deep, general result: for well-behaved functions, the order of differentiation genuinely does not matter, and either order may be chosen for computational convenience.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh mixed-partial computation, the learner chooses whichever differentiation order produces simpler intermediate expressions, confident the result will match the other order.

- **MC-3 — PARTIAL-IS-TOTAL-DERIVATIVE** (unranked by the Blueprint beyond its registry entry)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization. The single-variable identification of "the derivative" with "the" rate of change of a function is carried forward unmodified into a setting where multiple, direction-dependent rates now coexist, and $\partial f/\partial x$ is only one of them.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: treating $\partial f/\partial x$ as capturing how fast $f$ changes along an arbitrary path through $(x,y)$, rather than specifically the $x$-directional rate with $y$ frozen.
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's B-MC3 detector): for $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ moving along $x=t,y=t$, $df/dt=4t$ while $\partial f/\partial x=2t$ alone — these genuinely differ.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Sequence B-MC3 — re-anchor on the multivariable chain rule, $df/dt=(\partial f/\partial x)(dx/dt)+(\partial f/\partial y)(dy/dt)$, showing the partial is only one piece of the total.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh path through the domain, the learner correctly combines BOTH partials via the chain rule to find the total rate of change, rather than reporting a single partial alone.

## Analogies
- **Best — reading a topographic map's slope in only one compass direction at a time.** Standing at a point on a hillside, the slope you feel walking due east (holding your north-south position fixed) is a completely different number from the slope walking due north — exactly like $\partial f/\partial x$ and $\partial f/\partial y$ being two different directional slopes at the same point.
- **Alternative — a recipe scaled in only one ingredient at a time.** Doubling only the flour while holding every other ingredient exactly fixed changes the result differently than doubling only the sugar — exactly like freezing $y$ and varying only $x$ (or vice versa) isolates one variable's specific contribution.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "computing f_xy and f_yx is like computing two different things that might disagree."** This phrasing licenses MC-2 directly, implying an open question with two potentially different answers, when Clairaut's theorem guarantees agreement for smooth functions. Say "computing $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ two different ways is a CHECK, not a race between two competing answers — for smooth functions, they always match" instead.

## Demonstrations
- **The freeze-and-check verification.** For $f(x,y)=3x^2y^2$, substitute a specific number for $y$ (say $y=2$), differentiate the resulting single-variable function of $x$, and compare against the general formula $\partial f/\partial x=6xy^2$ evaluated at $y=2$. *Predict, before substituting, whether the general formula's value at $y=2$ will match the direct single-variable computation.* Confirming agreement — and catching a disagreement if the $y^2$ term was incorrectly differentiated — is the demonstration for MC-1.
- **The Clairaut verification.** For $f(x,y)=x^3y^2$, compute $f_{xy}$ by differentiating $f_y$ with respect to $x$, and $f_{yx}$ by differentiating $f_x$ with respect to $y$, independently. *Predict, before computing both, whether the two orders will produce the same result.* Getting the IDENTICAL result both ways is the demonstration for MC-2.
- **The path-versus-partial contrast.** For $f(x,y)=x^2+y^2$ moving along the path $x=t,y=t$, compute the TOTAL rate of change $df/dt=4t$ directly, then compare against $\partial f/\partial x=2t$ alone. *Predict, before computing both, whether the partial alone will match the total rate along this path.* Getting two DIFFERENT values ($4t$ versus $2t$) is the demonstration for MC-3.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction is the argued call for the formal limit definition and computational rule themselves (best introduced explicitly per the Blueprint's own Concrete-Pictorial-Abstract sequence), but the freeze-and-check verification (MC-1) and the path-versus-partial contrast (MC-3) are both genuinely discoverable by direct trial.
1. **Need** — "For $f(x,y)=3x^2y^2$, substitute $y=2$ and differentiate in $x$. Now substitute $y=3$ and differentiate in $x$ again. Do both match the general formula $6xy^2$ evaluated at those $y$-values?" They should, if computed correctly.
2. **Playground** — try the freeze-and-check verification on a couple more two-variable functions.
3. **Invention** — "Why does the total rate of change along a diagonal path differ from just $\partial f/\partial x$ alone?" Let the learner connect it to the missing contribution from $y$ also changing.
4. **Collision** — confront a learner who reported $\partial f/\partial x$ as "the" rate of change along a path with the direct disagreement between $df/dt$ and $\partial f/\partial x$ computed along that specific path.
5. **Formalisation** — state the multivariable chain rule precisely: $df/dt=(\partial f/\partial x)(dx/dt)+(\partial f/\partial y)(dy/dt)$.
6. **Compression** — "Freeze one, move the other — never both at once, unless you're combining them deliberately."

## Teaching Sequence
The freeze-the-other-variable computational discipline (per the Blueprint's own A01, targeting MC-1) must be established FIRST, since every subsequent skill — second-order partials, Clairaut's theorem, the partial-versus-total distinction — presupposes fluent, reliable first-order partial computation. The worked-example pair (per the Blueprint's own A02) extends this fluency across polynomial, exponential, and trigonometric functions before introducing any new conceptual content. The second-order partials and Clairaut's theorem (per the Blueprint's own A03, targeting MC-2) follow, since mixed partials are only meaningful once first-order partials are already reliable. The partial-versus-total-derivative distinction (also folded into A03, targeting MC-3) is placed LAST among the conceptual content, since it requires the learner to already be comfortable computing individual partials before contrasting them against a combined, path-dependent total. Turn-level scripts for A01–A04 are owned by the Blueprint's Component 4 and are not restated here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Worked Example** — the freeze-and-check computation (Blueprint's Stage C, freezing $y=2$ in $f(x,y)=x^2y+3y^2$ and differentiating in $x$), establishing the core computational discipline concretely. First action; anchors MC-1's repair before any abstract rule is stated.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "Will differentiating $-7y^2$ with respect to $x$ give $-14y$, or something else?" asked BEFORE working a checkpoint problem containing exactly this term. Surfaces MC-1 in one turn.
- **DO: Demonstration** — the Clairaut verification (Blueprint's A03 contrast pair), computing $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ via two independent orders and confirming agreement.
- **TEST-THINKING: Error Analysis** — "A student computed $\partial f/\partial x$ for a diagonal-path problem and reported it as the FULL rate of change along the path. What's missing?" targets MC-3 directly.

## Assessment Signals
- **Differentiates a term containing the frozen variable with respect to that frozen variable, rather than treating it as a constant** — MC-1. Route to the freeze-and-check verification, on the exact function in question.
- **Expects or treats $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$ as potentially different results requiring independent verification of "which is correct"** — MC-2. Route to the Clairaut verification, on the exact function in question.
- **Reports a single partial derivative as the complete rate of change along an arbitrary path where both variables vary** — MC-3. Route to the path-versus-partial contrast, on the exact function and path in question.
- **Correctly freezes the appropriate variable for each partial, confirms mixed partials agree via Clairaut's theorem, and combines both partials via the chain rule when a path's total rate of change is required** — the intended target state.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's A04 gate, MAMR 4/5 (⌈0.8×5⌉=4). ## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance here is "why does this y-squared term just disappear when I take the x-partial?" — a reasonable question given how automatic differentiating every visible term feels after single-variable calculus. The concept-specific smaller question returns to a direct substitution: **"Replace y with the specific number 5 everywhere in the function. Now what does that y-squared term look like — is it still changing as x changes?"** The learner substitutes and sees the term become a fixed number, unaffected by $x$. Then return: "exactly — once $y$ is frozen, that term is just a constant, and the derivative of a constant is zero. The 'disappearing' term was never contributing anything to the x-direction in the first place." If the frustration is instead about the partial-versus-total distinction, shrink to the bare check: **"Is $y$ actually staying fixed along this path, or is it changing too?"** Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.
