## Learning Objective

The learner can: state that an improper fraction has numerator ≥
denominator, representing a quantity ≥1, and that "improper" is a
historical name meaning "exceeds one whole," never "invalid"; convert
an improper fraction to a mixed number (divide, quotient=whole,
remainder=new numerator, denominator UNCHANGED) and a mixed number to
an improper fraction (whole×denominator+numerator, over the same
denominator), in both directions; and explain WHY each conversion
procedure works (what each step actually COUNTS), not merely execute
it mechanically.

## Core Understanding

`math.arith.fractions` already establishes that numerator counts parts
and denominator names part size. An **improper fraction** has
numerator≥denominator (e.g. 7/4), representing a quantity ≥1 whole —
critically, this is NOT an error or invalid form; "improper" is a
historical label meaning "the fraction exceeds one whole," never
"incorrect." **Conversion, improper→mixed**: divide numerator by
denominator; the QUOTIENT becomes the whole-number part; the REMAINDER
becomes the new numerator; the DENOMINATOR STAYS THE SAME (11/4:
11÷4=2 remainder 3 → 2¾, since the size of each remaining piece is
still ¼, unchanged by the division). **Conversion, mixed→improper**:
multiply the whole number by the denominator, add the numerator, keep
the denominator (2⅓: 2 wholes×3=6 thirds, plus 1 more third=7 thirds=
7/3) — the multiplication COUNTS the total number of denominator-sized
pieces contributed by the whole-number portion; addition then appends
the fractional part's own pieces. Both forms name the EXACT SAME point
on the number line (7/4 and 1¾ are the same location) — improper form
is generally preferred for computation (adding/multiplying fractions is
more direct without a separate whole-number part to track), while mixed
form is preferred for everyday communication and final presentation
where the whole-number part carries intuitive meaning.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "a fraction must always be less than 1; if the
  top number is bigger than the bottom, something is wrong"**: the
  learner treats numerator<denominator as a structural REQUIREMENT of
  what a fraction is, rather than one of several valid configurations.
  Shelf-life warning: this model rejects legitimate improper-fraction
  answers as errors, blocking acceptance of any arithmetic result
  ≥1 expressed in improper form.
- **Intermediate model — "improper fractions are valid, and I can
  convert between improper and mixed form using the divide-and-
  remainder or multiply-and-add procedures"**: the learner correctly
  accepts and converts improper fractions, but may execute the
  procedures as memorized formulas without explaining what each step
  actually counts, or may lose track of which number is the
  denominator during improper-to-mixed conversion. Upgrade trigger:
  being asked WHY the multiplication step in mixed→improper conversion
  produces the correct total.
- **Advanced model — "both forms are the SAME quantity, generated by
  precisely counting denominator-sized pieces; improper form is
  preferred for computation, mixed form for communication, and the
  choice between them is a strategic one, not a correctness one"**: the
  learner fluently explains the counting-story behind both conversion
  directions and selects the more convenient form based on the task at
  hand. Upgrade trigger: being asked to explain, using a piece-counting
  argument (not the formula), why converting back after converting
  forward must always return the original number.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner still believing improper
  fractions are inherently wrong (beginner model, directly triggering
  MC-1) should not be pushed toward conversion fluency or strategic
  form-selection (advanced model) before the validity of improper
  fractions is fully accepted — MC-1 is FOUNDATIONAL per the
  Blueprint's own MAMR.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant, FOUNDATIONAL failure treats "a fraction must be less
than 1" as a structural requirement rather than one possible
configuration, so any fraction with numerator≥denominator (like 7/3)
feels inherently "wrong" or "invalid" — this directly blocks accepting
legitimate arithmetic results (e.g., a sum of two proper fractions that
happens to exceed 1). A second failure, once improper fractions are
accepted, executes the mixed-to-improper conversion procedure
correctly but cannot explain WHY multiplying the whole number by the
denominator and adding the numerator produces the right total — the
procedure is applied as a memorized formula rather than a meaningful
piece-count. A third failure, in the improper-to-mixed direction,
correctly divides but then places the remainder over 1 rather than
retaining the original denominator (e.g., converting 11/4 as "2
remainder 3" into "2 3/1" instead of "2¾"), missing that the remaining
piece is still the SAME size as before division — the denominator
never changes.

## Misconceptions

Reusing the Blueprint's Misconception Registry (Educational Brain v1.0
format), birth-type classification added per this program's diagnostic
procedure — not re-derived:

### MC-1: Improper fractions are "wrong" (Foundational; Type 3 — language contamination)
**Trigger**: writes that 7/3 is "invalid" or "wrong" because the
numerator is bigger than the denominator.
**Diagnostic note**: classified Type 3 — the everyday-English
connotation of "improper" (meaning "incorrect" or "inappropriate") is
imported directly as the mathematical meaning, rather than the learner
overgeneralizing from limited numeric examples.
**Repair**: proper fraction (numerator<denominator, represents <1
whole), improper fraction (numerator≥denominator, represents ≥1
whole), and mixed number are ALL valid representations — 9/4=2¼=the
same quantity, different notation. "Improper" is a historical name
meaning "exceeds the whole," not "incorrect." Concrete grounding: 9
real pizza slices, each ¼ of a pizza, is a perfectly real quantity —
9/4 pizzas.
**MAMR**: FOUNDATIONAL — must be cleared before MC-2 or MC-3, since
conceptual acceptance of the improper form is a precondition for
engaging meaningfully with either conversion procedure.

### MC-2: Mixed-to-improper without understanding (Moderate; Type 1 — overgeneralization)
**Trigger**: correctly computes 3⅕→16/5 (3×5+1=16) but cannot explain
WHY the procedure works — treats it as a memorized formula.
**Repair**: every whole is secretly denominator/denominator — 3 wholes
=3×5/5=15/5. The multiplication COUNTS the total number of fifths
contributed by the whole-number portion; adding the fractional part's
own numerator (1) gives the grand total (16 fifths=16/5).

### MC-3: Improper-to-mixed denominator loss (Moderate; Type 1 — overgeneralization)
**Trigger**: uses long division correctly (11÷4=2 remainder 3) but
writes the result as "2 3/1" instead of "2¾" — placing the remainder
over 1 rather than retaining the original denominator.
**Repair**: the denominator names the SIZE of each piece — it does not
change just because the pieces are being split into "wholes" and
"leftover." 13/4 means pieces of size ¼; after dividing, the remaining
piece is STILL size ¼, so the denominator stays 4: 13÷4=3 remainder 1
→ 3¼ (not "3 1/1").

## Analogies

**Primary — pizza slices beyond one pizza (Blueprint's own analogy)**:
ordering 9 slices from a pizza cut into 4 means you have 9/4 pizzas —
a perfectly real, valid quantity: 2 whole pizzas plus ¼ of a third. The
fraction 9/4 describes this real amount exactly; there is nothing
"wrong" about it.

**Secondary — the round-trip guarantee (targets confidence, not a
specific misconception)**: converting a number to improper form and
then back to mixed form (or vice versa) must always return the
original — this built-in check is a genuine mathematical guarantee, not
a coincidence, since both directions describe the identical
piece-count from two different angles.

**Anti-analogy to retire**: "A fraction bigger than 1 doesn't really
make sense — fractions are supposed to be PARTS of a whole, smaller
than the whole." This directly invites MC-1 by treating "part of a
whole" as necessarily meaning "less than the whole," rather than
"measured in whole-sized or sub-whole-sized pieces," which
accommodates quantities of any size.

## Demonstrations

**Improper-to-mixed with denominator retention (breaks MC-3)**: 11/4:
11÷4=2 remainder 3 → 2¾ (NOT "2 3/1") — each remaining piece is still
size ¼.

**Mixed-to-improper with counting story (breaks MC-2)**: 4⅔: 4 wholes
=4×3/3=12/3 (12 thirds from the whole-number portion); plus the
fractional part's 2/3; total=14/3. Check: 14÷3=4 remainder 2=4⅔ ✓.

**Number-line placement**: 7/3 is 7 steps of size ⅓ from zero; since
6/3=2, 7/3 sits one step past 2, at 2⅓ — the SAME point on the number
line, confirming the equivalence directly.

**Form selection (Blueprint's own comparison)**: adding 2⅓+1⅔ is
simpler in improper form (7/3+5/3=12/3=4, one step); describing a
board's length as "4¾ feet" (mixed form) is clearer for everyday
communication than "19/4 feet."

## Discovery Questions

Present a strip-building activity from the Blueprint's own TA-A01: ask
the learner to use fraction strips to show 5 thirds, and observe how
many complete strips fill and how many thirds are left over — the
learner discovers 5/3=1⅔ largely unaided, directly experiencing the
improper-to-mixed relationship before either conversion procedure is
stated formally. Recommendation: guided discovery for the strip-
building insight (directly experiential); direct instruction for the
formal conversion algorithms and their WHY explanations (MC-2/MC-3's
repair), since the piece-counting justification requires explicit
scaffolding not independently rediscoverable from physical
manipulation alone.

## Voice Teaching Notes

**Register**: Never say "improper" implying "wrong" — say "improper
means it exceeds one whole, not that it's incorrect," reinforcing the
historical-naming distinction every time the term is introduced.

**Wait-time**: After presenting a division for improper-to-mixed
conversion (e.g., 17÷6=2 remainder 5), give extended wait-time before
revealing what denominator to use for the remainder — let the learner
attempt the conversion and notice the denominator-retention question
themselves.

**Load-bearing sentences**:
- "Improper doesn't mean wrong — it means the fraction goes beyond one
  whole; both forms name the exact same quantity."
- "The denominator names the SIZE of each piece — it never changes
  just because you're splitting into wholes and leftover."

**Voice channel note**: See `foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Likeliest utterance: "wait, isn't a fraction supposed to be less than
1 — how can the top number be bigger than the bottom?" — the concept-
specific smaller question: "if you order 9 slices from a pizza cut
into 4, is that a real, describable amount?" reframes the confusion
from "fractions must be less than 1 by definition" (MC-1's language-
contamination root) to "fractions describe any quantity measured in
equal-sized pieces, regardless of how many," directly isolating MC-1
with a concrete real-world quantity that plainly exceeds one whole.
