## Learning Objective

The learner can: define a corollary as a result that follows easily
from an already-proven theorem, requiring only a brief additional
argument or direct substitution; recognize that a corollary's proof
meets the identical rigor standard as any theorem's proof, directly
extending `math.found.theorem`'s own role-versus-rigor distinction; and
correctly distinguish a corollary (a NEW, distinct statement following
quickly from a theorem) from a mere restatement or special case that
adds no genuinely new content.

## Core Understanding

A corollary is a result that follows easily from a theorem, often
requiring only a brief additional argument or a direct substitution.
`math.found.theorem` already establishes that "corollary" names an
EXPOSITORY ROLE, not a lower standard of rigor — a corollary's own
proof, however brief, is exactly as complete and rigorous as any
theorem's; "brief" describes the LENGTH of the additional argument
needed, not a relaxation of what counts as proof. A genuine corollary
states something NEW — a distinct consequence worth naming separately
— even though deriving it from the parent theorem takes little extra
work; this distinguishes a corollary from a mere restatement of the
theorem in different notation, or an unremarkable special case that
adds no additional content worth highlighting. Corollaries typically
arise when a theorem's proof, once complete, makes some FURTHER fact
almost immediate — substituting a specific value, applying the theorem
to a special case, or combining it with one other already-known fact —
and an author judges that further fact significant enough to state and
prove (briefly) as its own labeled result, rather than leaving it
implicit for the reader to notice.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "a corollary is a theorem that's not important
  enough to be called a theorem"**: the learner treats "corollary" as
  signaling reduced significance in the same way `math.found.theorem`'s
  own MC-1 mistakenly treats "lemma," here applied to the sibling
  label. Shelf-life warning: this model cannot explain why some
  corollaries are cited and used more often than the theorems they
  followed from.
- **Intermediate model — "a corollary is a fully-proven, genuinely new
  result that follows quickly from an already-established theorem"**:
  the learner correctly separates role from rigor and recognizes a
  corollary's newness, but may still occasionally mistake a mere
  restatement of the parent theorem (adding nothing new) for a genuine
  corollary. Upgrade trigger: being shown a "corollary" that is actually
  just the parent theorem reworded, and asked whether it qualifies.
- **Advanced model — "corollaries mark genuinely new, quickly-derived
  consequences, distinguished from restatements by whether they add new
  content"**: the learner reliably distinguishes a genuine corollary
  from a trivial restatement, and recognizes WHY an author chose to
  highlight a specific quick consequence as worth its own label. Upgrade
  trigger: being asked to propose a plausible corollary of an unfamiliar
  theorem, distinct from a mere restatement.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner who still treats corollaries as
  lesser results (beginner model) should not be pushed into the
  restatement-versus-genuine-corollary distinction (advanced model)
  before the role-versus-rigor foundation, already established by
  `math.found.theorem`, is itself secure specifically for corollaries.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant failure — a direct, specific instance of `math.found.
theorem`'s own MC-1 (labels treated as a certainty hierarchy) — treats
"corollary" as signaling a less important or less rigorously proven
result than a "theorem," when in fact a corollary's proof, however
brief, meets an identical standard; "brief" is a statement about the
LENGTH of the required argument, given the parent theorem is already
available, not about the rigor of that argument. A second, independent
failure fails to distinguish a genuine corollary (which states
something new, even if quickly derived) from a trivial restatement or
unremarkable special case of the parent theorem that adds no additional
content — mistaking mere rewording for a genuine additional result.

## Misconceptions

Applying the birth-taxonomy diagnostic procedure directly, since no
Blueprint exists yet.

**MC-1 — "A corollary is less important or less rigorously proven than
a theorem" (Type 1, overgeneralization — a direct, specific instance of
`math.found.theorem`'s own MC-1, cited by reference, not re-derived,
applied specifically to the corollary label)**: believing a corollary's
brevity signals reduced rigor or reduced significance. Full trigger/
root-cause/repair pattern already established in `math.found.theorem`'s
Misconceptions section — this entry's own contribution is MC-2, which
that entry does not cover.

**MC-2 — "Any brief consequence of a theorem, even a mere restatement,
counts as a corollary" (Type 1, overgeneralization — "follows quickly"
is over-extended to include cases that add nothing genuinely new)**
- *Why*: since corollaries are characterized by requiring little
  additional argument, it's easy to assume ANYTHING derivable with
  little effort qualifies, including trivial rewordings that don't
  actually state anything the parent theorem didn't already say
  directly.
- *Symptom*: labeling a mere restatement of a theorem (in different
  notation, or applied to a trivially special case with no new content)
  as a "corollary," when it adds nothing beyond the original statement.
- *Detection probe*: present a theorem and a proposed "corollary" that
  is actually just the theorem restated in different symbols, and ask
  whether this genuinely qualifies as a corollary.
- *Recovery*: "a genuine corollary states something NEW — a distinct
  consequence, even if quickly derived. If the 'corollary' says nothing
  the original theorem didn't already directly say, it's a restatement,
  not a corollary. Check: does this add information, or just reword
  existing information?"
- *Verification*: the learner correctly distinguishes a genuine
  corollary (new content, quick derivation) from a mere restatement
  (no new content) when presented with both.

## Analogies

**Primary — the immediate payoff of a rule change**: If a new store
policy states "all electronics are 20% off" (the theorem), an immediate
corollary might be "a $500 laptop now costs $400" — a genuinely new,
specific fact that follows almost automatically by substitution, worth
stating explicitly because it answers a concrete question a shopper
might have, even though deriving it from the policy took no real
additional reasoning. This is different from simply restating "all
electronics are 20% off" in different words, which would add nothing.

**Anti-analogy to retire**: "A corollary is just a footnote to the real
theorem." "Footnote" implies diminished status, directly reinforcing
MC-1's mistaken hierarchy.

## Demonstrations

**Genuine corollary vs. mere restatement**: present a theorem (e.g.
about a general property holding for all elements of some class), then
present two candidate follow-ups side by side — one a genuine corollary
(a specific, new, quickly-derived consequence, like applying the
general theorem to a particular case that reveals something not stated
directly by the general form) and one a mere restatement (the identical
claim in different notation) — the learner sorts which is which.

**Equally rigorous, briefly argued**: walk a real corollary's proof
step by step, showing that despite its brevity (often just one or two
lines, directly citing and applying the parent theorem), every step is
fully justified — exactly as rigorous as the parent theorem's own,
longer proof.

## Discovery Questions

Present a theorem and ask the learner: "given this is now established,
what SPECIFIC new fact can you derive almost immediately, with very
little extra work?" — guiding discovery of the corollary-generation
process before the term is formally introduced. Recommendation: guided
discovery for identifying plausible corollaries of a given theorem
(directly generative and experiential); direct instruction for the
rigor-equivalence correction (MC-1) and the restatement-versus-
corollary distinction (MC-2), since both require precise contrast not
naturally rediscovered.

## Teaching Sequence

MC-1 (corollary treated as less rigorous) is addressed first,
re-anchoring `math.found.theorem`'s own existing repair applied
specifically to this label, since it is the more consequential and more
common error. MC-2 (restatement mistaken for genuine corollary) is
addressed second, as a more precise content-versus-notation
distinction that becomes relevant once the rigor question is settled.

## Tutor Actions

From `../../teaching-actions/`: **Demonstration** (the equally-rigorous
brief-proof walkthrough, the primary action targeting MC-1) →
**Matching** (sort candidate follow-ups into genuine corollaries versus
mere restatements, targeting MC-2) → **Discovery/generative exercise**
(propose plausible corollaries of a given theorem, consolidating both
lessons). **What doesn't fit**: a full treatment of proof-length as a
formal metric — this concept's scope is the informal, expository
judgment of "brief but genuinely new," not a quantitative standard.

## Voice Teaching Notes

**Register**: Consistent about the new-content test — every candidate
corollary should be checked with "does this say something the original
theorem didn't already directly say?"

**Wait-time**: When a corollary is introduced, pause and ask "what NEW
fact does this state, beyond the original theorem?" before confirming —
surfaces MC-2 directly.

**Load-bearing sentences**:
- "A corollary is exactly as rigorously proven as any theorem — brief
  doesn't mean weaker."
- "A genuine corollary says something new — a plain restatement isn't
  one."

**Voice channel note**: See `foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals

No item bank exists in a Blueprint for this concept; diagnostic

rigor assumption, assessment should explicitly ask "is this corollary's

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Likeliest utterance: "so a corollary isn't a 'real' proven result, just
a quick add-on?" — the concept-specific smaller question: "does the
corollary's proof skip any justification a longer proof wouldn't skip —
or is it just SHORTER because the theorem already did most of the
work?" reframes the confusion from "brief means less rigorous" to
"brief means the theorem already supplied most of what's needed" —
directly isolating MC-1's role-versus-rigor conflation.

## Memory Hooks

**Type**: declarative (the corollary role definition, its equal-rigor
status, and the new-content-versus-restatement test). Review form:
fresh theorems, asking the learner to propose a plausible genuine
corollary and distinguish it from a mere restatement, keeping both
misconceptions' guard-rails active. Interleaving partners:
`math.found.theorem` (the equal-rigor standard this entry directly
extends) and `math.found.lemma` (the sibling role addressing the
opposite direction — results that SUPPORT a theorem, rather than
results that FOLLOW from one).

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: `math.found.theorem` (the rigor standard both concepts
  share), `math.found.lemma` (the paired sibling role).
- **Far**: legal reasoning's "it follows that..." consequences drawn
  from an established ruling; policy analysis deriving specific,
  concrete implications from a general rule change.
- **Real-world**: any argument that draws out a specific, useful
  implication from a general established fact, worth stating explicitly
  even though the derivation itself is quick.
- **Expert transfer**: the learner, having just established or read an
  unfamiliar theorem, automatically scans for genuinely new, quickly-
  derivable consequences worth stating as corollaries, rather than
  treating the theorem's implications as exhausted by its own
  statement.
