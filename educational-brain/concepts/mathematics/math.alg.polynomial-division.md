## Learning Objective
- The learner can divide a polynomial p(x) by a divisor d(x) using long division, matching and cancelling the leading term of the current remainder at every step, continuing until the remainder's degree is less than the divisor's degree.
- The learner can divide by a monic linear divisor (x − c) using synthetic division, correctly identifying c and including zero placeholders for any missing-degree coefficients.
- The learner can accept a non-zero remainder as a normal, expected outcome of division — the Division Algorithm p(x) = d(x)q(x) + r(x) with deg r < deg d always holds, with or without r = 0.
- The learner can connect the Remainder Theorem (p(c) equals the remainder when dividing by x − c) to both methods, using direct evaluation as a fast check or shortcut.

## Core Understanding
Dividing a polynomial p(x) by a divisor d(x) produces a unique quotient q(x) and remainder r(x) satisfying p(x) = d(x)q(x) + r(x), with the single stopping condition that deg r < deg d — this is the polynomial Division Algorithm, and it is the direct structural cousin of integer division (29 = 7·4 + 1, with 1 < 7), not a new law invented for polynomials. Long division executes this by repeatedly asking "what quotient term, multiplied by the leading term of d(x), cancels the leading term of the current remainder?", subtracting the resulting partial product, and repeating on the new (lower-degree) remainder until the stopping condition is met — the procedure never asks about any term other than the leading one at each step, because the leading term is the only one guaranteed not to have already been cancelled by a prior step. Synthetic division is not a different method; it is long division's bookkeeping compressed into a coefficient-only array, valid only when the divisor is monic and linear (x − c), because that specific restriction is what makes every step's "what do I multiply by?" reduce to a single number (c) rather than a full polynomial division. The Remainder Theorem — p(c) equals the remainder of dividing p(x) by (x − c) — is not a separate fact to memorise alongside the division procedure; it falls directly out of the Division Algorithm itself: substituting x = c into p(x) = (x − c)q(x) + r makes the first term vanish, leaving p(c) = r exactly, whenever r is a constant (which it must be, since deg r < deg(x−c) = 1 forces r to be degree 0).

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — polynomial long division mirrors integer long division, step for step.** 29 ÷ 7 = 4 remainder 1 has the identical shape as (x³ − 2x² + x − 3) ÷ (x − 2): repeat "how many times does the divisor go in, subtract, bring down" until the remainder is smaller than the divisor. *Upgrade trigger*: a missing-degree term in the dividend (e.g. no x² term), where "bring down the next digit" has no direct polynomial analogue and a placeholder must be tracked explicitly. *Shelf life*: one to two sessions.
2. **Intermediate — at every step, only the leading term matters.** The quotient term is chosen to cancel the leading term of the *current* remainder, never any other term; everything else in the remainder is provisional and will be dealt with once it becomes the leading term. *Upgrade trigger*: synthetic division, where there is no visible "leading term" step at all — only a compact array of coefficients whose relationship to the leading-term logic is not immediately obvious. *Shelf life*: durable, and it remains the correct mental model for long division permanently; it simply needs a second model layered on top for the synthetic case.
3. **Advanced — synthetic division is long division's bookkeeping, valid only for monic linear divisors.** Every entry in the synthetic array corresponds to a coefficient that long division would also compute, just without writing the variable terms; the restriction to (x − c) is what collapses "divide the leading terms" into "multiply by c." *Upgrade trigger*: needing to explain *why* the remainder theorem works — this model shows the mechanical compression but not yet the algebraic reason p(c) = r.
4. **Expert — the remainder is a fixed point of evaluation, and division, factoring, and root-finding are three views of one relationship.** p(x) = (x − c)q(x) + r makes p(c) = r immediate by substitution; r = 0 iff c is a root iff (x − c) is a factor — division, the remainder theorem, and the factor theorem are not three topics but one algebraic identity examined from three angles. *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the model this concept's own two unlocks (`math.alg.remainder-theorem`, `math.alg.factor-theorem`) exist specifically to formalise.

## Why Students Fail
The single most damaging failure is a false universal rule imported from a narrow diet of practice: because factoring exercises are deliberately constructed to divide evenly, the learner concludes that a non-zero remainder signals a mistake, and either abandons a correct partial computation, restarts unnecessarily, or reports "no answer" when a genuinely valid quotient-and-remainder pair was already in hand. This is not a reasoning error so much as an unstated-assumption error: the general Division Algorithm (remainder permitted) was never explicitly distinguished from the special, evenly-dividing case that happens to dominate early practice sets. The second major failure is procedural and occurs mid-computation: at any step past the first, the learner divides the *last* term of the current remainder by the divisor's leading term, rather than the current remainder's own *leading* term — a false pattern-match to how integer long division's visual layout (digits appearing to align "at the end") is sometimes read, producing a nonsensical rational term where a clean polynomial term was expected. The third failure is scope-related and appears once synthetic division has been learned: its compactness is attractive enough that learners attempt to apply it to any divisor, including non-monic (2x + 3) or higher-degree (x² + 1) cases, because the restriction to monic linear divisors was demonstrated but never stated as an explicit, checkable precondition.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 2 registry (MC-1..MC-3) and its repair actions B01–B03, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — REMAINDER-MUST-BE-ZERO** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation from a practice-set artefact — nearly every division exercise the learner has seen was constructed to come out even, and the general case (nonzero remainder is normal) was never made an explicit, separate fact.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: "I got a remainder of −1, so I must have made a mistake."
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): "29 ÷ 7 — what is the remainder?" A learner who answers 0 for the integer case, or who abandons a genuinely correct nonzero polynomial remainder, confirms MC-1.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — restate the Division Algorithm explicitly, with "unique q(x) and r(x) exist, deg r < deg d" as the actual guarantee, and "r = 0" flagged as one special outcome among many, occurring only when d(x) genuinely divides p(x).
  - **Verification of death**: given (x + 3) ÷ (x + 1), the learner reports a nonzero constant remainder confidently, without re-checking their work for an error.

- **MC-2 — DIVIDE-WRONG-TERM** (foundational)
  - **Birth type**: Type 4, notation-induced — the visual layout of long division (writing terms in a column, subtracting) is misread as "match the last-written term," a false pattern borrowed from how the final step of integer long division sometimes looks, rather than the correct "always the leading term of the current remainder."
  - **Characteristic phrase**: mid-computation, dividing the constant or trailing term of the current remainder by the divisor's leading term instead of the current remainder's own leading term.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): presented with a remainder 4x² − 5x and divisor x − 2, asked which term to divide by the leading term of the divisor — choosing −5x rather than 4x² confirms MC-2.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — restate the rule with the word "leading" doing all the work: divide (leading term of remainder) by (leading term of divisor), every step, no exceptions, then multiply the *entire* divisor by that quotient term before subtracting.
  - **Verification of death**: given a multi-step long division with a missing-degree term forcing a placeholder, the learner correctly identifies the leading term at every step, unprompted.

- **MC-3 — SYNTHETIC-FOR-ANY-DIVISOR** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 5, instruction-induced — synthetic division's compactness is demonstrated on monic linear divisors without its precondition ever being stated as an explicit, checkable rule, so the learner generalises the *method* without generalising its *scope restriction*.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: attempting synthetic division on a non-monic divisor like 2x + 3, or a higher-degree divisor like x² + 1.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): "Can you use synthetic division for the divisor 2x + 1?" — answering yes confirms MC-3.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — state the precondition explicitly and checkably: the divisor must be exactly (x − c), monic and linear; for any other divisor, use long division.
  - **Verification of death**: given a mixed set of divisors (some monic linear, some not), the learner correctly sorts which are synthetic-division-eligible before attempting any computation.

## Analogies
- **Best — integer long division.** 29 ÷ 7 = 4 remainder 1, with the identical stopping rule (remainder < divisor) and the identical "quotient times divisor plus remainder equals dividend" check. This is the Blueprint's own P03 analogy and it is the direct anchor for MC-1's repair, because the learner already accepts that integer division can leave a nonzero remainder without any sense that an error occurred.
- **Alternative — synthetic division as long division's shorthand.** Just as a compact multiplication algorithm omits writing out full place-value expansions while still performing the identical arithmetic underneath, synthetic division omits writing the variable and its powers while performing the identical leading-term cancellation underneath — this targets the bridge from the intermediate to the advanced mental model directly.
- **Story analogy** — a toolkit with a general-purpose tool (long division, works on anything) and a specialised, faster tool that only fits one specific shape of job (synthetic division, only monic linear divisors); reaching for the specialised tool on the wrong job either fails outright or produces nonsense.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "synthetic division is the modern way to divide polynomials."** This framing (division method as an upgrade path rather than as two tools with different applicability) is precisely what licenses MC-3; synthetic division is not more advanced, it is more restricted.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "keep dividing until you reach zero."** This is MC-1 stated as an instruction; the correct stopping rule is "until the remainder's degree is less than the divisor's degree," which is very often, but not always, zero.

## Demonstrations
- **The remainder-is-normal collision.** Divide (x + 3) by (x + 1) live, reaching a nonzero constant remainder, then immediately verify via the Division Algorithm's own check: (x+1)·q(x) + r really does reconstruct (x+3). *Predict, before dividing, whether the remainder will be zero* — most learners will predict zero by default, and the mismatch is the demonstration.
- **The leading-term spotlight.** At each step of a multi-step long division, physically circle or highlight only the leading term of the current remainder before choosing the next quotient term, deliberately ignoring every other term until it becomes the leading one on a later step. *Predict the next quotient term before circling anything* — this operationalises Blueprint B02's repair as a standing visual habit.
- **The eligibility sort.** Present five divisors — (x − 3), (2x + 1), (x² + 4), (x + 5), (3x − 2) — and require the learner to sort them into "synthetic-eligible" and "long-division-only" *before* attempting any actual division. This directly targets MC-3 by separating the scope judgment from the computation itself.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for the long-division procedure itself, since it is a direct structural transplant of integer long division the learner already owns, and re-deriving it from scratch adds no genuine insight over stating the parallel explicitly and then practicing it. What is genuinely discoverable, and should be surfaced as a question, is the Division Algorithm's own guarantee about the remainder — specifically, that a nonzero remainder is not an error: (1) **Need** — "Divide (x + 3) by (x + 1). Is there a whole-number-style quotient with nothing left over?" (2) **Playground** — divide several pairs of polynomials, some evenly, some not, recording the remainder's degree relative to the divisor's degree each time. (3) **Invention** — "What's the rule for when you're allowed to stop dividing?" (4) **Collision** — offer a case the learner initially "corrects" by continuing to divide past the stopping point (e.g. treating a degree-0 remainder against a degree-1 divisor as still divisible), showing the result becomes nonsensical (a rational, non-polynomial term appears). (5) **Formalisation** — stop exactly when deg(remainder) < deg(divisor); the remainder need not be zero. (6) **Compression** — "Keep dividing while the remainder is at least as complex as the divisor. Stop the moment it isn't — zero or not."

## Teaching Sequence
Long division (TA-A01) must be secure, including the leading-term-only judgment that prevents MC-2, before synthetic division (TA-A02) is introduced — synthetic division's compressed notation offers no visible "leading term" to anchor the judgment on, so a learner who has not internalised *why* the leading term is chosen in long division has nothing to fall back on when the compact array produces an unexpected result. MC-1 (remainder-must-be-zero) is addressed first among the three misconceptions, ahead of both procedural misconceptions, because a learner who believes a nonzero remainder signals an error will not trust *any* correctly-executed division that happens to produce one, corrupting the diagnostic signal for every later assessment item. The eligibility contrast (TA-A03, targeting MC-3) is deliberately sequenced after both methods are independently fluent, so the choice between them can be taught as a genuine decision rule ("which tool fits this divisor?") rather than as a hedge introduced before either tool is trusted. The Blueprint's own Component 4 sequence (TA-A01 long division via the integer-division analogy → TA-A02 synthetic division worked-example pair with the Remainder Theorem connection → TA-A03 long-versus-synthetic contrast pair → TA-A04 mastery gate) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Demonstration** — the remainder-is-normal collision, run on the learner's own division result rather than a hypothetical one, so the "this doesn't need fixing" conclusion is felt as a consequence of their own correct work.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — the leading-term spotlight, required as a standing habit before every quotient-term choice, until the leading-term judgment is automatic and no longer needs to be narrated.
- **ORGANIZE: Sorting / Categorisation** — the eligibility sort, this concept's highest-value action for MC-3, because it separates the scope judgment from any computation.
- **TEST-THINKING: Error Analysis** — "a student divided by matching the last terms instead of the leading terms and got a fractional quotient term. What went wrong?" — stronger than direct correction because it requires the learner to locate the specific misapplied rule inside an otherwise plausible-looking attempt.
- **Does NOT fit: teaching synthetic division before long division is fluent.** Synthetic division's compact notation actively hides the leading-term logic that makes long division comprehensible; introducing it first risks the learner memorising an opaque array procedure with no underlying model.
- **Does NOT fit: drilling only evenly-dividing (zero-remainder) problems.** This is the exact practice-set artefact that manufactures MC-1; every problem set from the first session onward must include at least one genuinely nonzero-remainder item.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "match the *leading* term — always the leading term, nothing else." Slow down on "leading." Listen for a learner narrating a step by pointing at the wrong term ("so I take this last number here...") rather than the current remainder's own highest-degree term — the mis-pointed reference is the audible signature of MC-2 about to fire. For the remainder-is-normal case, listen for a rising, uncertain tone on reporting a nonzero remainder ("...and I got negative one? so that's wrong, right?") versus a flat, confident statement of the same result — the uncertain tone signals MC-1 is active even when the arithmetic itself was performed correctly. A confident, unprompted "this divisor isn't monic, so I need long division here" before any computation begins is the strongest positive signal this concept produces. Channel reality is owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals
- **A correct nonzero remainder reported as an error, or division abandoned/restarted unnecessarily** — MC-1; route to the remainder-is-normal collision, never to a re-explanation of the mechanics alone.
- **A fractional or otherwise nonsensical quotient term appearing mid-division** — MC-2, almost always traceable to dividing the wrong term of the current remainder; route to the leading-term spotlight.
- **Synthetic division attempted on a non-monic or higher-degree divisor** — MC-3; route to the eligibility sort before any further computation is attempted.
- **A missing zero-placeholder in a synthetic-division array (e.g. omitting the coefficient for an absent x² term)** — a distinct, narrower procedural gap named directly in the Blueprint's own Teaching Notes; diagnose and repair as a placeholder-discipline lapse, not as MC-2 or MC-3.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A04 gate, MAMR ⌈0.8×5⌉ = 4/5, including its P76 transfer probe (synthetic division with an explicit Remainder Theorem cross-check and full factorisation) — a gate passed without that verification-and-factor step does not certify the connections this concept's two unlocks depend on.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance is "I got a leftover and I don't know what to do with it" or a frozen stare after a nonzero remainder appears. The concept-specific smaller question grounds the moment in already-secured arithmetic: **"29 divided by 7 — what's the remainder there? Is that a mistake?"** — the learner already knows integer division can leave a remainder without anything being wrong, and the return move is simply: "polynomials work exactly the same way." If the freeze is specifically mid-long-division on which term to use next, shrink further: **"Look only at the very first term of what you have left. Ignore everything else for now. What is that term?"** — isolating the leading-term judgment from the full remainder's visual clutter. Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Transfer Connections
- **Near**: `math.alg.remainder-theorem` (the formalisation of p(c) = r this concept already previews via its own worked-example verification step), `math.alg.factor-theorem` (the corollary that r = 0 iff c is a root, directly usable for systematic factoring and root-finding).
- **Far**: integer long division and its remainder (the concept's own primary analogy, not merely decorative — the stopping rule, the "quotient times divisor plus remainder" check, and the general-case-permits-a-remainder fact are structurally identical).
- **Real-world**: any resource-allocation problem phrased as "divide this total into groups of a fixed structure, with some amount left over that doesn't fit a full group" — the remainder-is-normal lesson generalises directly.
- **Expert transfer**: the general algebraic principle that a division algorithm's remainder is a *residual*, bounded by a stopping condition relative to the divisor, not an error signal — the same structural fact underlies polynomial GCD computation, modular arithmetic, and the Euclidean algorithm.

## Cross-Subject Connections
- **Computer science**, genuine and strong: polynomial long division is a named, directly implemented algorithm in symbolic computation and error-correcting code systems (e.g. CRC checksums use polynomial division over a finite field, using this exact leading-term-cancellation logic).
- **Mathematics itself, forward-looking**: this concept's Remainder Theorem connection is the direct on-ramp to root-finding for higher-degree polynomials, which underlies later work in calculus (locating critical points of polynomial functions) and numerical methods.
