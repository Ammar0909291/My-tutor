## Learning Objective
- The learner can systematically apply elimination to a system whose target variable's coefficients are NOT already equal, opposite, or simple multiples of each other, by finding a common multiple and scaling each equation appropriately before adding or subtracting.
- The learner can correctly scale an entire equation — every term, on both sides — recognising that scaling only part of an equation silently produces a different, invalid equation with a different solution set.
- The learner can recognise that "multiply an equation by a constant" and "add a multiple of one equation to another" are precisely the row operations `math.linalg.row-reduction` formalises using matrix notation — the same arithmetic, not a separate or soon-to-be-replaced technique.

## Core Understanding
`math.alg.system-linear-equations` introduced elimination only for the fortunate case where a target variable's coefficients already happen to be equal, opposite, or simple multiples of one another — this concept supplies the genuinely general procedure, needed whenever that convenient coincidence does not hold. Because any two nonzero numbers have a common multiple (their least common multiple, at minimum, always works), elimination is never actually blocked by mismatched coefficients — it simply requires one additional preparatory step: scale each equation by whatever factor makes the target variable's coefficient equal to that common multiple in both, and only then add or subtract to cancel it. The single most consequential rule this scaling step depends on is that multiplying an equation by a constant means multiplying *every* term — each variable's coefficient and the constant on the right-hand side alike — because this is precisely what guarantees the operation preserves the equation's exact solution set; scaling only the term being eliminated (or any other partial subset of terms) silently produces a structurally different equation whose solution set has nothing to do with the original. This concept's deepest content, though, is not the mechanical scaling procedure but the recognition of what it actually *is*: multiplying an equation by a constant and adding a multiple of one equation to another are not two arbitrary algebraic tricks — they are exactly the two row operations that `math.linalg.row-reduction` will later apply to an augmented matrix's rows using compact notation, meaning this concept is the concrete, equation-based version of a technique that gets *systematised*, not replaced, by that later, more general method.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — when the coefficients don't line up, find a number both can become and scale to match.** For 3x+4y=11 and 5x+6y=19, y's coefficients (4 and 6) aren't simple multiples, so scale each equation to make both become 12. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to scale an equation and correctly touching every single term, including the constant — "scale to match" alone doesn't specify which terms are included. *Shelf life*: one session.
2. **Intermediate — scaling an equation means every term, both sides, with no exceptions.** Multiplying 3x+4y=11 by 3 means 9x+12y=33, every term touched identically. *Upgrade trigger*: connecting this concept's own two operations (scale, then add-a-multiple) to the row-operations framework `math.linalg.row-reduction` will name explicitly. *Shelf life*: durable and remains correct permanently.
3. **Advanced — recognise that this concept's own two legal moves are literally the row operations of matrix-based elimination, written with full equations instead of rows and columns.** The same arithmetic, performed identically, whether tracked as x's and y's or as entries in an augmented array. *Upgrade trigger*: a system large enough (many variables) that hand-planning which pairs of equations to combine, and in what order, becomes genuinely error-prone — motivating a systematic, column-by-column procedure instead of ad hoc planning.
4. **Expert — elimination via common multiples is the general-purpose two-equation instance of Gaussian elimination, and every later systematic linear-algebra technique for solving larger systems is this same idea, disciplined and scaled up.** *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the model that makes the transition into matrix methods feel like a notational upgrade to already-known arithmetic rather than an unrelated new subject.

## Why Students Fail
The most foundational failure is believing elimination simply does not apply once coefficients fail to already match or relate by a simple multiple — encountering 3x+4y=11 and 5x+6y=19, where 4 and 6 are not multiples of one another in either direction, the learner concludes there is no way to eliminate y, when in fact any pair of nonzero coefficients has a common multiple and elimination always remains available, provided the preliminary scaling step is performed. The second major failure is procedural and occurs even among learners who correctly attempt the scaling step: multiplying an equation "to eliminate a variable" is read as licence to touch *only* the term being eliminated, leaving the other terms (including, critically, the constant on the right-hand side) unscaled — producing an equation that superficially resembles the correct one but is actually invalid, since it no longer shares the original system's solution set, a fact the learner typically does not discover until a later check fails or is never performed at all. The third failure is not computational but conceptual, and it is specific to this concept's forward-looking content: without the explicit connection ever being drawn, the learner treats elimination-by-scaling as a self-contained, standalone technique, unrelated to the row operations `math.linalg.row-reduction` will introduce later — so that later concept is encountered as if it were entirely new material, rather than as the identical arithmetic the learner already commands, merely wearing different notation.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1..MC-3) and its repair actions B01–B03, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — ELIMINATION-ASSUMED-ONLY-WORKS-FOR-SIMPLE-MULTIPLES** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation — every elimination example the learner met in `math.alg.system-linear-equations` happened to have conveniently related coefficients, and without that convenience being flagged as a special case, the learner absorbs "elimination requires related coefficients" as a general rule rather than "elimination requires ONE preparatory scaling step when coefficients aren't already related."
  - **Characteristic phrase**: facing coefficients like 4 and 6, declaring the system cannot be solved by elimination.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): ask a learner to eliminate a variable whose coefficients aren't simple multiples, and check whether they claim elimination doesn't apply.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — re-walk the common-multiple procedure explicitly, re-anchoring on "any pair of nonzero coefficients has a common multiple — elimination always works, it just may need this extra scaling step first."
  - **Verification of death**: given a system with genuinely unrelated target coefficients, the learner finds a common multiple and proceeds unprompted, without declaring the system unsolvable by elimination.

- **MC-2 — PARTIAL-EQUATION-SCALING** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 4, notation-induced — "scale the equation to eliminate this variable" is read narrowly as an instruction about the target term specifically, rather than as an instruction about the equation as a single, indivisible object every term of which must scale together.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: multiplying only the y-term of 3x+4y=11 by 3, leaving 3x+12y=11 rather than the correct 9x+12y=33.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): ask a learner to scale an equation and check whether they multiply only some terms.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — directly compare the correctly-scaled equation against the partially-scaled one, checking the known genuine solution against the invalid version and showing it fails — re-anchoring on "every term, both sides, every time."
  - **Verification of death**: given a scaling task, the learner scales every term including the constant, unprompted, and can state why omitting any one term would invalidate the equation.

- **MC-3 — ELIMINATION-METHOD-TREATED-AS-UNRELATED-TO-ROW-OPERATIONS** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 5, instruction-induced — the connection between this concept's two legal moves (scale, add-a-multiple) and `math.linalg.row-reduction`'s formally-named row operations is never demonstrated side by side unless deliberately taught, so the two remain separate, unconnected pieces of knowledge in the learner's mind.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: treating elimination-by-scaling and row-reduction's row operations as two different, unrelated techniques to be learned independently.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): ask a learner whether elimination method and row operations are the same technique, and check for a "no."
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — re-walk the identical arithmetic performed with full equations versus with an augmented-matrix row notation, side by side, re-anchoring on "the exact same two operations, just written differently."
  - **Verification of death**: given a system solved by elimination, the learner can translate each scaling and combining step into the equivalent row-operation notation, unprompted.

## Analogies
- **Best — finding a common denominator, but for coefficients instead of fractions.** Just as adding fractions with different denominators requires finding a common denominator first (not a sign that addition "doesn't work"), eliminating a variable with unrelated coefficients requires finding a common multiple first — an extra preparatory step, not a barrier. This directly targets MC-1 by reframing the extra work as familiar rather than exceptional.
- **Alternative — a recipe scaled for a different number of servings, every ingredient at once.** Doubling a recipe means doubling every single ingredient, not just the one you happen to be focused on; scaling an equation works identically. This targets MC-2 directly.
- **Story analogy** — translating the same instructions into two different languages (equation notation and matrix/row notation): the underlying meaning and the actions performed are identical, only the surface vocabulary differs — directly modelling MC-3's repair.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "elimination works when the numbers line up nicely."** This licenses MC-1 by framing coincidental convenience as a precondition rather than a special case of a more general, always-available procedure.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "multiply the part you're eliminating by whatever makes it cancel."** This is precisely MC-2's mechanism stated as an instruction; the correct instruction names the whole equation as the object being scaled, never a term in isolation.

## Demonstrations
- **The common-multiple search, run live.** For 3x+4y=11 and 5x+6y=19, find the LCM of 4 and 6 (12), then determine the scaling factor for each equation to reach it, before any elimination is attempted. *Predict, before searching, whether a common multiple will exist for these two specific coefficients* — every pair of nonzero numbers does, and the demonstration confirms this directly, operationalising Blueprint B01's repair.
- **The partial-scaling failure, attempted live.** Scale only the y-term of an equation, then substitute the system's already-known genuine solution into the resulting (invalid) equation, showing it fails to satisfy it — then repeat with a full, correct scaling, showing the genuine solution now works. *Predict, before substituting, whether the partially-scaled equation will hold* — the failed prediction is the demonstration, operationalising Blueprint B02's repair.
- **The equation-versus-row side-by-side.** Solve one system twice, once with full equations and once with an augmented array performing the identical arithmetic, aligned step for step. This is the Blueprint's own TA-A03 contrast, restaged as a direct simultaneous comparison rather than two sequential worked examples.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for the common-multiple procedure itself, since it is a direct, mechanical extension of already-known least-common-multiple arithmetic, not a new principle requiring discovery. What is genuinely worth surfacing as a question, and is central to this concept's forward-looking content, is the row-operations connection: (1) **Need** — "You just scaled and added two equations to eliminate a variable. What were the two actual operations you performed?" (2) **Playground** — solve the same system twice, once tracking full equations and once tracking only the coefficient rows in an array, performing identical arithmetic each time. (3) **Invention** — "Are these genuinely the same steps, or two different methods that happen to agree?" (4) **Collision** — offer a step from one representation (equations) and ask the learner to produce the exact matching step in the other (rows), testing whether the correspondence is genuinely understood or merely coincidental. (5) **Formalisation** — scaling an equation and adding a multiple of one equation to another are exactly the two row operations of matrix-based elimination, expressed with full symbolic equations instead of array rows. (6) **Compression** — "Same two moves, different notation. You already know row reduction's core arithmetic."

## Teaching Sequence
The common-multiple procedure (TA-A01, targeting MC-1) is taught first, establishing that elimination is always available given the right preparatory step, before the whole-equation scaling discipline (TA-A02, targeting MC-2) is emphasised as a precondition for that procedure's own correctness — a learner who believes elimination is blocked by mismatched coefficients has no motivation to master a scaling discipline they don't yet know they need. The row-operations connection (TA-A03, targeting MC-3) is deliberately sequenced last, after the mechanical procedure is fluent, so the learner has genuine, hands-on experience with the two operations before being asked to recognise them in a new notation — recognising a connection to material not yet practiced would be an empty verbal exercise rather than a genuine insight. The Blueprint's own Component 5 sequence (TA-A01 common-multiple procedure via representation shift, TA-A02 whole-equation scaling via conflict evidence, TA-A03 row-operations contrast pair, TA-A04 mastery gate) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Demonstration** — the common-multiple search, run on the learner's own system rather than a fixed example, so "elimination always has a path forward" is experienced directly.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction / conflict evidence** — the partial-scaling failure, run as a genuine attempted shortcut that visibly fails, per the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence design (TA-A02), rather than presented as an abstract warning.
- **DO: Worked example, the equation-versus-row side-by-side** — run as a direct, simultaneous translation exercise rather than two separately-taught methods, this concept's highest-value action for preventing MC-3.
- **Does NOT fit: introducing the row-operations connection before the scaling procedure itself is fluent.** A learner still uncertain about the mechanics has no capacity to also evaluate whether a new notation describes the same operations.
- **Does NOT fit: presenting only systems whose coefficients already relate simply.** This never gives MC-1 a chance to surface, producing false confidence that elimination is more restrictive than it actually is.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "every term, both sides — the whole equation moves together." Slow down on "whole," every time a scaling step is narrated. Listen for a learner who names only the target variable's term when describing a scaling step ("I'll multiply the y by 3") rather than the full equation — the narrow framing predicts MC-2 reliably, even before any written error appears. For MC-1, listen for whether the learner attempts to find a common multiple as a matter of course upon seeing unrelated coefficients, versus pausing with genuine uncertainty about whether the problem is solvable at all — the uncertainty itself is diagnostic. A confident, unprompted "this is just a row operation" upon completing a scaling-and-adding step is the strongest positive signal for MC-3's repair. Channel reality is owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals
- **A system with unrelated coefficients declared unsolvable by elimination** — MC-1; route to the common-multiple search, never to a bare restatement of "find the LCM."
- **A scaled equation with only the target variable's term (and not the constant, or another variable's term) correctly multiplied** — MC-2, cleanly diagnostic; route to the partial-scaling failure demonstration so the invalidity is felt directly.
- **Elimination and row operations described as unrelated or independently-learned techniques** — MC-3; route to the equation-versus-row side-by-side.
- **A scaling-and-adding step spontaneously translated into row-operation language without being asked** — the strongest positive signal available, indicating MC-3's connection has genuinely landed rather than being recited on cue.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A04 gate, MAMR ⌈0.85×5⌉ = 5/5, including its P76 cross-link transfer probe against `math.linalg.row-reduction`, whose part (c) specifically requires articulating what genuinely NEW idea that later concept adds — a gate passed without that component does not certify the forward connection this concept exists specifically to build.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance is "these numbers don't match up, so I can't eliminate this one" — a precise statement of MC-1 in the learner's own words. The concept-specific smaller question redirects to already-known arithmetic: **"What's the smallest number both 4 and 6 divide into evenly? Now — could you scale each equation to reach that number?"** — using an already-secured LCM skill as the bridge rather than asserting that elimination "still works" as a bare claim. If the freeze is specifically at the scaling step's scope, shrink to a single check: **"You're multiplying this equation by 3. How many terms does this equation have? How many of them need the 3?"** — making the completeness of the scaling operation an explicit, countable question. Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Memory Hooks
- Concept type: **procedure**, extending the already-known elimination technique with a mandatory preparatory step (common-multiple scaling) and a forward-looking conceptual connection (row operations). Review by *application on mixed input* every cycle, including at least one system genuinely requiring a common-multiple search (not already-matched coefficients).
- Concept-specific deviation: keep at least one whole-versus-partial scaling comparison in active rotation even once the mechanics feel fluent — MC-2 is the misconception most likely to resurface silently under time pressure, when a learner reaches for a shortcut that happens to look plausible.
- Interleaving partners: `math.alg.system-linear-equations` (the basic elimination case and three-solution-case framework this concept generalises) and, going forward, `math.linalg.row-reduction` (this concept's own unlock, where the row-operations connection should be reinforced, not merely introduced once).

## Transfer Connections
- **Near**: `math.linalg.row-reduction` (this concept's own unlock — the systematic, matrix-based generalisation of exactly the two operations taught here).
- **Far**: `math.alg.system-3var`'s elimination cascade (the same common-multiple-and-scale logic, applied across three equations rather than two).
- **Real-world**: any situation requiring two quantities to be compared or combined on a common footing before further calculation — unit conversion before addition, or scaling two recipes to a shared batch size before comparing ingredient ratios, share the identical "find a common multiple, then combine" structure.
- **Expert transfer**: the general linear-algebra principle that row operations (scaling and combining) are the fundamental, solution-set-preserving moves underlying all systematic system-solving methods, from hand elimination through Gaussian elimination to computational linear algebra.
