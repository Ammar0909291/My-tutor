## Learning Objective
- The learner can state and apply $S=2\pi\int_a^b f(x)\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx$ for the surface area generated by rotating $y=f(x)$ about the $x$-axis from $x=a$ to $x=b$.
- The learner can explain the formula's two multiplicative components — $2\pi f(x)$ as the circumference of the circle traced at that point, and $\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx$ as the arc-length element already mastered in `math.calc.arc-length` — and recognize the formula as genuinely "circumference times arc length," summed by integration, not an arbitrary combination.
- The learner can compute $f'(x)$ first and substitute it into the square root correctly (squaring it) before setting up the integral.

## Core Understanding
The surface area of revolution formula $S=2\pi\int_a^b f(x)\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx$ is a direct multiplicative combination of two already-understood pieces, not a new independent idea. Picture the curve $y=f(x)$ split into the same infinitesimal straight-line segments used to derive arc length in the prerequisite concept: each segment has length $ds=\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx$. When that tiny segment is rotated about the $x$-axis, it sweeps out a thin circular BAND (like a narrow ring cut from a cone), whose lateral surface area is (to first order) its circumference times its own slant length — circumference $2\pi f(x)$ (since $f(x)$ is the radius at that point) times the segment's arc length $ds$. Summing all these thin bands via integration gives the total surface area. This is the SAME Riemann-sum-as-a-limit idea `math.calc.arc-length` and `math.calc.definite-integral` already established, now applied to a third geometric quantity (surface area, after length and after — in `math.calc.volume-revolution` — volume). Computing an actual surface area requires finding $f'(x)$ FIRST, squaring it correctly, and substituting into the square root BEFORE integrating; the square-root expression cannot be simplified or evaluated without this derivative step done carefully.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — "surface area of revolution is a new formula: 2π times the integral of f(x) times a square root thing."** The formula is applied by pattern-matching to a template, with the square-root factor treated as a fixed piece of boilerplate rather than something meaningful. *Upgrade trigger*: being asked why the square-root factor is there at all, or what would happen if it were dropped.
2. **Intermediate — "it's circumference (2πf(x)) times arc length (the square-root factor), added up by integrating."** The two factors are now named and connected to their geometric roles. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to actually compute a surface area, where finding and correctly squaring $f'(x)$ becomes the practical bottleneck.
3. **Advanced — "each thin rotated band's lateral area is its own circumference times its own slant length, exactly like unrolling a truncated cone; the arc-length factor IS that slant length, already derived in the prerequisite concept."** The formula's shape is now justified from first principles, connected explicitly to the arc-length derivation rather than merely paired with it. *Upgrade trigger*: encountering the volume-of-revolution formula, which multiplies by area ($\pi f(x)^2$, not $2\pi f(x)$) rather than circumference, and needing to keep the two constructions structurally distinct.
4. **Expert — surface area of revolution is one member of a family of "sweep a measured piece of a curve around an axis and multiply by the appropriate sweep-length" constructions, alongside volume of revolution (sweeps area) and general surfaces of revolution in higher dimensions.** The learner recognizes the construction pattern itself as reusable, not merely this one instance of it. *Shelf life*: permanent.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant failure is treating this as a variant of the volume-of-revolution setup already familiar from that adjacent technique: since both formulas involve $2\pi$ or $\pi$ times an integral of a function of $f(x)$, a learner overgeneralizes the pattern "revolution problems integrate some multiple of $f(x)$" and drops the arc-length factor entirely, integrating $2\pi\int f(x)\,dx$ alone — which computes something else altogether (closer to a volume-style quantity), not merely an imprecise surface area (MC-1, ARC-LENGTH-FACTOR-OMITTED-FROM-SURFACE-AREA-FORMULA). This is an overgeneralization from a nearby, superficially similar formula family, the same overextension-of-a-recently-learned-pattern shape documented for `math.calc.u-substitution`'s own MC-1 (extending a valid pattern past where its conditions hold) — here the "revolution formulas are just $c\int(\text{function of }f(x))\,dx$" pattern is extended past the point where it drops an essential factor. A second, distinct failure is a computational slip in the derivative step itself: because $f'(x)$ must be squared inside the square root, a learner who is not deliberate about this step may substitute $f'(x)$ into the square root without squaring it first, or square it incorrectly, producing an entirely different (and wrong) integrand (MC-2, DERIVATIVE-MISHANDLED-OR-NOT-SQUARED-CORRECTLY-IN-SETUP) — a straightforward procedural-execution error rather than a conceptual gap, the same class of slip already documented for `math.calc.derivative-rules`' own MC-2 (a coefficient or step dropped mid-procedure under the pressure of a multi-step computation).

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1, MC-2) and its own repair actions B01–B02. **The Blueprint's Misconception Registry carries a Severity column (Foundational, Moderate) but no explicit birth-type column**; both classifications below are independently assigned here.

- **MC-1 — ARC-LENGTH-FACTOR-OMITTED-FROM-SURFACE-AREA-FORMULA** (the Blueprint's own "Foundational" misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization. The volume-of-revolution formula family (integrating some multiple of $f(x)$ or $f(x)^2$ alone) is a superficially similar, more recently practiced pattern; a learner extends "revolution problems integrate a multiple of $f(x)$" past the point where surface area genuinely requires the additional arc-length factor.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: setting up $S=2\pi\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ (omitting the square-root factor entirely), or answering "no" when asked whether the arc-length factor is essential to the formula.
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's A01 hook): present the correct full formula alongside the incorrect simplified attempt that omits the square-root factor, and ask which one is correct.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — re-derive the formula's "circumference times arc length" origin from the rotated-band construction, connecting to the cylinder sanity check (Example 2) where the arc-length factor correctly reduces to 1 for a straight horizontal profile.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh function, the learner writes $\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}$ as part of the integrand without prompting, and can state, if asked, that omitting it produces a different geometric quantity, not merely an approximation.

- **MC-2 — DERIVATIVE-MISHANDLED-OR-NOT-SQUARED-CORRECTLY-IN-SETUP** (the Blueprint's own "Moderate" severity misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization/computational-slip — the same class of mid-procedure execution error already documented for `math.calc.derivative-rules`' own MC-2, here specific to skipping or misapplying the squaring step when substituting $f'(x)$ into the square root.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: writing $\sqrt{1+f'(x)}$ or $\sqrt{1+|f'(x)|}$ instead of $\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}$, or squaring $f'(x)$ incorrectly (e.g. $f'(x)=3x^2$ becoming $[f'(x)]^2=3x^4$ instead of $9x^4$).
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's A03 hook): present Example 3's full derivative-and-squaring step and ask the learner to verify each piece independently before it is revealed.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — re-compute $f'(x)$ step by step, then explicitly square it as a SEPARATE, visible step before substituting into the square root.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh function, the learner computes $f'(x)$, writes $[f'(x)]^2$ as an explicit intermediate step, and only then substitutes into $1+[f'(x)]^2$ — without skipping the intermediate squaring step even when working quickly.

## Analogies
- **Best — unrolling a lampshade (a frustum) into a flat fan shape.** A truncated cone's lateral surface, when unrolled flat, has an area computable as (average circumference) times (slant length) — exactly the circumference-times-arc-length structure this formula sums over infinitesimally thin bands.
- **Alternative — wrapping a label around a bottle whose radius changes along its height.** The amount of label material needed at each height is proportional to the circumference there times how much vertical (and slightly slanted) height that strip of label covers — the arc-length factor accounts for the "slanted," not purely vertical, coverage.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "surface area of revolution is just volume of revolution, but with $2\pi f(x)$ instead of $\pi f(x)^2$."** This phrasing licenses MC-1 directly, implying the only difference between the two formulas is which multiple of $f(x)$ is used, when the actual structural difference is the presence of an entirely separate factor (the arc-length term) that volume of revolution does not need at all. Say "surface area needs an extra factor — the arc-length of the curve being swept — that volume does not" instead.

## Demonstrations
- **The omitted-factor contrast.** For $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ on $[1,4]$, set up $2\pi\int_1^4\sqrt{x}\,dx$ (the incorrect, factor-omitted attempt) beside $2\pi\int_1^4\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{4x}}\,dx$ (the correct formula). *Predict whether the two setups will give the same value before evaluating either.* Getting two visibly different expressions is the demonstration for MC-1.
- **The cylinder sanity check.** For $f(x)=r$ (a constant) from $x=0$ to $x=h$, compute $f'(x)=0$, so $\sqrt{1+0}=1$, giving $S=2\pi\int_0^h r\cdot 1\,dx=2\pi rh$ — the familiar cylinder lateral surface area. *Predict what the arc-length factor should equal for a perfectly straight, horizontal profile before computing it.* Getting exactly 1 (no extra length contribution from a flat profile) confirms the "circumference times arc length" reading directly.
- **The squaring-step isolation.** For $f(x)=x^3$, compute $f'(x)=3x^2$ as one visible step, then $[f'(x)]^2=9x^4$ as a SEPARATE visible step, before substituting into $\sqrt{1+9x^4}$. *Predict $[f'(x)]^2$ before squaring it, checking whether the answer is $3x^4$ (dropping the square) or $9x^4$ (correct) matters.* Catching the $3x^4$ slip live is the demonstration for MC-2.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction is the argued call for the rotated-band derivation itself (the circumference-times-slant-length construction benefits from an explicit worked walkthrough rather than independent rediscovery at this level), but the omitted-factor contrast (MC-1) and the cylinder sanity check are both genuinely discoverable by direct comparison.
1. **Need** — "For $f(x)=\sqrt{x}$ on $[1,4]$, compute $2\pi\int_1^4\sqrt{x}\,dx$. Now compute $2\pi\int_1^4\sqrt{x}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{4x}}\,dx$. Are they the same?" They are not.
2. **Playground** — try the constant-function case $f(x)=r$ and see what the arc-length factor becomes.
3. **Invention** — "Why does a perfectly flat (constant) profile give an arc-length factor of exactly 1, while a steep profile gives a factor much bigger than 1?" Let the learner connect it to how much extra slanted distance a steep profile covers compared to a flat one.
4. **Collision** — confront a learner who proposed omitting the square-root factor with the cylinder check: if their simplified formula were correct, would it still reduce to $2\pi rh$ for a cylinder?
5. **Formalisation** — state the full formula explicitly, $S=2\pi\int_a^b f(x)\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx$, naming both factors by their geometric role.
6. **Compression** — "Circumference times arc length, integrated — never circumference alone."

## Teaching Sequence
The circumference-times-arc-length understanding (MC-1) must be established FIRST via the rotated-band construction, since the cylinder sanity check and the squaring-step care (MC-2) both depend on the learner already seeing the formula as a genuine product of two distinct geometric factors — a learner who has not made this connection has no framework for noticing when a squaring slip changes the answer, since they are not yet tracking the arc-length factor as a meaningful quantity in its own right. The cylinder sanity check follows directly, per the Blueprint's own A02, confirming the formula reduces correctly on the simplest possible case. The squaring-step isolation (MC-2) is introduced LAST, per the Blueprint's own A03, once the formula's structure is fluent and the learner's attention can be devoted entirely to executing the derivative-and-squaring step carefully. Turn-level scripts for A01–A04 are owned by the Blueprint's Component 5 and are not restated here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Worked Example** — the omitted-factor contrast (the incorrect simplified setup beside the correct full formula), with the learner predicting whether the two will agree BEFORE either is evaluated. First action; anchors the two-factor structure concretely.
- **DO: Demonstration** — the cylinder sanity check, run with the learner predicting the arc-length factor's value for a flat profile before it is computed.
- **TEST-THINKING: Error Analysis** — "A student computed the surface area of $f(x)=x^3$ from $x=0$ to $1$ using $\sqrt{1+3x^2}$ as the integrand. What's wrong?" targets MC-2 directly, isolating the squaring slip.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "Will integrating just $2\pi f(x)$ give the same value as the full formula?" asked BEFORE computing either, using the lampshade analogy as the point of contrast. Surfaces MC-1 in one turn.
- **Does NOT fit: general surfaces of revolution about the $y$-axis, revolution about an arbitrary axis, or the full differential-geometry surface-area machinery for parametric or implicit surfaces, here.** This concept covers rotation about the $x$-axis of an explicit function $y=f(x)$ only; the general cases belong to a more advanced treatment not yet present in the KG.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "circumference times arc length, integrated — the arc-length factor is not optional, it's what makes this a SURFACE area instead of some other quantity." Say it every time a new surface-area problem is set up, not just the first. Listen for a learner who sets up $2\pi\int f(x)\,dx$ as their proposed integral — that specific omission is the tell for MC-1. Listen for a learner who computes $f'(x)$ correctly but then writes the square root with $f'(x)$ un-squared, or squared incorrectly — that specific slip is the tell for MC-2, and is best caught by asking the learner to say the squaring step out loud as a separate sentence rather than folding it silently into the substitution. ## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance here is "why do I need the square root part at all, why can't I just integrate the function?" — a reasonable question given how naturally the volume-of-revolution formula (which the learner likely met just before this concept) seems to suggest "revolution formulas just integrate the function." The concept-specific smaller question returns to the flat-profile case: **"If the curve were perfectly flat and horizontal — no slant at all — what should the arc-length factor equal?"** The learner reasons that a flat, unslanted profile adds no extra length, so the factor should be exactly 1 — and checking $f'(x)=0$ gives $\sqrt{1+0}=1$, confirming their own reasoning. Then return: "that's exactly what the formula does — when there's no slant, the extra factor vanishes to 1 and you're just left with circumference times length, like a cylinder. When there IS a slant, the factor grows to account for the extra distance the surface actually covers." If the frustration is instead about a computational slip in squaring $f'(x)$, shrink to the bare check: **"Say your derivative out loud. Now say what that same expression squared looks like, out loud, as its own separate sentence."** Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.
