## Learning Objective

The learner can: state that subitizing is the perceptual ability to
instantly recognize the cardinality of a small set (≤4 items) WITHOUT
counting each element individually; correctly distinguish subitizing
(instant, pattern-based recognition) from rapid counting (still
sequential, just fast); and correctly identify that subitizing's
reliable range is small (typically ≤4), so sets of 5 or more require
either counting or a decomposition strategy (e.g., recognizing 6 as
"4 and 2" via two quick subitized sub-groups) rather than a single
instant read.

## Core Understanding

Subitizing is the perceptual — not procedural — ability to instantly
"see" how many objects are in a small set without stepping through the
counting sequence (`math.arith.counting`) at all. Cognitive science
distinguishes two forms: PERCEPTUAL subitizing (instant recognition of
1–4 items, present even in infants and some animals, with no learned
counting process involved) and CONCEPTUAL subitizing (recognizing a
larger quantity, like 6, by instantly perceiving it as a combination
of smaller subitized groups, e.g. two groups of 3 arranged as a
domino-style pattern). The key boundary is that PERCEPTUAL subitizing's
reliable range tops out around 4 items — beyond that, the visual
system can no longer instantly resolve an exact count without either
grouping (conceptual subitizing) or falling back to sequential
counting.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "subitizing means counting really fast"**: the
  learner believes recognizing "3 dots" instantly is just an
  accelerated version of counting 1, 2, 3 — not a qualitatively
  different, non-sequential perceptual process. Shelf-life warning:
  this model conflates a genuinely instant, non-verbal perceptual act
  with a compressed but still-sequential verbal count, which breaks
  down the moment the learner is asked to subitize an unfamiliar
  arrangement (not a memorized dice pattern) and reverts to counting
  under the "fast counting" model.
- **Intermediate model — "subitizing is instant recognition, but only
  works for very small, familiar patterns (like dice or dominoes)"**:
  the learner correctly identifies subitizing as non-sequential but
  believes it is tied to a specific memorized set of canonical
  arrangements, rather than a general perceptual capacity that works
  (within its range) for any small-set arrangement. Upgrade trigger:
  successfully subitizing 4 randomly-scattered (non-dice-pattern) dots.
- **Advanced model — "perceptual subitizing reliably covers ≤4 items in
  ANY arrangement; larger quantities can be conceptually subitized by
  instantly perceiving them as a COMBINATION of smaller subitized
  groups, not by extending the perceptual range itself"**: the learner
  explains why 7 dots arranged as "4 + 3" can be read almost instantly
  via two subitized sub-groups plus a fast addition, while 7
  scattered dots with no visible grouping cannot be subitized at all
  and require counting. Upgrade trigger: being asked to explain why
  professional card players and referees "instantly" read larger
  counts (via trained conceptual grouping, not an expanded perceptual
  range).
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner who still equates subitizing with
  fast counting (beginner model) should not be pushed toward conceptual
  subitizing's grouping strategy before the core beginner
  misconception (MC-3 below) is resolved — the grouping strategy
  presupposes genuine perceptual subitizing of the sub-groups first.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant, FOUNDATIONAL failure believes subitizing works reliably
for any set size, expecting to instantly "see" quantities of 6, 7, or 8
items the same way they instantly see 3 — not recognizing that
perceptual subitizing has a hard, well-documented range limit around 4
items, beyond which an instant, exact perceptual read is not possible
without grouping. A second failure believes subitizing only applies to
specific memorized arrangements (dice faces, domino patterns), missing
that the underlying perceptual capacity works for genuinely novel,
scattered arrangements within the ≤4 range. A third failure conflates
subitizing with fast counting — believing the two are the same process
at different speeds, rather than recognizing subitizing as a
qualitatively different, non-sequential, non-verbal perceptual act.

## Misconceptions

Authored directly via the birth-taxonomy diagnostic procedure — no
Blueprint exists for this concept:

### MC-1: SUBITIZING-RANGE-UNBOUNDED (Foundational; Type 2 — perceptual intuition)
**Description**: Learner expects to instantly recognize the exact
count of any small-to-medium set (e.g., 6 or 7 scattered items) the
same way they instantly recognize 3, not realizing perceptual
subitizing's reliable range tops out around 4.
**Trigger condition**: shown 6-7 scattered (non-grouped) objects for a
brief instant and asked for the exact count without counting.
**Repair target**: explicit range demonstration — contrast instantly
and correctly subitizing 3-4 items against reliably failing to
instantly subitize 7 scattered items (near-universal failure at that
range confirms the boundary experientially, not just as an asserted
rule).

### MC-2: SUBITIZING-REQUIRES-CANONICAL-PATTERN (Moderate; Type 1 — overgeneralization)
**Description**: Learner believes subitizing only works for familiar,
memorized arrangements (dice-face patterns, domino layouts), having
generalized from early exposure to those specific patterns to a belief
that subitizing IS pattern memorization rather than a general
perceptual capacity.
**Trigger condition**: shown 3-4 objects in a genuinely novel, scattered
(non-dice) arrangement and asked to subitize.
**Repair target**: demonstrate successful instant recognition of
several different, non-canonical arrangements of the same small
quantity (e.g., 4 dots scattered randomly vs. 4 dots in a square vs. 4
dots in a line), showing the capacity transfers across arrangements
within the ≤4 range.

### MC-3: SUBITIZING-EQUALS-FAST-COUNTING (Moderate; Type 3 — language contamination)
**Description**: The everyday phrase "instantly" gets reinterpreted as
"very quickly counting" rather than "without counting at all" — the
word "instant" contaminates the concept with an implied (compressed)
sequential process, when genuine perceptual subitizing involves no
sequential step-through at all.
**Trigger condition**: asked to explain HOW they know a set of 3 dots
has 3 items, learner describes an internal (even if fast) counting
process ("I counted really quick") rather than a single perceptual
read.
**Repair target**: contrast subitizing's ALL-AT-ONCE perceptual read
(no sequential component, verifiable by testing recognition speed
against set size — subitizing time stays roughly flat for 1-4 items,
while counting time scales with each additional item) against genuine
fast counting (time still scales, just compressed).

## Analogies

**Primary — instantly recognizing a friend's face vs. checking an
ID card feature-by-feature**: recognizing a close friend's face in a
crowd is instant — you don't sequentially check "nose, then eyes, then
chin" before concluding "that's my friend." Checking a stranger's ID
card, by contrast, requires sequentially comparing features one by
one. Subitizing a small set of dots is like the face — one instant,
holistic perceptual read; counting a larger set is like the ID
check — a genuine step-by-step process.

**Anti-analogy to retire**: "Subitizing is just counting really fast in
your head." This directly invites MC-3 by suggesting the process is
still sequential, merely compressed in time, rather than a
qualitatively different, non-sequential perceptual act.

## Demonstrations

**Range-boundary contrast (targets MC-1)**: briefly flash 3 dots, then
4 dots, then 7 dots (each for under one second) and ask for the exact
count each time. Learners near-universally succeed instantly at 3 and
4, and near-universally fail (or must guess/estimate) at 7 without a
longer look or an explicit counting pass — directly demonstrating the
range boundary through firsthand failure, not just an asserted rule.

**Arrangement-generalization contrast (targets MC-2)**: subitize 4 dots
in a dice-five-minus-center pattern, then 4 dots scattered randomly,
then 4 dots in a straight line — success across all three arrangements
demonstrates the capacity is general, not tied to one memorized layout.

**Timing contrast (targets MC-3)**: time recognition of 2 dots vs. 4
dots (both should feel roughly instant, flat response time) against
recognition of 6 vs. 9 dots via explicit counting (response time
visibly scales with the extra items) — the flat-vs-scaling timing
pattern is the empirical signature distinguishing subitizing from
counting.

## Discovery Questions

Briefly flash sets of 2, 3, 4, and then 7 scattered dots (each under
one second) and ask the learner to report the count each time without
counting aloud — the learner discovers firsthand that recognition
feels effortless and instant up to 4, then suddenly requires guessing
or a second look at 7. Recommendation: guided discovery for the range
boundary (directly experiential from the flash-and-report exercise);
direct instruction for the perceptual-vs-sequential distinction (MC-3's
repair), since the internal-process distinction is not independently
observable without an explicit timing contrast.

## Teaching Sequence

MC-3 (subitizing equals fast counting) is addressed first, since
correctly understanding subitizing as a genuinely non-sequential
perceptual act is the conceptual foundation the range boundary (MC-1)
and arrangement-generality (MC-2) both depend on. MC-1 (range
unbounded) is addressed second, via the flash-and-report demonstration.
MC-2 (canonical-pattern dependence) is addressed last, since it
concerns a narrower, less consequential over-restriction of an
already-correctly-understood capacity.

## Voice Teaching Notes

**Register**: Say "recognize instantly, without counting" rather than
"count fast" — the phrase "without counting" is load-bearing and
directly guards against MC-3.

**Wait-time**: After a flash-and-report trial at 7 items, give brief
wait-time before revealing that near-universal failure at that range
is expected, not a personal shortcoming — this is a perceptual
boundary, not a skill deficit.

**Load-bearing sentences**:
- "Subitizing means seeing the count all at once — no counting step at
  all, not even a fast one."
- "The instant range tops out around four — past that, you're either
  grouping smaller subitized chunks or counting."

**Voice channel note**: See `foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals

### Gate 1 (MC-3 check)
Ask the learner to explain, in their own words, how they know a
flashed set of 3 dots has 3 items. Pass: describes an instant,
all-at-once perceptual read, not a (even fast) sequential count.

### Gate 2 (MC-1 check)
Flash 4 scattered dots (pass expected: instant, correct count) then
flash 7 scattered dots for the same brief duration (pass expected:
learner reports inability to instantly determine the exact count
without a longer look or explicit counting).

### Gate 3 (MC-2 check)
Successfully subitize the same quantity (4) presented in three
different, non-canonical (non-dice) arrangements.

### Gate 4 (range statement)
State, in one sentence, the approximate reliable range of perceptual
subitizing (≤4) and what strategy applies beyond it (conceptual
subitizing via grouping, or counting).

### Transfer probe (independence mode — no cross_links)
A card dealer appears to "instantly" know a hand has 8 cards without
visibly counting one by one. Explain how this is possible given
subitizing's ≤4 perceptual range. Pass: correctly identifies this as
conceptual subitizing — instantly perceiving the hand as a combination
of smaller subitized (or trained/memorized) groups, e.g. two groups of
4, rather than an expanded perceptual range.

**Mastery criterion**: correct performance on all 4 gates plus the
transfer probe, consistent with KG mastery_threshold 0.9.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Likeliest utterance: "I just knew it was 3, I didn't count — but for
the bigger group I had to actually count them, why?" — the
concept-specific smaller question: "did the second group feel like one
instant glance, or did you have to look at parts of it one at a time?"
directly surfaces the learner's own firsthand experience of the range
boundary, converting an unexplained personal observation into the
concept's core distinction (instant perceptual read vs. sequential
counting) using evidence the learner already generated themselves.

## Memory Hooks

**Type**: perceptual/procedural (the flash-and-report exercise trains
and verifies the instant-recognition skill directly) + declarative (the
≤4 range boundary and the perceptual-vs-sequential distinction). Review
form: brief periodic flash-and-report drills at varying set sizes (2,
3, 4, and an occasional 5-7 to re-confirm the boundary holds).
Interleaving partner: `math.arith.counting` (the sequential process
subitizing is explicitly contrasted against).

## Transfer Connections

**Near transfer**:
- `math.arith.counting` (sibling relationship — subitizing supplements
  counting for very small sets and motivates WHY counting is needed at
  all beyond the perceptual range)

**Far transfer**:
- Early arithmetic fluency: instantly recognizing small addends (e.g.
  "3 and 2 more") without recounting from 1 depends on subitizing the
  sub-groups
- Estimation and quick-check skills in later mathematics (recognizing
  "about 5" at a glance before a precise count, as a plausibility
  check on a computed answer)
