## Learning Objective
Verify whether a subset $I$ of a ring $R$ is an IDEAL by checking three conditions — $(I,+)$ is an
additive subgroup of $(R,+)$, LEFT absorption ($rI\subseteq I$ for all $r\in R$), and RIGHT
absorption ($Ir\subseteq I$ for all $r\in R$, coinciding with left absorption in commutative
rings) — correctly distinguishing a TWO-SIDED ideal from a one-sided one; construct PRINCIPAL
ideals $\langle a\rangle=aR=\{ra:r\in R\}$ (reusing `math.abst.ring-theory`'s own ring structure
directly); and prove that the KERNEL of any ring homomorphism is always a two-sided ideal.

## Core Understanding
An ideal $I$ of a ring $R$ is a non-empty subset satisfying TWO layers of structure, both reusing
`math.abst.ring-theory`'s own definitions directly. First, $(I,+)$ must be an additive SUBGROUP of
$(R,+)$ — non-empty, closed under $+$, closed under additive inverses (the SAME subgroup criterion
already familiar from `math.abst.subgroup`, applied here to the ring's additive structure). Second,
$I$ must be closed under ABSORPTION by every element of $R$, not merely by elements of $I$ itself:
LEFT absorption means $r\cdot i\in I$ for every $r\in R$ and every $i\in I$ (written $rI\subseteq
I$); RIGHT absorption means $i\cdot r\in I$ for every $r\in R$ and every $i\in I$ (written
$Ir\subseteq I$). A TWO-SIDED ideal satisfies BOTH. In a COMMUTATIVE ring, left and right
absorption automatically coincide (since $ri=ir$ for all elements), so the distinction collapses
— but in a NON-COMMUTATIVE ring, the two conditions are genuinely independent, and a subset can
satisfy one without the other.

This absorption requirement is what makes an ideal STRICTLY STRONGER than a SUBRING: a subring only
needs closure under multiplication BY ITS OWN ELEMENTS ($i\cdot j\in I$ for $i,j\in I$); an ideal
needs absorption by EVERY element of the AMBIENT ring $R$, including elements entirely outside $I$.
Every ideal happens to be a subring (absorption by $I$'s own elements is a special case of
absorption by all of $R$), but not every subring is an ideal.

A PRINCIPAL ideal $\langle a\rangle$, generated by a single element $a\in R$, is the SMALLEST ideal
containing $a$. In a COMMUTATIVE ring, $\langle a\rangle=aR=\{ra:r\in R\}$ — reusing $R$'s own
multiplication directly to sweep out every multiple of $a$. In $\mathbb Z$, $\langle n\rangle
=n\mathbb Z$, exactly matching the familiar subgroups of $(\mathbb Z,+)$ under multiples of $n$.

The QUOTIENT RING $R/I$ is well-defined EXACTLY WHEN $I$ is a two-sided ideal — this is the
concept's central payoff, previewing `math.abst.quotient-ring` directly: one-sided absorption alone
is not enough to make the coset multiplication $(a+I)(b+I)=ab+I$ well-defined.

Finally, the KERNEL THEOREM: for ANY ring homomorphism $\varphi:R\to S$, $\ker(\varphi)=\{r\in
R:\varphi(r)=0\}$ is ALWAYS a two-sided ideal of $R$ — never merely a subring, regardless of
whether $R$ itself is commutative. Proof: for $k\in\ker(\varphi)$ and any $r\in R$,
$\varphi(rk)=\varphi(r)\varphi(k)=\varphi(r)\cdot0=0$ (left absorption) and
$\varphi(kr)=\varphi(k)\varphi(r)=0\cdot\varphi(r)=0$ (right absorption) — both hold unconditionally,
because $0$ absorbs under multiplication on EITHER side in $S$.

## Mental Models
- **"An ideal needs absorption by the WHOLE ring, not just by its own elements — that's what makes
  it stronger than a subring."**
- **"Left absorption ($rI\subseteq I$) and right absorption ($Ir\subseteq I$) are independent
  checks in a non-commutative ring — only both together make it two-sided."**
- **"The principal ideal $\langle a\rangle=aR$ sweeps out every ring-element multiple of $a$, not
  just integer multiples."**
- **"A kernel is ALWAYS a two-sided ideal — automatic from the homomorphism property, on both
  sides, every time."**

## Why Students Fail

### MC-1: IDEAL-MISSING-ABSORPTION
- **Surface form**: verifies $(I,+)$ is an additive subgroup and confirms $I$ is closed under
  multiplication BY ITS OWN ELEMENTS, but never checks $ri\in I$ for $r\in R\setminus I$ — treats
  the ideal criterion as identical to the subring criterion.
- **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization (Blueprint's own declared FOUNDATIONAL severity, here
  attributed to the ideal criterion's surface resemblance to the already-mastered subgroup/subring
  criterion — both require additive-subgroup structure plus a closure condition, and the closure
  condition's SCOPE — own elements only, versus absorption by the whole ring — is the genuinely new
  and easily-missed part). Since `math.abst.subgroup`'s own criterion only ever required closure
  WITHIN $H$, that narrower pattern is carried forward unchanged into the ideal setting, where the
  criterion is actually broader.
- **Repair**: re-verify absorption explicitly for an element $r\in R\setminus I$ (not merely
  $r\in I$) in the specific candidate subset in question.

### MC-2: ONE-SIDED-ABSORPTION-SUFFICIENT
- **Surface form**: in a non-commutative ring, verifies only LEFT absorption ($rI\subseteq I$) and
  declares the subset a two-sided ideal without separately checking RIGHT absorption.
- **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization (Blueprint's own declared severity for this
  misconception's trigger pattern, here attributed to commutative-ring habit transfer: in every
  commutative ring the student has worked with so far — including $\mathbb Z$, $\mathbb Q$, and
  every $R[x]$-style example from earlier concepts — left and right absorption are automatically
  the same fact, so checking one has always sufficed, and that shortcut is carried forward
  unmodified into a setting, non-commutative rings, where it genuinely fails). Since the two
  conditions coincide in every commutative example previously encountered, no prior evidence has
  ever forced the two checks to be treated as separate.
- **Repair**: re-verify BOTH left and right absorption independently and explicitly for the
  specific non-commutative candidate subset in question, using a genuine non-commutative example
  (matrix ring) where the two conditions can diverge.

### MC-3: PRINCIPAL-IDEAL-IS-CYCLIC-SUBGROUP
- **Surface form**: generates the principal ideal $\langle a\rangle$ as $\{na:n\in\mathbb Z\}$
  (integer multiples only, the cyclic-SUBGROUP-generation formula), rather than $\{ra:r\in R\}$
  (multiplication by every RING element).
- **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization (Blueprint's own declared severity, here attributed
  to the two formulas coinciding EXACTLY in $\mathbb Z$ itself — where "multiply by an integer" and
  "multiply by a ring element" are the same operation — so the integer-multiple formula, learned
  first and validated repeatedly there, is carried forward unmodified into rings like $\mathbb
  R[x]$, where ring-element multiplication is a strictly richer operation than integer scaling).
  The coincidence in $\mathbb Z$ provides no evidence to distinguish the two formulas until a
  genuinely different ring is tried.
- **Repair**: re-generate the specific principal ideal in question using $\{ra:r\in R\}$ explicitly,
  in a ring where this differs from integer multiples of $a$ (e.g. $\langle x^2\rangle$ in
  $\mathbb R[x]$, which contains $x^3=x\cdot x^2$, not merely integer multiples of $x^2$).

## Analogies
- **"A subring only needs to stay closed among its own members — like a club whose members only
  ever interact with each other. An ideal must ABSORB contact from EVERYONE outside the club too:
  multiply any outsider by any club member, and the result must land back in the club."**
- **Anti-analogy**: an ideal is NOT simply "a subring that happens to be an ideal too" — absorption
  by the whole ring is a strictly STRONGER, additional requirement, not an automatic consequence of
  being a subring.

## Demonstrations
- **Demonstration 1 (targets MC-1)**: $n\mathbb Z$ is an ideal of $\mathbb Z$ — full three-step
  proof: $(n\mathbb Z,+)$ is an additive subgroup (non-empty via $0$, closed under $+$ since
  $nk_1+nk_2=n(k_1+k_2)$, closed under inverses since $-(nk)=n(-k)$); absorption:
  $m\cdot(nk)=n(mk)\in n\mathbb Z$ for ANY $m\in\mathbb Z$ (not just $m\in n\mathbb Z$) — the
  absorption check explicitly uses an arbitrary ring element $m$, not merely an element of the
  candidate ideal itself.
- **Demonstration 2 (targets MC-2)**: in the non-commutative ring $M_2(\mathbb R)$, let
  $L=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&0\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb R\right\}$. For any matrix
  $M\in M_2(\mathbb R)$, $ML\in L$ (LEFT absorption holds — verified by direct matrix
  multiplication), but $LM\notin L$ in general (RIGHT absorption FAILS) — $L$ is genuinely a LEFT
  ideal but NOT a right ideal, hence not two-sided; this is the exact transfer-probe example the
  Blueprint uses, worked concretely here.
- **Demonstration 3 (targets MC-3)**: $\langle x^2\rangle$ in $\mathbb R[x]$ contains $x^3=x\cdot
  x^2$ (using the ring element $x$, not an integer) — a genuine element of the principal ideal that
  the wrong "integer multiples only" formula $\{n\cdot x^2:n\in\mathbb Z\}$ would never produce,
  since $x^3$ is not an integer multiple of $x^2$.

## Discovery Questions
1. "Is it enough to check that a subset is closed under multiplication by its OWN elements, or must
   you check absorption by the WHOLE ring?"
2. "In a non-commutative ring, does checking left absorption automatically confirm right absorption
   too?"
3. "Does the principal ideal $\langle a\rangle$ consist of INTEGER multiples of $a$, or RING-ELEMENT
   multiples of $a$?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. **Anchor**: connect to `math.abst.ring-theory`'s own ring definition and to `math.abst.subgroup`'s
   already-mastered additive-subgroup criterion, framing an ideal as "additive subgroup, PLUS
   absorption by the whole ring — not just its own elements."
2. **Conflict evidence**: the $M_2(\mathbb R)$ left-ideal-but-not-right-ideal demonstration,
   breaking MC-2 directly by showing the two absorption checks genuinely diverge.
3. **Contrast pair**: the subring criterion (closure among own elements only) directly against the
   ideal criterion (absorption by the whole ring), isolating MC-1; the integer-multiple formula
   against the ring-element formula for principal ideals, isolating MC-3.
4. **Mastery gate**: require a correct three-part ideal verification (additive subgroup, left
   absorption, right absorption), a correct principal-ideal construction using ring elements (not
   integers), and a correct proof that a kernel is always a two-sided ideal, at the Blueprint's own
   stated PASS_CRITERION of 5/5.

## Tutor Actions
- Never accept "it's closed, so it's an ideal" without an explicit absorption check against
  elements OUTSIDE the candidate subset.
- In a non-commutative ring, always require BOTH left and right absorption to be checked
  separately and explicitly before declaring a two-sided ideal.
- When a principal ideal is generated, require the learner to state the formula using ring elements
  ($\{ra:r\in R\}$), not integer multiples, unless the ring genuinely is $\mathbb Z$ itself.

## Voice Teaching Notes
- Say "does that absorption check use an element from OUTSIDE the subset, or only from inside it?"
  whenever the subring/ideal distinction is at risk of being missed.
- When only left absorption has been checked in a non-commutative setting, ask "have you checked
  the right side too — do they have to match here?"

## Assessment Signals
- **Rung 1 (recognition)**: learner correctly verifies the additive-subgroup condition for a
  candidate ideal.
- **Rung 2 (application)**: learner correctly checks BOTH left and right absorption for a candidate
  subset in a non-commutative ring, correctly classifying it as one-sided, two-sided, or not an
  ideal at all.
- **Rung 3 (transfer)**: learner correctly proves that the kernel of a given ring homomorphism is a
  two-sided ideal, citing the absorption argument on both sides explicitly.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
- If MC-1 recurs, re-verify absorption explicitly against an element outside the candidate subset
  for the specific case in question.
- If MC-2 recurs, re-verify both sides independently for the specific non-commutative example in
  question.
- If MC-3 recurs, re-generate the specific principal ideal using ring-element multiples, in a ring
  where this genuinely differs from integer multiples.
