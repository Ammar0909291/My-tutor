## Learning Objective
- The learner can differentiate both sides of an implicit equation in $x$ and $y$ with respect to $x$, applying the Chain Rule to every $y$-term (since $\frac{d}{dx}[y^n]=ny^{n-1}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}$), and solve algebraically for $\frac{dy}{dx}$.
- The learner can recognize why implicit differentiation is genuinely necessary for equations that cannot be solved explicitly for $y$ as a function of $x$, and can find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ directly, without ever isolating $y$.
- The learner can correctly distinguish differentiating an $x$-term (ordinary power rule, no extra factor) from a $y$-term (chain-rule factor $\frac{dy}{dx}$ required every time), and recognizes implicit differentiation as the PREFERRED method even for equations that COULD be solved explicitly.

## Core Understanding
When an equation mixes $x$ and $y$, with $y$ understood as SOME function of $x$ (even without an explicit formula for it), differentiating a $y$-term like $y^n$ with respect to $x$ is exactly a Chain Rule application: $\frac{d}{dx}[y^n]=ny^{n-1}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx}$, with $y(x)$ playing the role of the Chain Rule's inner function. This factor $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is NOT optional — it is `math.calc.chain-rule`'s own required inner-derivative factor, needed every single time a $y$-term is differentiated, standing in sharp contrast to an $x$-term like $x^n$, which needs no such extra factor since $x$ is literally the variable being differentiated with respect to. Implicit differentiation is genuinely NECESSARY, not merely a shortcut, for equations like $x^3+y^3=6xy$ that cannot be algebraically rearranged into an explicit closed-form formula $y=f(x)$ at all — differentiating both sides directly (applying the Chain Rule to every $y$-term and the Product Rule wherever $x$ and $y$ are multiplied together) and then solving algebraically for $\frac{dy}{dx}$ obtains the derivative WITHOUT ever needing an explicit formula for $y$. Even for equations where explicit solving IS possible (like a circle, $x^2+y^2=25$, giving two branches $y=\pm\sqrt{25-x^2}$), implicit differentiation is often PREFERRED: it handles both branches simultaneously in one pass, avoiding the extra bookkeeping of two separate branch-by-branch differentiations and $\pm$-case tracking.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — "differentiate both sides, and whenever you see a $y$, multiply by $\frac{dy}{dx}$."** A mechanical rule followed without yet distinguishing WHY $y$-terms need the extra factor while $x$-terms don't. *Upgrade trigger*: forgetting to collect and isolate the scattered $\frac{dy}{dx}$ terms after differentiating, treating differentiation alone as the complete task.
2. **Intermediate — "$y$ is a function of $x$, so every $y$-term is a Chain Rule application; after differentiating both sides, collect every $\frac{dy}{dx}$ term and solve algebraically, exactly like solving for any unknown."** The full two-phase procedure (differentiate, then algebraically isolate) is now explicit. *Upgrade trigger*: encountering an equation with an $xy$ term, requiring the Product Rule ALONGSIDE the Chain Rule.
3. **Advanced — "implicit differentiation isn't only for equations that can't be solved explicitly — it's often the more efficient choice even when explicit solving IS possible, since it handles multiple branches in one pass."** The technique's own EFFICIENCY, not just its necessity, is now understood. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to apply the technique to a genuinely applied problem (economics, physics) where $x$ and $y$ represent meaningful quantities.
4. **Expert — recognizing implicit differentiation as a direct, disciplined generalization of the Chain Rule to a bookkeeping situation where the "inner function" $y(x)$ has no explicit formula, needed for the SAME reason the Chain Rule is always needed: an inner quantity's own rate of change must be accounted for.** This concept is seen as adding no new differentiation rule, only a new application context. *Shelf life*: permanent.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant failure is a direct transplant of `math.calc.chain-rule`'s own foundational misconception into this new bookkeeping context — the Blueprint itself explicitly names this connection: differentiating a $y$-term like $y^2$ as if it were an ordinary $x$-term, omitting the required chain-rule factor $\frac{dy}{dx}$, is exactly the same "stop after the outer piece" error already documented as the single highest-frequency error for the Chain Rule generally (MC-1, CHAIN-RULE-FACTOR-OMITTED-ON-Y-TERMS). A second, distinct failure is procedural incompleteness: since every SIMPLER differentiation task ends the moment the derivative is computed, a learner treats implicit differentiation the same way, differentiating both sides correctly but leaving the resulting $\frac{dy}{dx}$ terms scattered on both sides of the equation without performing the essential algebraic step of collecting and isolating them — an additional completion step genuinely new to this concept, easy to overlook precisely because no prior differentiation task ever required it (MC-2, DY-DX-TERMS-NOT-COLLECTED-AND-ISOLATED). A third failure is a belief about WHEN to apply the technique rather than a computational error: because most introductory treatments introduce implicit differentiation specifically in the context of equations that "can't be solved explicitly," a learner infers the technique is a last-resort fallback rather than recognizing it as often the more EFFICIENT choice even when explicit solving remains available (MC-3, IMPLICIT-DIFFERENTIATION-TREATED-AS-LAST-RESORT-ONLY).

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1, MC-2, MC-3) and its own repair actions B01–B03. **The Blueprint's Misconception Registry carries a Severity column (Foundational, Foundational, Moderate) but no explicit birth-type column**; all three classifications below are independently assigned here.

- **MC-1 — CHAIN-RULE-FACTOR-OMITTED-ON-Y-TERMS** (the Blueprint's own explicitly declared "Foundational" misconception — the Blueprint's own text names this as "the direct transplant of `math.calc.chain-rule`'s own inner-derivative-missing misconception into this context")
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, direct transplant/overgeneralization of `math.calc.chain-rule`'s own INNER-DERIVATIVE-MISSING misconception — the identical mechanism, applied to a $y$-term rather than an explicit composite function.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: writing $\frac{d}{dx}[y^2]=2y$ (treating a $y$-term exactly like an $x$-term), with no $\frac{dy}{dx}$ factor.
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's B01 P41): ask a student to differentiate a $y$-term and check whether they omit the $\frac{dy}{dx}$ factor.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — re-walk Example 1's explicit contrast between the $x^2$ term (no extra factor) and the $y^2$ term (factor required), re-anchoring on "$y$ is a function of $x$ here — every $y$-term is a composite function needing the Chain Rule, exactly like any other composition."
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh implicit equation, the learner writes the $\frac{dy}{dx}$ factor on every $y$-term without prompting, before attempting to solve for $\frac{dy}{dx}$.

- **MC-2 — DY-DX-TERMS-NOT-COLLECTED-AND-ISOLATED** (the Blueprint's own second "Foundational" misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 5, instruction-induced. Every simpler differentiation task the learner has previously encountered ends the moment the derivative is computed — there is no prior context requiring an ADDITIONAL algebraic completion step, so the genuinely new requirement (collect and isolate $\frac{dy}{dx}$) is easy to treat as unnecessary, since the pattern "differentiation is the whole task" held reliably in every earlier context.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: leaving $\frac{dy}{dx}$ terms scattered on both sides of the differentiated equation, without collecting them onto one side and solving.
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's B02 P41): ask a student to finish solving for $\frac{dy}{dx}$ after differentiating both sides, and check whether they stop before collecting scattered $\frac{dy}{dx}$ terms.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — re-walk Example 2's explicit term-collection and factoring stage, re-anchoring on "differentiating both sides is only HALF the task — you still have to solve the resulting equation for $\frac{dy}{dx}$, just like solving for any unknown."
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh implicit equation, the learner explicitly collects every $\frac{dy}{dx}$ term onto one side, factors it out, and divides — completing the algebraic solve without prompting.

- **MC-3 — IMPLICIT-DIFFERENTIATION-TREATED-AS-LAST-RESORT-ONLY** (the Blueprint's own third registered misconception, "Moderate" severity)
  - **Birth type**: Type 5, instruction-induced. Because implicit differentiation is most commonly INTRODUCED via equations that genuinely cannot be solved explicitly, the learner infers this necessity condition is also the technique's ONLY valid use case, never having been shown a case where it is chosen for efficiency despite explicit solving being available.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: answering "yes" when asked whether implicit differentiation should only be used when an equation genuinely cannot be solved explicitly for $y$.
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's A03 hook): "should I only use implicit differentiation when an equation genuinely can't be solved explicitly for y?" — a "yes" answer reveals MC-3.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — re-walk Example 3's side-by-side comparison (one implicit pass versus two separate explicit-branch computations reaching the identical answer), re-anchoring on "implicit differentiation is often simply the more efficient choice, not merely a fallback."
  - **Verification of death**: given an equation that COULD be solved explicitly (like a circle), the learner still considers implicit differentiation as a genuine option, not merely a fallback for equations that resist explicit solving.

## Analogies
- **Best — asking someone their height without measuring them directly, using only a known relationship (their shadow's length and the sun's angle).** You never isolate "height = ..." explicitly; you work with the RELATIONSHIP itself and extract the rate of change you need — exactly like never isolating $y=f(x)$ and instead extracting $\frac{dy}{dx}$ directly from the relating equation.
- **Alternative — solving for an unknown salary raise from a total-compensation equation, without first isolating "salary" as a standalone formula.** You differentiate/manipulate the WHOLE equation and then algebraically solve for the ONE quantity you want ($\frac{dy}{dx}$), never needing a separate formula for $y$ itself along the way.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "implicit differentiation is a special trick for when you're stuck."** This vague phrasing licenses MC-3 directly, framing the technique as a last resort. Say "implicit differentiation is a DIRECT method for finding $\frac{dy}{dx}$ from any relationship between $x$ and $y$ — sometimes it's the ONLY way, and sometimes it's simply the FASTEST way" instead.

## Demonstrations
- **The x-term-versus-y-term contrast.** Differentiate the two terms of $x^2+y^2=25$ side by side, showing $x^2$ needs no extra factor while $y^2$ needs $\frac{dy}{dx}$. *Predict whether both terms will differentiate the same way before computing.* Getting a genuinely different treatment for the $y$-term is the demonstration for MC-1.
- **The unfinished-solve reveal.** Differentiate $x^2+y^2=25$ to get $2x+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0$, then stop and ask "is this the final answer for $\frac{dy}{dx}$?" *Predict whether this equation IS the answer before completing the solve.* Realizing an additional algebraic step (isolating $\frac{dy}{dx}=-x/y$) is still required is the demonstration for MC-2.
- **The two-methods race.** Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ for $x^2+y^2=25$ via implicit differentiation (one pass) versus via explicit branch-by-branch differentiation on BOTH $y=\sqrt{25-x^2}$ and $y=-\sqrt{25-x^2}$ (two passes). *Predict which route will take fewer total computations before doing both.* Reaching the SAME formula via strictly less work with implicit differentiation is the demonstration for MC-3.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction is the argued call for the basic Chain-Rule-on-$y$-terms procedure itself (it is a direct, specific application of the already-mastered Chain Rule, not independently rediscoverable at this level), but the necessity of the algebraic collection step (MC-2) and the efficiency case for implicit differentiation even when explicit solving is available (MC-3) are both genuinely discoverable by direct comparison.
1. **Need** — "Differentiate both sides of $x^2+y^2=25$. Is the resulting equation, $2x+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0$, already the formula for $\frac{dy}{dx}$?" The learner should recognize it is NOT — $\frac{dy}{dx}$ is still tangled inside the equation.
2. **Playground** — try the same "is this the final answer" check on a couple more implicitly-differentiated equations.
3. **Invention** — "What algebraic steps would isolate $\frac{dy}{dx}$ from this equation?" Let the learner connect it to solving any equation for an unknown — collect, factor, divide.
4. **Collision** — confront a learner who stopped after differentiating with the "is this the final answer?" question, revealing the incomplete state.
5. **Formalisation** — state the full two-phase procedure explicitly: differentiate both sides (Chain Rule on every $y$-term), THEN collect and solve for $\frac{dy}{dx}$.
6. **Compression** — "Every $y$-term gets a $\frac{dy}{dx}$ tag — then collect the tags and solve, like any unknown."

## Teaching Sequence
The Chain-Rule-on-$y$-terms discipline (MC-1) must be established FIRST, since the algebraic collection step (MC-2) is meaningless if the differentiation itself is already wrong — a learner who has omitted $\frac{dy}{dx}$ factors cannot meaningfully practice collecting terms that were never written down. Per the Blueprint's own A01/A02 ordering, the collection-and-solving discipline follows directly, using a SECOND example (with a genuine Product Rule step, since $x$ and $y$ are multiplied together) to give the collection step real algebraic substance rather than a trivial one-term isolation. The efficiency argument for implicit differentiation over explicit branch-by-branch work (MC-3) is introduced LAST, per the Blueprint's own A03, once the mechanics are fluent — since it is a STRATEGIC judgment about when to prefer the technique, not a new computational skill. Turn-level scripts for A01–A04 are owned by the Blueprint's Component 5 and are not restated here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Worked Example** — the x-term-versus-y-term contrast (differentiating both terms of $x^2+y^2=25$ side by side), with the learner predicting whether both terms will be treated identically BEFORE either is differentiated. First action; anchors the Chain-Rule-on-$y$-terms discipline concretely.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "After differentiating both sides, is $2x+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0$ already the answer for $\frac{dy}{dx}$?" asked immediately after a differentiation is completed. Surfaces MC-2 in one turn.
- **DO: Demonstration** — the two-methods race (implicit versus explicit branch-by-branch, on the same circle equation), run with the learner comparing the number of separate computations required by each route.
- **TEST-THINKING: Error Analysis** — "A student differentiated $xy=10$ and wrote $y+x=0$. What's wrong?" (targets both a missing Product Rule application on the left AND a missing Chain Rule factor, since the correct differentiation is $y+x\frac{dy}{dx}=0$) targets MC-1 directly, in a context also requiring the Product Rule.
- **Does NOT fit: introducing related rates (differentiation with respect to TIME) or inverse trig derivatives here.** Both are the two concepts this one directly unlocks, and each deserves its own dedicated treatment building on this concept's own fluency.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "every $y$-term gets a $\frac{dy}{dx}$ tag — then collect the tags and solve, like solving for any unknown." Say it every time a new implicit equation is differentiated, not just the first. Listen for a learner who differentiates a $y$-term with no mention of a second multiplied factor — that fluent-but-incomplete confidence is the tell for MC-1, the identical tell already documented for `math.calc.chain-rule`'s own foundational MC-1. Listen for a learner who states the differentiated equation and immediately calls it "the answer" — that premature stopping point is the tell for MC-2. ## Assessment Signals
- **Differentiates a $y$-term as if it were an $x$-term, omitting the $\frac{dy}{dx}$ factor** — MC-1. Route to the x-term-versus-y-term contrast, on the exact equation in question.
- **Differentiates correctly but leaves $\frac{dy}{dx}$ terms scattered without collecting and solving** — MC-2. Route to the unfinished-solve reveal, on the exact equation in question.
- **States or implies implicit differentiation should only be used when explicit solving is impossible** — MC-3. Route to the two-methods race, on an equation where explicit solving is genuinely available.
- **Correctly applies the Chain Rule to every $y$-term, collects and isolates $\frac{dy}{dx}$, and recognizes the technique's efficiency even when explicit solving is possible** — the intended target state.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's A04 gate, MAMR 4/5 (⌈0.8×5⌉). ## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance here is "wait, is $2x+2y\frac{dy}{dx}=0$ my final answer, or do I need to do more?" — a reasonable question given that every prior differentiation task ended at exactly this point. The concept-specific smaller question returns to a familiar algebraic frame: **"If I told you $3+2y=0$ and asked you to solve for $y$, what would you do? Now do the SAME thing, treating $\frac{dy}{dx}$ as the unknown you're solving for."** The learner applies ordinary equation-solving to the $\frac{dy}{dx}$-containing equation, recognizing it as structurally the same task. Then return: "differentiating both sides was only step one — step two is solving the resulting equation for $\frac{dy}{dx}$, exactly like solving for any unknown." If the frustration is instead about WHY implicit differentiation is used when explicit solving seems possible, shrink to the bare check: **"How many separate formulas would you need to write down if you solved $x^2+y^2=25$ explicitly for $y$? Now: how many did implicit differentiation need?"** Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Memory Hooks
- Concept type: **procedure with two embedded precision requirements plus one strategic judgment** (the Chain-Rule-on-$y$-terms discipline and the collect-and-isolate algebraic step are both procedural precision points, and the choice to use implicit differentiation even when explicit solving is available is a conceptual/strategic judgment, all needed together). Review by *requiring the learner to write out the fully-differentiated equation BEFORE beginning to solve for $\frac{dy}{dx}$, as a visibly separate step*, never accepting a jump straight from the original equation to a final $\frac{dy}{dx}$ formula, since skipping the visible intermediate step lets MC-1 and MC-2 both pass undetected on problems where the final answer happens to look plausible.
- Concept-specific deviation: keep at least one equation in the review rotation containing an $xy$-style mixed term (requiring the Product Rule ALONGSIDE the Chain Rule), since a learner practiced only on pure-power-term equations (like $x^2+y^2=25$) may not generalize the Chain-Rule discipline to a context also requiring a different rule simultaneously.
