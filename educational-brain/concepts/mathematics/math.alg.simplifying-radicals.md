## Learning Objective
- The learner can simplify a square root by factoring the radicand and extracting the largest perfect-square factor, rewriting √n as a√b where b carries no further perfect-square factor.
- The learner can extend the identical extraction principle to higher-index radicals, matching the extraction target to the root's own index (perfect cubes for cube roots, and so on).
- The learner can verify a simplified radical is genuinely *fully* simplified, by re-checking the remaining radicand for any leftover perfect-power factor rather than stopping after the first successful extraction.

## Core Understanding
Simplifying a radical means rewriting it so that nothing extractable remains hidden inside the radical sign — the radicand, after simplification, must contain no factor that is itself a perfect power matching the root's index. The mechanism is the product rule for radicals, already established in `math.alg.radicals`: ⁿ√(xy) = ⁿ√x · ⁿ√y, applied by factoring the radicand to isolate its largest perfect n-th-power piece and pulling that piece's root out in front. This concept's entire contribution beyond that parent definition is a single, precise discipline: *largest*, not merely *a*. Extracting some perfect-square factor and stopping — √72 = √(4×18) = 2√18 — is a genuinely correct intermediate step, but it is not the finished answer, because 18 itself still hides a further perfect-square factor (9); only continuing until no extractable factor remains (2√18 = 2×3√2 = 6√2, or equivalently spotting 72 = 36×2 directly) counts as fully simplified. The same discipline, with the same "keep checking until nothing remains" verification habit, extends unchanged to higher-index roots — a cube root is simplified by extracting perfect cubes rather than perfect squares, but the underlying question ("does anything extractable remain?") is identical regardless of index.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — find a perfect-square piece and pull it out.** For √12, spot that 12 = 4×3 and 4 is a perfect square, giving 2√3. *Upgrade trigger*: a radicand like 72, where the *first* perfect-square factor found (4) is not the *largest* one available (36), so "pull one out" alone produces an under-simplified answer. *Shelf life*: one session.
2. **Intermediate — after every extraction, check again.** Having pulled 4 out of 72 to get 2√18, the job is not done until 18 is itself checked for a remaining perfect-square factor (it has one: 9), and the check-and-continue cycle repeats until nothing is left to extract. *Upgrade trigger*: a cube-root or higher-index radical, where "perfect square" is no longer the target and the extraction rule must generalise. *Shelf life*: durable and permanently correct, once installed as a habit rather than a one-off instruction.
3. **Advanced — go straight for the largest factor by full prime factorisation, when the radicand is unfamiliar.** 72 = 2³×3², so the largest perfect-square factor is 2²×3² = 36, extractable in one pass without any iterative checking. *Upgrade trigger*: a radicand large enough that spotting the largest factor by inspection is unreliable, motivating a systematic (prime-factorisation-based) approach as a fallback.
4. **Expert — "fully simplified" is a well-defined canonical form, and any two correct simplification paths must converge on it.** Whether reached by iterative partial extraction (4, then 9) or direct largest-factor spotting (36 at once), the destination — 6√2 — is unique, because it is defined by the radicand having zero remaining extractable factors, a property of the *number*, not of the *path* taken to discover it. *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the model that makes "is this simplified?" a checkable yes/no question rather than a matter of opinion or partial credit.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant and almost universal failure is mistaking a genuinely correct partial extraction for a complete one: having correctly pulled a perfect-square factor out from under the radical, the learner reports that result as final without re-examining the remaining radicand for a further extractable factor — √72 becomes 2√18 and stops there, when 18 = 9×2 still hides a perfect square. This is not an error of *method* (the extraction performed was entirely correct) but of *verification discipline*: no step in the process signals, on its own, that more work remains, so the habit of re-checking must be built deliberately rather than emerging naturally from getting the extraction itself right. The second failure is a validity error rather than a completeness one: the learner attempts to extract a factor from under the radical that is not actually a perfect power matching the root's index at all — for instance, pulling out a factor of 3 from √12 because 3 divides 12, without checking that 3 is genuinely a perfect square (it is not). This failure is comparatively rarer once the product rule itself is secure, but it recurs specifically under time pressure, when a learner reaches for the first available factor rather than deliberately checking it against the required perfect-power form.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1..MC-2) and its repair actions B01–B02, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — PARTIAL-EXTRACTION-MISTAKEN-FOR-FULL-SIMPLIFICATION** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of "I performed the extraction step correctly, so I am done" — a reasonable inference for a single-step procedure, applied to a task that is actually iterative until a stopping condition (no extractable factor remains) is explicitly checked.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: reporting √72 = 2√18 as a final, fully simplified answer.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): present the two-step case and ask directly whether 2√18 is fully simplified.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — re-check the remaining radicand (18) explicitly for a further perfect-square factor, continuing the extraction (18 = 9×2, so 2√18 = 2×3√2 = 6√2) rather than accepting the first correct-but-incomplete result.
  - **Verification of death**: given √200, the learner reaches 10√2 directly (or via iterative checking that genuinely terminates there), not 2√50 or 5√8, and can confirm unprompted that 2 has no further perfect-square factor.

- **MC-2 — NON-PERFECT-POWER-FACTOR-INCORRECTLY-EXTRACTED** (foundational)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of "any factor of the radicand can be pulled partway out," collapsing the specific requirement (the factor must itself be a perfect power matching the index) into the much weaker, incorrect requirement (the factor merely divides the radicand).
  - **Characteristic phrase**: attempting to extract a factor from under a radical sign that is a genuine divisor of the radicand but not itself a perfect square (or matching perfect power).
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): review a submitted extraction and check whether the extracted factor is genuinely a perfect power of the matching index.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — re-verify the extracted factor is genuinely a perfect power (2²=4, 3²=9, 4²=16... for square roots; 2³=8, 3³=27... for cube roots) before it is removed from under the radical, treating this verification as a mandatory checkpoint rather than an optional sanity check.
  - **Verification of death**: given a radicand with several divisors, only some of which are perfect squares, the learner correctly identifies and extracts only the genuine perfect-square factor.

## Analogies
- **Best — searching a drawer for the largest matching set before closing it.** Pulling out one matching pair of socks and closing the drawer, when a larger matching set was still inside, is technically a correct action performed on an incomplete search — the drawer (radicand) must be checked again before the task counts as done. This directly targets MC-1's completion-judgment error.
- **Alternative — a security check that only accepts an exact match, not a rough resemblance.** A factor that merely divides the radicand, without being a genuine perfect power, fails the check and must not be extracted — this targets MC-2's looser, incorrect extraction criterion directly.
- **Story analogy** — packing the largest box available before resorting to several smaller ones; both partial-then-continued packing and directly-finding-the-largest-box packing reach the same final arrangement, mirroring the iterative-versus-direct routes to the same fully simplified radical.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "simplify means make the number under the root smaller."** This is roughly true but dangerously incomplete: it gives no criterion for *when to stop*, licensing MC-1's premature stopping point (18 is smaller than 72, so this framing offers no signal that more extraction remains).
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "if it divides evenly, you can pull it out."** This is exactly MC-2 stated as a rule; the correct criterion is not "divides evenly" but "is itself a perfect power of the matching index."

## Demonstrations
- **The re-check, staged live.** Simplify √72 deliberately in two visible stages — extract 4 to reach 2√18, then explicitly pause and ask "is there anything left to extract from 18?" before continuing to 6√2. *Predict, before checking, whether 18 hides a further perfect-square factor* — the pause itself, not merely the final answer, is the demonstration, operationalising Blueprint B01's repair as a standing habit.
- **The largest-factor shortcut, contrasted with the iterative route.** Simplify √72 both ways side by side — spotting 36 directly versus extracting 4 then 9 — showing both converge on 6√2. This grounds the advanced mental model (go straight for the largest factor when confident) as a genuine shortcut rather than a different, competing method.
- **The perfect-power verification check.** Before extracting any factor, require the learner to state explicitly which power it is a perfect power *of* (e.g. "9 is 3 squared") rather than merely noting that it divides the radicand — this directly operationalises Blueprint B02's repair as a pre-extraction habit.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for the core extraction mechanism itself, since it is a direct application of `math.alg.radicals`' own already-established product rule, not a new principle to re-derive. What is genuinely discoverable, and should be surfaced as a question, is the completeness criterion this concept exists specifically to sharpen: (1) **Need** — "Simplify √72 by extracting 4. Is 2√18 as simple as this can get?" (2) **Playground** — attempt to simplify 2√18 further, discovering 18 = 9×2 still has a perfect-square factor. (3) **Invention** — "How would you know, just by looking at a simplified radical, whether you're really done?" (4) **Collision** — offer a genuinely fully-simplified radical (e.g. √15, with no perfect-square factor at all) alongside an under-simplified one (2√18), and ask the learner to sort which needs more work — a rule based only on "does the number look smaller" fails to distinguish them reliably. (5) **Formalisation** — fully simplified means the remaining radicand has no factor that is a perfect power of the matching index; check this directly, don't rely on the number merely looking small. (6) **Compression** — "After every extraction, ask: does anything perfect-power-shaped still hide inside? Stop only when the answer is genuinely no."

## Teaching Sequence
Square-root extraction with the full "check again" discipline (TA-A01/TA-A02, targeting MC-1) must be secure before higher-index radicals are introduced (TA-A03) — extending the extraction target from perfect squares to perfect cubes is a small notational generalisation, but the completeness-checking *habit* that MC-1 targets must already be automatic, or it will need to be re-taught twice, once per index. The perfect-power verification check (targeting MC-2) is deliberately interleaved throughout rather than taught as a separate stage, because MC-2's error (extracting a non-perfect-power factor) can occur at any point in the iterative extraction process, not only at the first step — the verification habit needs to be attached to *every* extraction attempt, not scheduled as a one-time lesson. The Blueprint's own Component 5 sequence (TA-A01 largest-factor extraction via conceptual shift, TA-A02 completeness-check contrast pair, TA-A03 higher-index generalisation, TA-A04 mastery gate) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Demonstration** — the re-check staged live, run on the learner's own partial extraction rather than a hypothetical one, so the "not done yet" realisation is felt as a consequence of their own correct-but-incomplete work.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "is there anything left to extract?" asked after every extraction, as a standing pre-answer checkpoint, until the habit is automatic.
- **ORGANIZE: Sorting / Categorisation** — sorting a set of "simplified" and "not-yet-fully-simplified" radicals (including genuine near-misses like 2√18) before correcting any of them, isolating the completeness judgment from the extraction arithmetic itself.
- **Does NOT fit: drilling only radicands whose largest perfect-square factor is obvious at a glance (e.g. √16, √25).** These never give MC-1 a chance to surface, because there is no smaller, tempting, incomplete factor to stop at prematurely.
- **Does NOT fit: teaching the completeness check as a one-time rule stated once at the start.** MC-1's whole mechanism is that the check is *skipped in the moment of relief at having found something to extract*; the check must be rehearsed as a standing habit, not merely stated as a fact.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "is there anything left in there — check again." Slow down on "check again," every time, regardless of how confident the learner sounds. Listen for a tone of relief or finality immediately after a first successful extraction ("okay, got it, two root eighteen") — that relief is the audible signature of MC-1 about to lock in a premature stopping point, and the recovery is to interrupt gently before the answer is finalised, not after. For MC-2, listen for a learner justifying an extraction by divisibility alone ("three goes into twelve, so...") rather than by a perfect-power check ("is three a perfect square? no") — the divisibility-only justification is the diagnostic phrase. A learner who states, unprompted, "let me check if that's fully reduced" after any extraction has crossed the real threshold of this concept. ## Assessment Signals
- **A correct extraction reported as final when a further perfect-power factor remains (e.g. 2√18)** — MC-1; route to the re-check demonstration, never to a bare restatement of "make sure it's fully simplified."
- **An extracted factor that merely divides the radicand without being a genuine perfect power** — MC-2, cleanly diagnostic; route to the perfect-power verification check.
- **The largest factor spotted directly, with no intermediate partial-extraction step** — the strongest positive signal available, indicating the advanced mental model (direct largest-factor spotting) has taken hold; do not require the iterative route once this appears reliably.
- **Correct extraction on a square root, followed by a fumbled attempt to identify the correct target power on a cube root (e.g. still checking for perfect squares)** — a narrower index-generalisation gap, not this concept's own foundational misconceptions; diagnose and repair via the higher-index worked examples specifically.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A04 gate, MAMR ⌈0.8×5⌉ = 4/5, including its P76 transfer probe (a carpentry diagonal-brace-length problem requiring the learner to critique a colleague's genuinely incomplete simplification) — a gate passed without that critique-of-another's-work item does not certify the completeness-checking habit independent of the learner's own extraction fluency.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance is "wait, is this done or not?" — a genuinely well-posed uncertainty about the completeness criterion, not confusion about the extraction mechanics themselves. The concept-specific smaller question isolates the check from any further computation: **"Just look at the number left under the root — 18. Forget the 2 out front for a second. Does 18 have a perfect-square factor hiding in it?"** — narrowing attention to exactly the one remaining judgment. If the freeze is at the extraction-validity question (MC-2's territory) instead, shrink to a bare fact check: **"Is 3 a perfect square? What number, squared, gives 3?"** — confirming the perfect-power test directly, on ground the learner can answer from memorised squares alone. Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.
