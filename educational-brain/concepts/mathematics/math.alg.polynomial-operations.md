## Learning Objective
- The learner can add and subtract polynomials by grouping and combining terms of matching degree, leaving genuinely unlike-degree terms untouched.
- The learner can rewrite a subtraction p(x) − q(x) as p(x) + (−1)·q(x) and correctly negate every term of q(x), not only the first.
- The learner can multiply any two polynomials by distributing every term of the first factor across every term of the second, then collecting, without depending on FOIL as anything more than a special-case shortcut.
- The learner can predict and verify the degree of a sum (at most the larger of the two degrees) and of a product (exactly the sum of the two degrees), and use that prediction as a self-check.

## Core Understanding
Polynomial addition, subtraction, and multiplication are not three new operations to learn; they are `math.alg.like-terms` and the distributive law, applied at the scale of a whole polynomial instead of a single bracket. Addition and subtraction group terms by degree and combine coefficients within each degree, exactly as before — the only genuinely new fact is that subtraction of an entire polynomial means negating *every* term of the polynomial being subtracted, because p(x) − q(x) is p(x) + (−1)·q(x), and −1 distributes across all of q(x)'s terms, not just its first. Multiplication is the distributive law run twice over: every term of the first factor multiplies every term of the second, producing deg(p)·deg(q)-many... no — producing (number of terms in p) × (number of terms in q) partial products, which are then collected by degree. FOIL is a mnemonic for exactly one case of this — two terms times two terms — and it is dangerous precisely because it looks like a complete method rather than the 2×2 instance of a general rule; the moment either factor has three or more terms, a learner who has only ever been taught FOIL has nothing to fall back on. The degree arithmetic — deg(p+q) ≤ max(deg p, deg q), deg(p·q) = deg p + deg q — is not a separate fact to memorise; it falls directly out of "combine like terms" (addition cannot create a higher-degree term, and can only cancel the top one) and "every term times every term" (the single highest-degree product is always leading-term times leading-term).

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — line up the columns by degree, then add down each column.** Like the receipt/coin-sorting picture: x³ terms in one pile, x² in another, and so on; only same-pile items combine. *Upgrade trigger*: subtraction, where "line up and add" alone gives no instruction for what to do with the minus sign between the two polynomials. *Shelf life*: one to two sessions.
2. **Intermediate — subtraction is addition of the negative, term by term.** p(x) − q(x) = p(x) + (−1)·q(x); every term of q(x) changes sign before anything is combined. *Upgrade trigger*: multiplication, where there is no "column" to line terms up in beforehand — the terms of the product do not exist until the distribution is performed. *Shelf life*: durable, and it remains correct forever; it simply stops being sufficient on its own once multiplication enters.
3. **Advanced — multiplication is the distributive law applied (terms of p) × (terms of q) times, and FOIL is its 2-by-2 special case.** A grid with rows for one factor's terms and columns for the other's, one cell per partial product, generalises cleanly to any number of terms on either side. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to predict, before multiplying anything, how many terms the fully-expanded (but not yet collected) product will contain, and how many distinct degrees it can produce.
4. **Expert — degree arithmetic is a free consequence of the operation's structure, not a separate rule.** deg(p+q) ≤ max(deg p, deg q) because addition can only combine or cancel matching-degree terms, never invent a higher one; deg(p·q) = deg p + deg q because the unique highest-degree product is always leading-term × leading-term, and no other pairing can reach or exceed it. *Shelf life*: permanent — this is the model underlying every later claim about polynomial degree, including why a degree-n polynomial has at most n roots.

## Why Students Fail
The most consequential failure is confined almost entirely to subtraction: the learner negates only the first term of the polynomial being subtracted and leaves the rest as written — (x² + 3x + 1) − (x² − 2x + 4) becomes x² + 3x + 1 − x² − 2x + 4 rather than x² + 3x + 1 − x² + 2x − 4. This happens because the minus sign is read as a local prefix attached to the term it visually precedes (the first term inside the second polynomial's brackets), rather than as multiplication of the *entire* trailing polynomial by −1; it is the polynomial-scale reappearance of the same notational misreading `math.alg.simplification`'s MC-2 produces for a single bracket, and it is more damaging here because a three-or-more-term polynomial gives it more room to go unnoticed. The second major failure is the reverse problem in multiplication: the learner has internalised FOIL as *the* method for multiplying polynomials, rather than as a memory aid for the specific two-term-by-two-term case, so a product like (x + 2)(x² − 3x + 5) either stalls (FOIL has no natural extension past four products) or is forced into four terms when six are actually required, silently dropping two partial products. A quieter failure sits underneath both: `math.alg.like-terms` MC-1 (combining unlike terms) resurfaces the moment addition or multiplication produces several same-looking terms of different degrees — a learner otherwise executing the procedure correctly can still merge an x³ and an x² term out of sheer term-count pressure.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 2 registry (MC-1..MC-3) and its Component 4/5 repair actions B01–B03, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — UNLIKE-TERMS-COMBINE** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation — the direct polynomial-scale recurrence of `math.alg.like-terms` MC-1, triggered afresh because a multi-term polynomial sum or product presents many terms at once and revives the "merge everything" numeric-arithmetic pressure.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: "x² + x = 2x²" or "x² + x = 2x," and in multiplication, "(x²)(x) = x²" (multiplying, rather than adding, the exponents incorrectly, or the reverse error of adding exponents when combining rather than multiplying).
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): "Does 3x² + 2x equal 5x², 5x, or cannot be simplified?" — an answer of 5x² or 5x confirms MC-1.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — re-anchor on "x² = x·x is a different quantity from x, the way area differs from length," then require both terms be independently checked for matching variable *and* matching exponent before any combination.
  - **Verification of death**: given (4x³ + 2x² − 5) + (−x³ − 3x² + 2x + 1), the learner produces 3x³ − x² + 2x − 4 unprompted, correctly leaving the lone 2x term uncombined with anything.

- **MC-2 — SUBTRACTION-DISTRIBUTES-FIRST-TERM-ONLY** (foundational)
  - **Birth type**: Type 4, notation-induced — the minus sign between two polynomials is read as modifying only the adjacent term, not as multiplication of the whole trailing polynomial by −1; this is `math.alg.simplification` MC-2 recurring at polynomial scale, where the larger number of trailing terms makes a partial negation both easier to make and harder to spot.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: shown (x² + 3x + 1) − (x² − 2x + 4), "x² + 3x + 1 − x² − 2x + 4" (only the leading x² term of the second polynomial changed sign).
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): "Compute (5x² + 3x + 1) − (2x² − x + 4). Which is correct after removing the parentheses: (A) 5x²+3x+1−2x²−x+4, or (B) 5x²+3x+1−2x²+x−4?" — choosing (A) confirms MC-2.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — rewrite the subtraction explicitly as addition of (−1)·(second polynomial) first, requiring every one of its terms to be individually negated before anything is combined, using the debt-reversal framing (owing the negative of a whole bundle reverses every component of the bundle).
  - **Verification of death**: given (2x³ + x − 5) − (x³ − 3x² + 2), the learner produces x³ + 3x² + x − 7, correctly recovering the sign of the middle term that MC-2 would have left untouched.

- **MC-3 — FOIL-FOR-ALL-PRODUCTS** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 5, instruction-induced — FOIL is typically taught, and drilled, as a self-contained named procedure for binomial×binomial multiplication, without being explicitly framed as one instance of "every term times every term"; the learner has no fallback once a factor outgrows two terms because none was ever offered.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: attempting (x + 1)(x² + 2x + 3) "by FOIL," producing exactly four partial products (First, Outer, Inner, Last) instead of the required six, and stalling or silently dropping two products.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): "Compute (x+1)(x²+2x+3) using FOIL. How many partial products do you get: (A) 4, or (B) 6?" — choosing (A) confirms MC-3.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — generalise explicitly: FOIL is (2 terms)×(2 terms) = 4 products; here it is (2 terms)×(3 terms) = 6 products; lay out the grid method (rows × columns, one cell per partial product) as the general tool FOIL was always secretly a special case of.
  - **Verification of death**: given (2x + 1)(x² + x − 4), the learner distributes all six partial products (2x³, 2x², −8x, x², x, −4) before collecting, without invoking FOIL by name.

## Analogies
- **Best — sorting coins by type.** Quarters, dimes, and nickels total only within their own type; you cannot add a quarter and a dime and call the result "two quarters." x² terms, x terms, and constants are the coin types of polynomial addition — this is the Blueprint's own P03 analogy and it directly targets MC-1, because the "type" check (variable *and* exponent) is exactly what a learner reaching for a quick numeric merge is skipping.
- **Alternative — owing the negative of a whole bundle.** If you owe a friend "the negative of" a bundle of items (2x² − x + 4), every component of the bundle reverses — this targets MC-2 directly, since a bundle's negation cannot stop partway through the bundle.
- **Story analogy** — a factory line where every item on conveyor belt A must be paired with every item on conveyor belt B before assembly is complete; skipping a pairing (as FOIL implicitly does once a belt carries more than two items) means some assembled units never get made.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "FOIL is how you multiply polynomials."** This is precisely MC-3, stated as a rule rather than flagged as an error; FOIL should be introduced, if at all, only *after* the general distribute-every-term-across-every-term method is secure, as a shorthand for its most common special case.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "the minus sign changes the sign of the next number."** This is MC-2 stated as an instruction; the correct frame is "the minus sign changes the sign of *every remaining term of the whole polynomial that follows it*."

## Demonstrations
- **The rewrite-as-addition.** Take (x² + 3x + 1) − (x² − 2x + 4), rewrite it live as (x² + 3x + 1) + (−1)(x² − 2x + 4), distribute the −1 across all three terms visibly, and only then combine. *Predict the sign of every term of the second polynomial first*, before performing the arithmetic — this operationalises Blueprint B02 as a standing habit rather than an error-triggered correction.
- **The grid, built term by term.** For (x + 2)(x² − 3x + 5), draw a 2-row × 3-column grid, fill in all six partial products live, and only then sum by diagonal (degree). *Predict the number of cells before filling any in* — this is the Blueprint's own worked example 2, restaged so the learner commits to a term-count prediction before the mechanical work begins.
- **The degree self-check, run after every product.** After multiplying, state deg(p) and deg(q) separately, predict deg(p·q) = deg(p) + deg(q), then verify the collected result's leading term matches that prediction. This demonstration doubles as the concept's own built-in error detector and should be modelled as a habit, not an occasional aside.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for the core mechanics (like-term collection at polynomial scale, and the distributive law generalised to multi-term × multi-term) — both are extensions of already-secured rules (`math.alg.like-terms`, `math.alg.simplification`), not new principles a learner could plausibly re-derive, and treating them as discoverable risks manufacturing MC-1/MC-2 as plausible-seeming "discovered" shortcuts. What genuinely earns a discovery framing is the degree-arithmetic result, because it is a real, checkable pattern the learner can find rather than be told: (1) **Need** — "What degree do you expect (x² + 1) + (x³ − x) to have?" invites a guess. (2) **Playground** — compute several sums and several products of polynomials of known degree, recording the degree of each result. (3) **Invention** — "What's the rule connecting the degrees of the two polynomials to the degree of the sum? Of the product?" (4) **Collision** — offer a near-cancellation case, e.g. (x³ + x) + (−x³ + 1), where the sum's degree drops below max(deg p, deg q); a "sum always equals the larger degree" rule fails immediately. (5) **Formalisation** — deg(p+q) ≤ max(deg p, deg q) (with equality failing only on cancellation of the leading terms); deg(p·q) = deg p + deg q always, because the top-degree product term is unique. (6) **Compression** — "Addition can only tie or lose the highest degree; multiplication always adds the degrees."

## Teaching Sequence
Addition and subtraction (TA-A01, targeting MC-1 first) must be secure before multiplication (TA-A02) is introduced, because multiplication produces strictly more terms per problem and therefore more opportunities for MC-1's merge reflex to fire — introducing it before the merge reflex is under control conflates a multiplication error with a still-unresolved collection error. Within addition/subtraction, MC-1 (unlike-terms merged) is addressed before MC-2 (sign mishandling in subtraction) is drilled, because a learner who is still merging unlike terms cannot reliably demonstrate whether a subtraction sign error is present or absent — the two failures mask each other if tackled simultaneously. Multiplication itself must be taught via the general distributive/grid method (TA-A02) before FOIL is ever named, precisely to prevent MC-3 from forming in the first place — this concept's Blueprint deliberately never introduces FOIL as an opening method, using it only, if at all, as a retrospective label for the binomial×binomial case once the general method is fluent. The Blueprint's own Component 4 sequence (TA-A01 addition/subtraction → TA-A02 multiplication → TA-A03 contrast pair → TA-A04 mastery gate) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **ORGANIZE: Sorting / grid construction** — building the multiplication grid row-by-row, column-by-column, before any arithmetic is performed; this concept's single highest-value action for preventing MC-3, because it makes "every term times every term" a spatial fact rather than a verbal rule.
- **DO: Demonstration** — the rewrite-as-addition for subtraction, run on the learner's own partially-correct attempt so the specific missed sign is visible against their own work.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — the degree self-check, required before and after every multiplication, turning degree arithmetic into a standing verification habit rather than a fact recited once and forgotten.
- **TEST-THINKING: Error Analysis** — "a student computed (x+1)(x²+2x+3) using FOIL and got four terms. What did they miss, and why doesn't FOIL apply here?" — stronger than a direct correction because it requires the learner to articulate FOIL's actual scope, not just avoid it.
- **Does NOT fit: teaching FOIL as an opening method.** Doing so manufactures MC-3 by construction; FOIL, if used at all, belongs after the general method is fluent, as a named shortcut for its own special case.
- **Does NOT fit: extended addition/subtraction-only drilling before multiplication begins.** MC-1's merge reflex is most reliably tested and cleared under multiplication's higher term-count pressure, and delaying multiplication indefinitely produces false confidence in addition/subtraction fluency.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence for subtraction is "the minus sign negates the *whole* second polynomial, every term." Slow down on "whole" and "every." Listen for a learner reading (x² + 3x + 1) − (x² − 2x + 4) aloud pausing only after the first term of the second polynomial ("x squared, plus three x, plus one, minus x squared... minus two x, plus four") — the pause placement, or its absence before "minus two x," is audible evidence of whether the negation is being applied term-by-term or has already collapsed to "just the first one." For multiplication, listen for the word "FOIL" itself: a learner who reaches for FOIL by name on a three-term factor, rather than pausing to ask "how many products do I need," is very likely mid-MC-3, and the recovery should redirect to counting terms before any acronym is invoked. A confident "six products" or "this needs the grid, not FOIL" stated before any multiplication begins is the strongest positive signal available for this concept. Channel reality is owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals
- **5x² or 5x on "does 3x² + 2x equal 5x², 5x, or cannot be simplified"** — MC-1; route to the area-versus-length re-anchor, never to a bare restatement of the rule.
- **Only the leading term of the subtracted polynomial changes sign** — MC-2, cleanly diagnostic on its own; route to the rewrite-as-addition demonstration.
- **Exactly four partial products on a 2-term×3-term (or larger) multiplication** — MC-3; route to the grid method, explicitly counting rows × columns before any product is computed.
- **Correct partial products, then a collection error mixing degrees** — a residual MC-1 surfacing post-multiplication rather than a multiplication failure; diagnose and repair as MC-1, not as a new error type.
- **Degree self-check volunteered unprompted after a product** — the strongest positive signal this concept produces; it indicates the expert-level model (degree arithmetic as a free structural consequence) has taken hold, not merely been memorised.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A04 gate, MAMR ⌈0.85×5⌉ = 5/5, including its P76 transfer probe (expand-and-simplify a trinomial times a binomial with an explicit degree-arithmetic verification step) — a gate passed without that verification step does not certify the degree-arithmetic learning objective.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance at subtraction is "wait, do I change all the signs or just the first one?" — a genuine, well-posed uncertainty rather than confusion about the material's difficulty. The concept-specific smaller question drops to a two-term case with the rule made maximally visible: **"(5) − (2 − 3). First rewrite it as (5) + (−1)(2 − 3). What is −1 times 2? What is −1 times −3?"** — two bare numeric facts, assembled afterward into the general pattern. At multiplication, the likely utterance is "I don't know how to FOIL this, it has three terms" — the recovery should explicitly validate that FOIL genuinely does not apply, rather than searching for a way to force it: **"You're right, FOIL only works for two terms times two terms. This is two terms times three terms — how many products do you think that makes?"** Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Memory Hooks
- Concept type: **procedure**, three related operations (add, subtract, multiply) sharing one underlying law. Review by *application on mixed-degree, mixed-operation input* every cycle — a review set containing only same-operation, same-degree-count items certifies fluency the learner has not actually generalised.
- Concept-specific deviation: keep at least one subtraction item and one three-or-more-term multiplication item permanently in rotation; MC-2 and MC-3 both decay faster than the addition-only procedure, because ordinary practice sets skew toward addition and two-term products by default.
- Interleaving partners: `math.alg.like-terms` (the collection step this concept scales up, and the concept most likely to silently erode without joint review) and `math.alg.polynomial` (degree and standard-form vocabulary this concept's every claim depends on).

## Transfer Connections
- **Near**: `math.alg.polynomial-division` (this concept's own unlock — division inverts multiplication and needs fluent multiplication to verify a division's result), `math.alg.factoring` (factoring is this concept run in reverse: given a product, recover the factors).
- **Far**: matrix addition and multiplication, where the same "combine matching positions" versus "every element times every element, then sum by position" structure reappears with a different notation and a genuinely non-commutative multiplication.
- **Real-world**: combining and scaling multi-part cost or quantity formulas (e.g. total material cost as a function of a variable dimension) — any formula built from several labelled components that must first be expanded before being combined with another such formula.
- **Expert transfer**: the general principle that a multi-part operation applied to a compound object must touch every part of that object, exactly once, in a traceable way — the same discipline underlying correct implementation of any distributive or "map over every element" computation.
