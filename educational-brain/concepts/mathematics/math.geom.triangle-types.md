## Learning Objective
Students will classify triangles by side lengths (scalene, isosceles, equilateral) and by angle measures (acute, right, obtuse), recognizing that these are two independent classification systems that apply simultaneously to every triangle.

## Core Understanding
A triangle can be classified in two distinct ways:
1. **By sides**: comparing the lengths of all three sides
   - Scalene: all three sides have different lengths
   - Isosceles: exactly two sides are equal in length
   - Equilateral: all three sides are equal in length
2. **By angles**: comparing the measures of all three interior angles
   - Acute: all three angles are less than 90°
   - Right: one angle equals exactly 90°
   - Obtuse: one angle is greater than 90°

These classification systems are independent — a right triangle can be scalene or isosceles; an equilateral triangle is always acute (all angles exactly 60°).

## Mental Models
1. **The side-measurement toolkit**: physically measuring (or imagining measuring) the side lengths; laying sides on top of each other; the "tick mark" representation used in geometry diagrams
2. **The angle-measurement toolkit**: using a protractor, or visualizing angle openings; recognizing the right angle (90°) as the reference marker; understanding "wider opening = bigger angle"
3. **The 2×3 grid**: visualizing a mental 2×3 classification table (by-sides vs. by-angles categories), reinforcing that each triangle occupies one cell in each dimension

## Why Students Fail
1. **Conflating size with classification**: drawing a large acute triangle and a small equilateral triangle, then assuming the larger one is "more of a triangle" or deserves a different classification
2. **Anchor to prototypes**: early teaching typically shows acute isosceles or acute scalene triangles; students develop a visual expectation ("triangles have a peak at the top, two long equal sides, and pointy angles") and reject right/obtuse triangles as "not really triangles" or "broken"
3. **Over-applying one system**: learning "isosceles has two equal sides" but then forgetting that *every* isosceles triangle also has a by-angles classification — some are acute, some are right, some are obtuse
4. **Orientation bias**: rotating a triangle 45° confuses students who learned to recognize it in a standard orientation; they re-count sides or re-estimate angles based on how it looks tilted, not on invariant properties

## Misconceptions
**MC-1: VISUAL-APPEARANCE-OVERRIDES-MEASUREMENT (Type 2, perceptual intuition)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "It looks like it has two equal sides" (without measuring); "That's a pointy triangle, so it must be acute"
- **Mechanism**: Students rely on visual impression rather than actual measurement or angle checking. A 5.0–5.05–5.0 cm triangle drawn to appear scalene visually tricks them; a right triangle rotated 45° doesn't "look right"
- **Evidence signature**: Correct classifications when triangles are drawn in textbook-standard orientations; incorrect when the same triangle is redrawn rotated, scaled differently, or drawn imprecisely
- **Repair path**: Measure, then classify; always check all three sides/angles, not just visual impression

**MC-2: PROTOTYPE-BIAS (Type 5, instruction-induced)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "Real triangles are pointy at the top"; "That's not a triangle, the top doesn't have a point"; "A right triangle is too flat"
- **Mechanism**: Early examples (typically scalene acute triangles with one vertex pointing upward) become the prototype. Right triangles are rarely shown in the first teaching; obtuse triangles appear unfamiliar
- **Evidence signature**: Accepts acute triangles readily; hesitates or rejects right/obtuse triangles, even when told they are valid; may say "that's a triangle, but a *weird* one"
- **Repair path**: Show diverse triangle types early; explicitly teach that obtuse/right are equally valid; use multiple orientations for the same triangle

**MC-3: ONE-CLASSIFICATION-SYSTEM-AT-A-TIME (Type 1, overgeneralization)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "Is it isosceles or acute?" (as if these are alternatives); "I classified it by sides, so I don't need to classify it by angles"; "All equilateral triangles are the same type"
- **Mechanism**: Students treat by-sides and by-angles classifications as redundant or mutually exclusive, not recognizing them as two independent lenses
- **Evidence signature**: Correctly classifies by sides OR by angles when asked for one; fails to provide both; may invent a single "name" like "acute-isosceles" and resist breaking it into separate components
- **Repair path**: Explicitly label both systems; teach the 2×3 grid; show examples of the same triangle classified both ways

**MC-4: RIGHT-ANGLE-AS-SPECIAL-MARKER-ERASES-SIDE-CLASSIFICATION (Type 5, instruction-induced)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "That's a right triangle" (stopping there, not mentioning whether the legs are equal); "If it has a right angle, it's not isosceles"
- **Mechanism**: The right angle is visually salient; students fixate on it as "the" defining feature and skip the independent side-classification check
- **Evidence signature**: Classifies a right triangle with two equal legs as only "right," omitting "isosceles"; cannot name both dimensions of a 45°-45°-90° triangle without cueing
- **Repair path**: Template-prompt: "Is it scalene, isosceles, or equilateral? *And separately*, is it acute, right, or obtuse?"

## Analogies
1. **Vehicle classification**: A vehicle is classified both by engine type (electric, gas, diesel) *and* by body style (sedan, SUV, truck) — these are independent systems, and a diesel electric hybrid SUV fits multiple categories
2. **Clothing labels**: A garment has both a material (cotton, wool, polyester) and a style (shirt, pants, jacket) — you describe both, not one at the expense of the other

## Demonstrations
1. **The side-tick-mark exploration**: Display three triangles with tick marks indicating equal sides. Have students identify which sides are equal without measuring; then verify by measurement or overlay. Repeat with rotations/rescalings of the same triangle to show tick marks are invariant under transformation
2. **The angle-benchmark exploration**: Show a right angle (90°) first in isolation; mark it as the reference. Then show triangles where all angles are smaller than 90° (acute), where one angle equals 90° (right), and where one angle is larger than 90° (obtuse). Use a protractor or angle-arc markings
3. **The 2×3 grid live build**: Physically draw or project a 2×3 table on the board. Gradually place 6 diverse triangles into the cells: one in each position (e.g., acute-scalene, acute-isosceles, acute-equilateral, right-scalene, right-isosceles, obtuse-scalene). Discuss why some cells are empty or rare (e.g., no right-equilateral, no obtuse-equilateral)

## Discovery Questions
1. Can you find a triangle with two equal sides? How do you know they're equal without using a ruler?
2. If I rotate this triangle, do its side lengths change? Do the angle measures change?
3. This triangle has two equal sides. Can you classify it by angles too? Does every triangle have *both* a by-sides *and* a by-angles classification?
4. Can a triangle be both isosceles *and* obtuse? Can you draw one?
5. What's the smallest number of triangles you'd need to show all six possible "types" (all three by-sides categories paired with all three by-angles categories)? Why are some pairs impossible?

## Teaching Sequence
1. **Activation (5–7 min)**: Show a single triangle in standard orientation (acute isosceles). Ask students to describe it in as many ways as possible. Record their words (pointy, balanced, three sides, etc.). Gently guide: "Some of you are talking about side lengths, some about angles. Let's separate these into two systems"
2. **Side-classification anchor (10–12 min)**: Introduce the three by-sides categories using a physical toolkit (sticks of different lengths, or a diagram with clear tick marks). Define scalene, isosceles, equilateral. Have students physically build or identify examples. *Rotation test*: rotate a triangle; confirm side lengths are invariant
3. **Angle-classification anchor (10–12 min)**: Introduce the three by-angles categories using angle-benchmark markings (right angle first, then acute/obtuse). Measure a few triangles with a protractor or angle-arc diagrams. Define acute, right, obtuse
4. **Independence discovery (5–10 min)**: Ask: "Is an equilateral triangle acute or obtuse?" (Answer: acute — all angles are 60°). "Can an isosceles triangle be right?" (Yes, the 45°-45°-90° triangle). Build the 2×3 grid together. Place example triangles in each cell as students identify them
5. **Guided practice (10–15 min)**: Show diverse triangles in varied orientations. For each: "First, classify by sides. Then classify by angles." Require both answers. Correct common errors in real time (visual bias, prototype confusion, one-system-only)
6. **Summary and connection (5 min)**: Review: every triangle has *two* independent classifications. Neither erases the other; both are always true

## Tutor Actions
- **Elicit side/angle classification separately**: "I hear you saying it's a right triangle. That's the angle classification. Now, how would you describe the sides?"
- **Measure before confirming**: When a student claims two sides are equal, ask them to measure first, then confirm or revise
- **Probe orientation invariance**: "If I rotate this triangle 90°, are the side lengths still equal? Why?" (Repair: "The triangle itself doesn't change when we rotate it; only our view does")
- **Correct misconception MC-2 early and explicitly**: "Right triangles and obtuse triangles are just as much *triangles* as these pointy ones. In fact, a right triangle is very important in math — don't skip it"
- **Reinforce independence**: "Let's make a prediction: can we have a *scalene* right triangle? What would that look like?" (Build it, measure, verify)

## Voice Teaching Notes
- **Emphasis markers**: Stress "exactly two equal" for isosceles (not "at least two," which would include equilateral); stress "less than 90°" for acute (not "small" or "pointy")
- **Hesitation-recovery moves**: If a student hesitates on an unfamiliar triangle orientation, do NOT skip to confirmation — ask them to *measure first*. The measurement is the recovery
- **Load-bearing sentences**:
  - "Every triangle has *both* a by-sides name *and* a by-angles name"
  - "Rotating a triangle doesn't change its side lengths or angles — only our viewpoint"
  - "A right triangle can be scalene or isosceles, but not equilateral" (stops false generalizations)
- **Register notes**: Use "equal" and "the same length" interchangeably early; introduce "congruent" and "congruent sides" only after this concept is solid, in a later geometry concept (e.g., math.geom.congruent-triangles)

## Assessment Signals
- **Correct classification by sides + correct classification by angles in a single turn** = AUTOMATIC (student solidly has both systems)
- **Correct side classification but hesitates on angles** = FRAGILE-BY-ANGLES (may have MC-2 or MC-3; reinforce angle measurement)
- **Correct angle classification but "forgets" side classification when prompted for both** = FRAGILE-BY-INDEPENDENCE (MC-3; re-teach the 2×3 grid)
- **Visual-appearance-based error despite correct earlier answer on a rotated version** = MC-1 active (perceptual intuition; repair with measurement discipline)
- **Rejects a right/obtuse triangle as "not really" a triangle** = MC-2 (prototype bias; show diverse examples; normalize obtuse/right as equally valid)

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
**Immediate tier (right-now, same turn)**:
1. If MC-1 (visual bias): "Let's measure instead of looking. Which two sides are actually the same length?" Physically measure or mark with tick marks
2. If MC-2 (prototype bias): "Triangles come in many shapes. This *is* a triangle. It has three sides and three angles, just like the pointy ones we saw earlier"
3. If MC-3 (one-system-at-a-time): "I need *both* answers: what's the side classification *and* the angle classification?" Re-prompt with the 2×3 grid template

**Follow-up tier (consolidation)**:
- Return to MC-1/MC-2 errors in the next session with the same triangle rotated or rescaled; verify that classification doesn't change
- Explicitly assign a probe asking for *both* classifications of a single triangle; require both answers before advancing
- Use the 2×3 grid template on every follow-up probe until the student confidently fills it in without hesitation

## Memory Hooks
- **The 2×3 grid visualization**: Write or draw 6 cells (3 by-sides categories × 2 by-angles categories shown, omitting impossible cell). Each cell shows one example triangle. This single diagram encodes the entire concept structure
- **Tick marks = equal sides**: Visual tick marks on sides are the learner's persistent anchor for side classification across contexts
- **Right angle as reference**: The 90° right angle is the pivot point for angle classification; acute is "smaller than 90," obtuse is "larger than 90"

## Transfer Connections
1. **Acute triangles → later trigonometry**: Right triangles are the foundation for sine/cosine/tangent; students with MC-2 (prototype bias against right triangles) will struggle later
2. **Isosceles triangles → symmetry properties**: Once solid on "two equal sides," students can learn that isosceles triangles have a line of symmetry, perpendicular bisector properties, and angle-bisector theorems (e.g., math.geom.isosceles-triangle-theorem, if it exists)
3. **Equilateral triangles → regular polygons**: Equilateral triangles are the simplest regular polygon; the transfer to other regular polygons (hexagons, etc.) flows smoothly if side/angle independence is solid
4. **By-angles classification → triangle-angle-sum**: Knowing that acute/right/obtuse triangles exist motivates the discovery that "all triangle angles add to 180°" — students can verify this across all three angle types

## Cross-Subject Connections
- **Physics (force vectors)**: Right triangles are essential for resolving forces into components; prototype bias (MC-2) becomes a concrete liability
- **Engineering (structural design)**: Triangles with different angle/side profiles have different load-bearing properties; classification is the first step toward understanding structural stability
