## Learning Objective
Define $R[x]$ — polynomials with coefficients drawn from an ARBITRARY ring $R$ (not just
$\mathbb R$, generalizing `math.alg.polynomial`'s real-coefficient polynomials) — verifying it
satisfies the ring axioms R1-R5 (reusing `math.abst.ring-theory`'s own axiom-checking procedure
directly); state that $F[x]$ for a FIELD $F$ is a EUCLIDEAN DOMAIN admitting a polynomial DIVISION
ALGORITHM, and recognize this depends critically on $F$ being a field; and recognize, at
orientation level, that an IRREDUCIBLE polynomial in $F[x]$ generates a MAXIMAL ideal (reusing
`math.abst.ideal`'s own ideal concept directly), illustrated by $\langle x^2+1\rangle$ in
$\mathbb R[x]$ producing the quotient ring $\mathbb C$.

## Core Understanding
`math.alg.polynomial` studied polynomials $a_nx^n+\cdots+a_0$ with REAL coefficients. $R[x]$
generalizes this directly: coefficients $a_i$ are drawn from ANY ring $R$ (not necessarily
commutative, not necessarily containing multiplicative inverses), with addition and multiplication
defined the same way — add/multiply coefficients of matching-degree terms, using $R$'s own $+$ and
$\cdot$. Verifying $R[x]$ satisfies the ring axioms R1-R5 uses the EXACT SAME checking procedure
`math.abst.ring-theory` already established, applied to this richer object.

When the coefficient ring is specifically a FIELD $F$, $F[x]$ supports a POLYNOMIAL DIVISION
ALGORITHM: for any $f,g\in F[x]$ with $g\ne0$, there exist unique $q,r\in F[x]$ with $f=qg+r$ and
$\deg(r)<\deg(g)$ — exactly like integer long division, but for polynomials. This process needs, at
each step, to DIVIDE by the divisor's leading coefficient — an operation requiring that coefficient
to have a multiplicative inverse. If $R$ is NOT a field, this division can genuinely FAIL to
produce a polynomial quotient with coefficients still in $R$ — the algorithm is not merely harder
over a general ring, it can be entirely unavailable. Notably, EVEN when $F$ IS a field, $F[x]$
itself is NEVER a field: $x$ has no multiplicative inverse in $F[x]$, since for any nonzero
$p(x)\in F[x]$, $\deg(x\cdot p(x))=\deg(x)+\deg(p(x))\ge1$, while $\deg(1)=0$ — no polynomial can
multiply $x$ back to $1$.

An IRREDUCIBLE polynomial in $F[x]$ (one that cannot be factored into two lower-degree polynomials
over $F$) generates a MAXIMAL ideal — an ideal (reusing `math.abst.ideal`'s own definition directly)
not properly contained in any ideal other than $F[x]$ itself. A maximal ideal's quotient ring is
always a FIELD, so factoring $F[x]$ by an irreducible polynomial's ideal constructively BUILDS a new
field. A REDUCIBLE polynomial's ideal, by contrast, is never maximal — it sits properly inside a
larger ideal generated by one of its own factors.

## Mental Models
- **"$R[x]$ just swaps in ANY ring $R$ for the coefficients — check the ring axioms the exact same
  way you already know."**
- **"A polynomial ring is NEVER a field, no matter how nice $R$ is — $x$ itself is a permanent
  obstruction."**
- **"The division algorithm needs to INVERT the divisor's leading coefficient — that's only
  guaranteed possible over a field."**
- **"Irreducible generates MAXIMAL, not merely prime — the quotient is a genuine field."**

## Why Students Fail

### MC-1: POLYNOMIAL-RING-ASSUMED-TO-INHERIT-FIELD-PROPERTY
- **Surface form**: believes $F[x]$ is automatically a field whenever $F$ is a field, missing that
  $x$ itself never has a multiplicative inverse in any polynomial ring.
- **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization (Blueprint's own declared Foundational severity, here
  attributed to a natural but false assumption that a "generalization" of a nice structure ($F$
  being a field) automatically inherits every nice property — while $R[x]$ does automatically
  inherit ring-hood from $R$'s own ring axioms, field-hood is genuinely NOT automatic, and nothing
  about the ring-axiom-inheritance pattern signals this exception without the explicit degree
  argument being worked through).
- **Repair**: re-walk the degree-counting argument showing $x$ has no inverse in the specific
  $F[x]$ in question, re-anchoring on the permanent obstruction $x$ poses.

### MC-2: DIVISION-ALGORITHM-ASSUMED-TO-WORK-OVER-ANY-RING
- **Surface form**: believes the polynomial division algorithm works over any ring $R[x]$, missing
  that it specifically requires the coefficient ring to be a field, to invert leading coefficients.
- **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization (Blueprint's own declared Foundational severity, here
  attributed to ordinary integer long division, encountered first and never requiring any special
  condition on the numbers involved, being over-generalized into the polynomial setting without
  recognizing that the polynomial algorithm's specific reliance on inverting a leading coefficient
  introduces a genuinely new requirement absent from integer division). Since the algorithm's
  MECHANICS look identical to familiar long division, the extra field requirement is easy to miss
  until a concrete failure is encountered.
- **Repair**: re-attempt the specific failing division in question over the non-field ring,
  identifying exactly which step fails (inverting a non-invertible leading coefficient) and why.

### MC-3: IRREDUCIBLE-IDEAL-ASSUMED-ONLY-PRIME-NOT-MAXIMAL
- **Surface form**: treats "irreducible polynomial generates a prime ideal" as the complete fact,
  missing that in $F[x]$ specifically, irreducible generates the STRONGER property of a maximal
  ideal.
- **Birth type**: Type 5, instruction-induced (Blueprint's own declared Moderate severity, here
  attributed to "prime" being introduced earlier and more generally as ideal theory's default
  vocabulary for factorization-related properties, so the STRONGER, more specific fact — that in
  $F[x]$ irreducible reaches all the way to maximal, not merely prime — is easy to under-claim
  without this concept's own explicit treatment).
- **Repair**: re-walk the specific irreducible polynomial's quotient-ring computation in question,
  confirming the quotient is a genuine FIELD (the defining property of maximal, strictly stronger
  than merely prime).

## Analogies
- **"$R[x]$ is like translating a familiar language's number system into a new alphabet — the
  grammar rules (ring axioms) transfer automatically, but a special feature like 'every word has an
  opposite' (multiplicative inverses) does NOT transfer just because the underlying alphabet
  supports it elsewhere."**
- **Anti-analogy**: the division algorithm is NOT "the same long division, just with polynomials
  instead of numbers" in full generality — it carries a hidden requirement (a field of
  coefficients) that plain integer division never needed.

## Demonstrations
- **Demonstration 1 (targets MC-1)**: in $\mathbb Q[x]$ ($\mathbb Q$ IS a field), does $x$ have an
  inverse? Some $p(x)$ with $x\cdot p(x)=1$ would need $\deg(x\cdot p(x))\ge1$ while $\deg(1)=0$ —
  impossible. $\mathbb Q[x]$ is NOT a field, despite $\mathbb Q$ being one.
- **Demonstration 2 (targets MC-2)**: dividing $x^2+3x+2$ by $x+1$ in $\mathbb Q[x]$ works cleanly:
  $x^2+3x+2=(x+1)(x+2)+0$. Dividing $x^2$ by $2x$ in $\mathbb Z[x]$ ($\mathbb Z$ is NOT a field)
  would need quotient $x/2$ — not an integer-coefficient polynomial at all; the algorithm genuinely
  fails, precisely because $2$ has no inverse in $\mathbb Z$.
- **Demonstration 3 (targets MC-3)**: in $\mathbb R[x]$, $x^2+1$ is irreducible (no real roots); the
  ideal $\langle x^2+1\rangle$ is maximal, and $\mathbb R[x]/\langle x^2+1\rangle\cong\mathbb C$ — a
  genuine FIELD, confirming maximality, not merely primality. Contrast with $x^2-1=(x-1)(x+1)$,
  reducible: $\langle x^2-1\rangle$ sits properly inside $\langle x-1\rangle$, NOT maximal.

## Discovery Questions
1. "If $F$ is a field, is $F[x]$ automatically a field too?"
2. "Does the polynomial division algorithm work over any ring $R[x]$, or does it need something
   special about $R$?"
3. "Does an irreducible polynomial's ideal being 'maximal' mean something stronger than just
   'prime'?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. **Anchor**: connect to `math.alg.polynomial`'s real-coefficient polynomials and to
   `math.abst.ring-theory`'s own axiom-checking procedure, framing $R[x]$ as the direct
   generalization checked the same familiar way.
2. **Conflict evidence**: the $x$-has-no-inverse-in-$\mathbb Q[x]$ degree argument and the
   $x^2\div2x$ failed-division example, breaking MC-1 and MC-2 directly.
3. **Contrast pair**: the clean $\mathbb Q[x]$ division against the failed $\mathbb Z[x]$ division,
   further isolating MC-2; the irreducible $x^2+1$ (maximal, quotient $=\mathbb C$) against the
   reducible $x^2-1$ (not maximal), isolating MC-3.
4. **Mastery gate**: require a correct ring-axiom verification for $R[x]$ over a non-field $R$, a
   correct explanation of why $x$ has no inverse, a correct diagnosis of a failed division, and a
   correct irreducible-vs-reducible maximality classification, at the Blueprint's own stated MAMR
   of 4/5.

## Tutor Actions
- Never accept "$F[x]$ is a field because $F$ is" without requiring the degree argument for $x$'s
  non-invertibility to be stated.
- When a polynomial division is attempted over a non-field coefficient ring, require the learner to
  identify exactly which step requires inverting a non-invertible coefficient.

## Voice Teaching Notes
- Say "does every nice property of $F$ automatically transfer to $F[x]$, or is field-hood a special
  exception?" whenever inheritance is assumed too broadly.
- When a maximal-ideal claim is made, ask "is the quotient ring genuinely a FIELD, or only an
  integral domain?"
