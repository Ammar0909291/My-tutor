## Learning Objective

The learner can: compute sums in their head using flexible strategies —
making tens (decomposing an addend to complete the nearest ten),
decomposing numbers into convenient parts, and using known number
bonds — rather than mentally replicating the written column algorithm;
flexibly CHOOSE whichever decomposition makes a specific calculation
easiest, rather than applying one fixed decomposition rule
universally; and recognize that mental strategies, applied correctly,
are exactly as accurate as the written algorithm.

## Core Understanding

Mental addition is a family of FLEXIBLE strategies for computing sums
without writing anything down — genuinely different in character from
mentally simulating the written column algorithm (which requires
tracking carries and columns in working memory, a much heavier load).
Three core strategies: MAKING TENS (e.g., 8+7: take 2 from the 7 to
complete 8 into 10, leaving 5, so 10+5=15); DECOMPOSING NUMBERS (e.g.,
47+25: split into 47+20=67, then 67+5=72); and NUMBER BONDS (using
memorized pairs that sum to a round number, like knowing 6+4=10
instantly, to speed up larger calculations). Crucially, there is no
single "correct" decomposition — the SAME sum can be computed via
different equally-valid mental routes, and choosing whichever route is
easiest for the specific numbers involved is the actual skill, not
memorizing one fixed procedure. Mental strategies, executed correctly,
produce EXACTLY the same accurate result as the written algorithm —
they are alternative routes to the identical sum, not an approximate
shortcut.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "mental addition means doing the written
  algorithm in my head, including tracking carries without writing
  them down"**: the learner attempts to mentally simulate column
  addition with carrying, a heavy working-memory load, rather than
  using genuinely different flexible strategies. Shelf-life warning:
  this model can work for small, single-carry sums but breaks down
  quickly as numbers grow, since tracking multiple carries without
  writing anything down overloads working memory.
- **Intermediate model — "I use making-tens or decomposition
  strategies, but I always apply the same fixed split (e.g., always
  breaking off the ones digit) regardless of which numbers are
  actually involved"**: the learner has adopted a genuine mental
  strategy but treats it as a single rigid procedure rather than a
  flexible toolkit, missing that a DIFFERENT decomposition might be far
  easier for a specific pair of numbers. Upgrade trigger: being shown
  two different decompositions of the same sum and asked which is
  easier and why.
- **Advanced model — "mental addition is a flexible toolkit —
  making tens, decomposing, and number bonds — and I choose whichever
  strategy makes THIS SPECIFIC calculation easiest, recognizing all
  correctly-executed mental routes give the identical, exactly accurate
  answer as the written algorithm"**: the learner fluently selects
  and switches between strategies based on the specific numbers
  involved, and trusts mental results as fully accurate. Upgrade
  trigger: being asked to solve the same problem two different ways
  mentally and confirm both give the same answer.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner still attempting to mentally
  replicate the written algorithm's carrying (beginner model, MC-1)
  should not be pushed toward flexible strategy-selection
  (intermediate/advanced models) before at least one genuine mental
  strategy (making tens) is comfortably established as an alternative,
  not a mental rehearsal of the written procedure.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant, FOUNDATIONAL failure attempts to mentally simulate the
written column-addition algorithm, including tracking carries without
writing them down — since the written algorithm was taught first and
thoroughly practiced, it becomes the only known method, even though
holding multiple digits and carries in working memory without any
written support is far harder than using a genuinely different mental
strategy. A second failure, having learned one specific decomposition
strategy (e.g., always splitting off the ones digit), applies it as a
fixed, universal procedure rather than flexibly choosing whichever
decomposition is actually easiest for the specific numbers at hand —
missing that mental math's real strength is adaptability, not
memorizing one particular split. A third failure believes mental
addition is inherently less accurate or reliable than the written
algorithm, avoiding it even in situations where it would be
appropriate and equally accurate, based on an unfounded feeling that
unwritten work must be less trustworthy.

## Misconceptions

Authored directly via the birth-taxonomy diagnostic procedure — no
Blueprint exists for this concept:

### MC-1: MENTAL-MATH-REQUIRES-WRITTEN-ALGORITHM-IN-HEAD (Foundational; Type 5 — instruction-induced)
**Description**: Learner attempts to mentally replicate the exact
written column-addition procedure (tracking carries and columns
without writing them down) rather than using genuinely different,
lighter-weight mental strategies — the written algorithm, taught and
practiced first, becomes the default even when unsuited to mental
execution.
**Trigger condition**: asked to compute a sum "in your head," learner
visibly attempts to track column-by-column carries mentally, often
losing track partway through.
**Repair target**: introduce the making-tens strategy as a genuinely
DIFFERENT approach — for 8+7, complete 8 to 10 by taking 2 from the 7
(leaving 5), then 10+5=15 — no columns, no carrying, no written
algorithm simulated at all.
**MAMR**: FOUNDATIONAL — every later flexible-strategy skill depends
on first recognizing mental addition as a genuinely different family
of techniques, not a mental version of the written procedure.

### MC-2: DECOMPOSITION-ORDER-FIXED (Moderate; Type 1 — overgeneralization)
**Description**: Learner treats one specific decomposition (e.g.,
always splitting an addend into its tens and ones parts, regardless of
the numbers involved) as the single correct method, over-generalizing
from the first strategy demonstrated to a belief that mental addition
has one fixed procedure.
**Trigger condition**: given a sum where a different decomposition
would be far easier (e.g., 47+25, where making-tens on 47+3+22 is
smoother than a fixed tens/ones split), learner applies the same rigid
split regardless.
**Repair target**: present the SAME sum computed two different valid
ways (e.g., 47+25 as 47+20+5 versus as (47+3)+22) and ask which felt
easier — different numbers often make different decompositions easier,
and choosing flexibly is the actual skill.

### MC-3: MENTAL-ADDITION-LESS-ACCURATE-THAN-WRITTEN (Moderate; Type 2 — perceptual intuition)
**Description**: Learner believes mental strategies are inherently
less reliable than the written algorithm, avoiding mental computation
even when appropriate, based on a felt (but unfounded) sense that
unwritten work must be less trustworthy.
**Trigger condition**: given a mental-addition-appropriate problem,
learner insists on writing it out first "to be safe," even for numbers
well within comfortable mental range.
**Repair target**: solve the same problem both mentally and with the
written algorithm side by side, confirming the answers match exactly —
a correctly executed mental strategy is exactly as accurate as the
written algorithm, since both compute the identical sum via different
routes.

## Analogies

**Primary — two routes to the same destination (a general flexible-
strategy framing)**: driving to a destination, there might be a
highway route and a scenic back-roads route — both genuinely arrive at
the SAME destination, just via different paths, and neither is "more
correct" than the other. Mental addition strategies work identically:
making-tens and decomposition are different routes to the identical
sum the written algorithm also reaches — choosing whichever route
suits the specific trip (numbers) is the actual skill.

**Anti-analogy to retire**: "Mental addition means doing the same
column-by-column steps as the written method, just without paper."
This directly invites MC-1 by framing mental addition as a mental
rehearsal of the written procedure rather than a genuinely different,
lighter-weight family of strategies.

## Demonstrations

**Making-tens contrast (targets MC-1)**: 8+7 via the written algorithm
(mentally tracking: ones column 8+7=15, write 5, carry 1 — awkward
without paper) versus making-tens (take 2 from 7 to complete 8 into
10, leaving 5, so 10+5=15 — no carrying tracked at all, same answer).

**Flexible-decomposition contrast (targets MC-2)**: 47+25 computed as
47+20+5=72 (splitting the second addend by place value) versus as
(47+3)+22=72 (making 50 first, then adding the remaining 22) — both
valid, both give 72, and which feels easier can genuinely differ by
learner or by the specific numbers.

**Accuracy-equivalence check (targets MC-3)**: solve 63+29 mentally
(making tens: 63+27+2... or 63+30-1=92) and via the written algorithm
(63+29 column-by-column with one carry) side by side, confirming both
give 92 exactly.

## Discovery Questions

Present 8+7 and ask the learner to try computing it "without doing the
column method in your head" — the learner, prompted to find another
way, discovers the making-tens route (completing 8 to 10) themselves
or with light guidance, directly experiencing that a genuinely
different, lighter mental path exists. Recommendation: guided
discovery for finding an alternative mental route to a specific sum
(directly experiential from being asked to avoid the written-algorithm
simulation); direct instruction for explicitly naming and practicing
the making-tens and decomposition strategies as a general toolkit
(MC-2's repair), since recognizing these as a reusable, flexible family
of techniques (rather than one-off tricks) is not independently
rediscoverable without being named.

## Teaching Sequence

MC-1 (mental math requires written algorithm in head) is addressed
first, since establishing at least one genuinely different mental
strategy (making tens) as viable is the foundation the later
flexibility and accuracy-trust skills depend on. MC-2 (decomposition
order fixed) is addressed second, once a first strategy is secure,
broadening it into a flexible toolkit. MC-3 (mental addition less
accurate) is addressed last, as a confidence/trust issue rather than a
mechanical skill gap.

## Voice Teaching Notes

**Register**: Say "find a different way" rather than "do it in your
head" when introducing mental addition — the emphasis on a genuinely
different strategy (not a silent rehearsal of the written method) is
load-bearing and directly guards against MC-1.

**Wait-time**: After presenting 8+7 and asking for a non-written-
algorithm approach, give extended wait-time before revealing the
making-tens strategy — let the learner attempt to discover an
alternative route themselves.

**Load-bearing sentences**:
- "Mental addition isn't the written method without paper — it's a
  genuinely different, flexible set of shortcuts."
- "Choose whichever split makes THIS sum easiest — there's no single
  correct decomposition."

**Voice channel note**: See `foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals

### Gate 1 (MC-1 check)
Compute 9+6 mentally using the making-tens strategy, describing the
steps aloud. Pass: completes 9 to 10 by taking 1 from 6 (leaving 5),
then 10+5=15 — no column/carry simulation described.

### Gate 2 (MC-2 check)
Given 38+24, describe two different valid ways to decompose this sum
mentally, and state which feels easier. Pass: at least two genuinely
different valid decompositions offered (e.g., 38+20+4 vs. 38+2+22),
with a reasoned preference stated.

### Gate 3 (MC-3 check)
Compute 56+37 both mentally and via the written algorithm, and confirm
the results match. Pass: both methods give 93, confirmed to agree.

### Gate 4 (application)
Compute 68+45 mentally, choosing whichever strategy seems easiest, and
justify the choice. Pass: correct result (113) via a coherently
described flexible strategy.

### Transfer probe (independence mode — no cross_links)
A cashier mentally computes change for a $47 purchase paid with a $100
bill, without a calculator. Describe a mental strategy for computing
$100-$47 quickly (this connects addition and subtraction — recognizing
$47+$53=$100 via making-tens reasoning is often faster than direct
subtraction). Pass: correctly uses an addition-based mental strategy
(e.g., $47+3=$50, +$50=$100, so change is $53) rather than attempting
mental column subtraction.

**Mastery criterion**: correct performance on all 4 gates plus the
transfer probe, consistent with KG mastery_threshold 0.85.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Likeliest utterance: "I always mess up when I try to add numbers in my
head, I lose track of the carrying" — the concept-specific smaller
question: "what if you tried completing the first number to the
nearest ten first?" directly redirects the learner away from mentally
simulating the written algorithm (the source of the "losing track"
problem) toward a genuinely lighter-weight strategy suited to mental
execution.

## Memory Hooks

**Type**: procedural (executing making-tens, decomposition, and
number-bond strategies) + declarative (mental strategies are exactly
as accurate as the written algorithm; there is no single correct
decomposition). Review form: fresh mental-addition prompts of
increasing size, periodically paired with a "describe two ways to
solve this" flexibility check to keep MC-2's guard-rail active.
Interleaving partner: `math.arith.addition` (the operation these
