## Learning Objective
- The learner can define an improper integral with an infinite limit (Type I), $\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx$, as $\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^t f(x)\,dx$ — a proper definite integral up to a finite cutoff, followed by a limit at infinity.
- The learner can define an improper integral with a singularity (Type II), where the integrand is unbounded at some point in $[a,b]$, as a limit approaching that point from the proper-integral side — and correctly identify a singularity even when both limits of integration are finite.
- The learner can determine whether an improper integral CONVERGES (the defining limit exists and is finite) or DIVERGES (the limit fails to exist or is infinite), and correctly conclude a divergent improper integral has NO numeric value.

## Core Understanding
An improper integral is not computed by a new, independent set of rules — it is DEFINED as a limit of ordinary, proper integrals already fully understood. There are two structurally different reasons an integral can be "improper," both resolved by the same overall strategy: replace the problematic piece with a finite substitute, then take a limit. Type I arises when the interval of integration is unbounded: $\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx$ is defined as $\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^tf(x)\,dx$ — compute the proper integral up to a finite cutoff $t$, then let $t$ grow without bound. Type II arises when the INTEGRAND itself is unbounded somewhere in (or at the boundary of) an otherwise perfectly finite interval — a singularity — and is resolved by integrating properly from a safe distance away from the trouble point, then letting that safe distance shrink to zero. These two sources of impropriety are genuinely different (an unbounded interval versus an unbounded function value), and a singularity can occur with BOTH limits of integration perfectly finite, so impropriety is never signaled by infinity appearing explicitly in the limits alone. Whichever type, the resulting improper integral CONVERGES if its defining limit exists and is finite (that finite number IS the value), or DIVERGES if the limit fails to exist or is infinite — in which case the improper integral has NO numeric value at all, not an approximate or symbolic one. Writing down an improper integral's symbolic form is always possible regardless of outcome; whether the underlying limit actually settles to a finite number is a separate question requiring genuine computation, never assumed from the setup looking reasonable.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — "an improper integral is one with infinity in the limits of integration."** Impropriety is identified purely by the visible presence of $\infty$ as a bound, with no attention to whether the integrand itself might be unbounded somewhere finite. *Upgrade trigger*: encountering $\int_0^1\frac{1}{\sqrt x}\,dx$ — both limits finite — and being told this is still improper.
2. **Intermediate — "an improper integral is defined as a limit of a proper integral, whether the interval is infinite or the integrand blows up somewhere finite; compute the proper integral first, then take the limit."** Both sources of impropriety are now recognized, though convergence may still be assumed rather than actively verified. *Upgrade trigger*: computing $\int_1^\infty\frac1x\,dx$ and finding the limit grows without bound, contradicting an assumption that a valid-looking setup must produce a finite answer.
3. **Advanced — "whether an improper integral converges or diverges must be COMPUTED, never assumed — two superficially similar setups (like $1/x^2$ versus $1/x$, both integrated to infinity) can have opposite outcomes; and a singularity can hide anywhere in an interval, not just at an endpoint."** The convergence-must-be-checked discipline and the full-interval-scanning habit are both now explicit. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to compare an improper integral's convergence to a series' convergence, previewing the integral test for series.
4. **Expert — improper integrals generalize the definite integral's own domain of applicability, following the SAME "define via a limit of already-understood objects" strategy used whenever a mathematical operation is extended beyond its original, safely-behaved setting.** The learner recognizes this limit-based-extension pattern as reusable, not specific to integration. *Shelf life*: permanent.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant failure identifies impropriety purely by the visible presence of infinity as a limit of integration: since Type I improper integrals (the ones most commonly introduced first) always show $\infty$ explicitly, a learner comes to believe that is the DEFINING feature of impropriety, missing that an integrand can be unbounded (a singularity) even with both limits of integration perfectly finite (MC-1, IMPROPRIETY-ASSUMED-TO-REQUIRE-INFINITE-LIMITS) — a Type 1 overgeneralization of the pattern from typical introductory Type I examples, extended past the equally common Type II case. A second, distinct failure carries forward the universal experience of every PROPER integral computed so far — that following the rules correctly always produces a specific numeric answer — into the improper setting, assuming any improper integral set up with valid integration and limit rules must likewise evaluate to some specific finite number, missing that divergence (no value at all) is a genuine and common possible outcome requiring actual verification (MC-2, IMPROPER-INTEGRAL-ASSUMED-TO-ALWAYS-CONVERGE) — again a Type 1 overgeneralization, here of "a well-formed computation always produces a number" extended past the point where a limit can genuinely fail to settle. A third failure, once a learner has correctly learned to check for singularities, still checks only the ENDPOINTS of the interval, missing a singularity buried at an INTERIOR point, and proceeds with a direct antiderivative evaluation that silently ignores the blow-up (MC-3, SINGULARITY-LOCATION-OVERLOOKED-WITHIN-INTERVAL) — a Type 1 overgeneralization of the pattern from typical introductory Type II examples (where the singularity sits conveniently at an endpoint), extended past interior-singularity cases that require splitting the integral into two separate pieces.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 6 registry (MC-1, MC-2, MC-3) and its own repair actions B01–B03. **The Blueprint's Misconception Registry carries a Severity column (Foundational, Foundational, Moderate) but no explicit birth-type column**; all three classifications below are independently assigned here.

- **MC-1 — IMPROPRIETY-ASSUMED-TO-REQUIRE-INFINITE-LIMITS** (the Blueprint's own "Foundational" misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization. Type I examples (infinite limits, introduced first and most memorably) are extended into a universal rule for what "improper" means, missing that a singularity in the integrand — with both limits of integration perfectly finite — is an equally valid and equally common source.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: answering "yes" when asked whether an integral is only improper if infinity literally appears as one of its limits of integration.
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's A02 hook): "is an integral only 'improper' if infinity literally appears as one of its limits of integration?" — a "yes" answer reveals MC-1.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — re-walk Example 2's direct identification of the singularity at $x=0$, re-anchoring on checking whether the INTEGRAND stays bounded throughout the interval, not just whether infinity appears in the limits.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh integral with a finite-looking interval but an unbounded integrand somewhere within it, the learner correctly identifies it as improper without prompting.

- **MC-2 — IMPROPER-INTEGRAL-ASSUMED-TO-ALWAYS-CONVERGE** (the Blueprint's own "Foundational" misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization. Every proper integral computed prior to this concept produces a specific finite number by following the rules correctly; that universal experience is extended into the improper setting, where a limit can genuinely fail to settle to any finite value.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: reporting a specific finite number for $\int_1^\infty\frac1x\,dx$ without actually computing the defining limit, or answering "yes" when asked whether every improper integral must evaluate to some specific number.
  - **Detection probe** (Blueprint's A03 hook): "does every improper integral, since it's set up using perfectly valid integration and limit rules, necessarily evaluate to SOME specific number?" — a "yes" answer reveals MC-2.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — re-walk Example 3's direct computation showing $\lim_{t\to\infty}\ln t=\infty$, re-anchoring on "the limit must be COMPUTED — a reasonable-looking setup does not guarantee a finite answer."
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh improper integral, the learner computes the defining limit explicitly before reporting convergence or divergence, and correctly reports "no value" when the limit is infinite or fails to exist.

- **MC-3 — SINGULARITY-LOCATION-OVERLOOKED-WITHIN-INTERVAL** (the Blueprint's own "Moderate" severity misconception)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization. Typical introductory Type II examples place the singularity conveniently at an interval's endpoint; the habit of checking endpoints only is extended past interior-singularity cases, which require splitting the integral into two separately-treated pieces.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: attempting a direct antiderivative evaluation of $\int_{-1}^1\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$ without noticing or accounting for the blow-up at the interior point $x=0$.
  - **Detection probe**: give an integral like $\int_{-1}^1\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$, where the singularity is at the interior point $x=0$, and check whether a direct antiderivative evaluation is attempted without splitting at the singularity.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — re-anchor on scanning the ENTIRE interval, including interior points, for anywhere the integrand blows up; an interior singularity requires SPLITTING the integral into two pieces, each treated as its own improper integral approaching the singularity from its own side.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh integral with an interior singularity, the learner splits it at the singularity into two separately-evaluated improper integrals before attempting any computation.

## Analogies
- **Best — measuring the total paint needed to cover an infinitely long wall, one finite section at a time.** You cannot paint "the whole infinite wall" directly; instead, compute the paint needed up to a finite marker, then ask what happens as that marker moves further and further out — exactly the proper-integral-then-limit strategy for Type I.
- **Alternative — approaching a cliff edge cautiously, never stepping over it.** To measure something near a genuinely dangerous drop-off (a singularity), you measure from a safe distance away, then ask what happens as that safe distance shrinks toward zero — exactly the Type II strategy for a singularity.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "an improper integral is just a definite integral with a bigger number plugged in."** This phrasing licenses MC-2 directly, implying the computation always resolves to a specific value just as an ordinary definite integral does, when in fact the DEFINING limit itself might not settle to any finite number at all. Say "an improper integral is a LIMIT of definite integrals — and limits can fail to exist, so the outcome must be checked, never assumed" instead.

## Demonstrations
- **The finite-interval singularity.** For $\int_0^1\frac{1}{\sqrt x}\,dx$, note both limits of integration (0 and 1) are perfectly finite, then identify the integrand blowing up as $x\to0^+$. *Predict whether this integral is "improper" before checking the integrand's behavior, based on the limits alone.* Confirming it IS improper despite finite-looking limits is the demonstration for MC-1.
- **The convergent-versus-divergent contrast.** Compute $\int_1^\infty\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$ (converges to 1) beside $\int_1^\infty\frac1x\,dx$ (diverges to $\infty$) — two visually similar setups. *Predict, before computing either limit, whether both will converge to a finite value.* Getting one convergent and one divergent result is the demonstration for MC-2.
- **The interior-singularity split.** For $\int_{-1}^1\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$, attempt a direct antiderivative evaluation first (an incorrect shortcut), then identify the blow-up at the interior point $x=0$ and split the integral into two separate improper integrals. *Predict, before checking, whether the interval's finite endpoints alone guarantee no hidden singularity inside.* Finding the interior blow-up is the demonstration for MC-3.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction is the argued call for the formal limit-based definitions themselves (a specific mathematical convention best presented explicitly), but the finite-interval-singularity recognition (MC-1) and the convergent-versus-divergent discrimination (MC-2) are both genuinely discoverable by direct trial.
1. **Need** — "Is $\int_0^1\frac{1}{\sqrt x}\,dx$ improper, even though both limits of integration are finite numbers?" Check the integrand's behavior near $x=0$ to find out.
2. **Playground** — try checking a couple more integrals with finite-looking limits for a hidden integrand blow-up.
3. **Invention** — "Why does $\int_1^\infty\frac{1}{x^2}\,dx$ converge while $\int_1^\infty\frac1x\,dx$ diverges, when both integrands look similar?" Let the learner connect it to how quickly each function shrinks toward zero.
4. **Collision** — confront a learner who assumed every improper integral converges with the direct computation $\lim_{t\to\infty}\ln t=\infty$.
5. **Formalisation** — state both definitions precisely: Type I as $\lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^tf(x)\,dx$; Type II as a limit approaching the singularity from the proper-integral side.
6. **Compression** — "Improper means a limit is doing the work — and limits can fail."

## Teaching Sequence
Type I (the proper-integral-then-limit strategy for an infinite interval, per the Blueprint's own A01) must be established FIRST, since it is the most straightforward instance of the "replace the problematic piece with a finite substitute, then take a limit" strategy this whole concept relies on. Type II (per the Blueprint's own A02, directly targeting MC-1) follows, using the SAME strategy applied to a structurally different source of impropriety — the direct evidence that a finite-looking interval can still be improper is most convincing once the general strategy is already familiar from Type I. The convergence-must-be-checked discipline (per the Blueprint's own A03, targeting MC-2) is placed LAST, once both types of impropriety are recognized, so the convergent-versus-divergent contrast can draw on genuine fluency with computing the defining limits rather than introducing the computation for the first time. The interior-singularity case (MC-3) is folded into ongoing practice as a refinement of the Type II recognition skill, since it is a narrower failure mode of the SAME skill rather than a separate conceptual stage. Turn-level scripts for A01–A04 are owned by the Blueprint's Component 5 and are not restated here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Worked Example** — the Type I proper-integral-then-limit computation (Example 1, $\int_1^\infty\frac1{x^2}\,dx$), shading the infinite region first and asking what the total area could even mean. First action; anchors the general strategy concretely.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "Is $\int_0^1\frac1{\sqrt x}\,dx$ improper, given both limits are finite numbers?" asked BEFORE checking the integrand's behavior. Surfaces MC-1 in one turn.
- **DO: Demonstration** — the convergent-versus-divergent contrast (Example 1 beside Example 3), computing both defining limits explicitly and finding opposite outcomes.
- **TEST-THINKING: Error Analysis** — "A student evaluated $\int_{-1}^1\frac1{x^2}\,dx$ using the antiderivative directly, without checking for a singularity inside the interval. What's wrong?" targets MC-3 directly.

## Assessment Signals
- **Declares an integral not improper solely because both limits of integration are finite, without checking the integrand for a singularity** — MC-1. Route to the finite-interval-singularity demonstration, on the exact integral in question.
- **Reports a specific finite value for an improper integral without computing the defining limit, or assumes convergence by default** — MC-2. Route to the convergent-versus-divergent contrast, on the exact integral in question.
- **Attempts a direct antiderivative evaluation across an interval containing an interior singularity, without splitting the integral** — MC-3. Route to the interior-singularity split, on the exact integral in question.
- **Correctly identifies both types of impropriety (unbounded interval or unbounded integrand, anywhere in the interval), computes the defining limit explicitly, and reports "no value" for a divergent case** — the intended target state.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance here is "both the limits of integration are just 0 and 1 — how can this be improper?" — a reasonable question given that "improper" is so often introduced via visible infinity symbols. The concept-specific smaller question returns to the integrand itself: **"As $x$ gets very close to 0, what happens to $\frac{1}{\sqrt x}$ — does it stay bounded, or does it grow without bound?"** The learner evaluates the function at a few small values and sees it growing arbitrarily large. Then return: "that's the source of the impropriety — not the LIMITS of integration, but the FUNCTION VALUE blowing up somewhere inside. The same limit-based treatment as before still applies, just approaching the trouble spot instead of approaching infinity." If the frustration is instead about assuming convergence, shrink to the bare check: **"Compute the limit. What number does it actually approach — or does it grow without bound?"** Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Memory Hooks
- Concept type: **procedure with an embedded verification-before-computation requirement** (identifying BOTH the source of impropriety, anywhere in the interval, and whether the resulting limit actually converges are conceptual understanding points, not mere computational steps, and must be actively checked, not assumed). Review by *requiring the learner to state, before computing anything, WHERE the impropriety comes from (an infinite interval, or a specific point where the integrand blows up) and WHERE that point sits (endpoint or interior)*, never accepting a correct final numeric answer alone as evidence of understanding, since a learner can occasionally reach a correct convergent value while having missed or mislocated the actual source of impropriety.
