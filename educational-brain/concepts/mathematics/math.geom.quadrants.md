# Quadrants (math.geom.quadrants)

- **Concept ID**: math.geom.quadrants

- **Mastery Threshold**: 0.95
- **Estimated Hours**: 1.0
- **KG Status**: active (NCERT Grade 6, Common Core Grade 6, Cambridge Primary)

## Learning Objective
Students will name the four quadrants of the coordinate plane in standard order (I, II, III, IV, counter-clockwise from upper-right), state the sign pattern of x and y coordinates in each quadrant, locate a point in the correct quadrant from its ordered pair, and determine the quadrant of a point without plotting it.

## Core Understanding
The coordinate plane is divided by the x-axis and y-axis into four quadrants, labelled with Roman numerals I through IV in counter-clockwise order starting from the upper-right region. Quadrant I: x > 0, y > 0 (both positive). Quadrant II: x < 0, y > 0 (x negative, y positive). Quadrant III: x < 0, y < 0 (both negative). Quadrant IV: x > 0, y < 0 (x positive, y negative). Points on the axes belong to no quadrant. The standard counter-clockwise labelling convention (starting at the upper-right and going left) is a convention, not a theorem, but it is universal and must be memorized as-is. The sign pattern is the operational key: any point's quadrant can be determined from its (x, y) signs without plotting.

## Mental Models
1. **The compass-rose analogy**: Quadrant I is "north-east" (both positive), Quadrant II is "north-west" (x negative, y positive), Quadrant III is "south-west" (both negative), Quadrant IV is "south-east" (x positive, y negative) — the geographic compass direction maps directly onto the sign pattern
2. **The sign table**: a 2×2 table with rows "x positive / x negative" and columns "y positive / y negative" — each cell is one quadrant; filling in the four Roman numerals in the correct cells is the definitive memory anchor
3. **The counter-clockwise walk**: starting at Quadrant I (upper-right), walk counter-clockwise around the plane — I → II → III → IV → back to I; the numbering always follows this path, never clockwise

## Why Students Fail
1. **Clockwise labelling**: numbering the quadrants I → II → III → IV clockwise (down-right, down-left, up-left, up-right) instead of the standard counter-clockwise; this is the single most common error
2. **Sign errors in Quadrants II and IV**: Quadrant II (x negative, y positive) and Quadrant IV (x positive, y negative) are the most commonly confused because their sign patterns are "mixed" — students swap them, believing Quadrant II is x positive
3. **Misplacing axes-points in quadrants**: calling a point on the x-axis or y-axis "in Quadrant I" or another quadrant, rather than recognising it is on an axis and not in any quadrant
4. **Forgetting the Roman numeral convention**: using Arabic numerals (1, 2, 3, 4) or starting the count at the wrong region

## Misconceptions
**MC-1: CLOCKWISE-LABELLING (Type 5, instruction-induced)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "Quadrant II is at the bottom right" (correct only under clockwise labelling, wrong under the standard convention)
- **Mechanism**: the clockwise direction is more culturally familiar (clocks, degree measurement often starts at 3 o'clock); absent explicit instruction on the counter-clockwise convention, students default to what feels natural
- **Evidence signature**: consistently places II in lower-right, III in lower-left, IV in upper-left, inverting all four
- **Repair path**: "Standard convention goes COUNTER-clockwise from the upper-right. Picture a counter-clockwise rotation: start at I (upper-right), go left to II (upper-left), continue down to III (lower-left), then right to IV (lower-right). Write the walk on your diagram."

**MC-2: QUADRANT-II-IV-SIGN-SWAP (Type 1, overgeneralization)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "The point (−3, 5) is in Quadrant IV because x is negative" — placing Quadrant IV signs incorrectly
- **Mechanism**: students remember "Quadrant I = both positive" and "Quadrant III = both negative" correctly, but confuse the two "mixed" quadrants (II and IV); they cannot reliably recall which quadrant has which mixed sign pattern without an anchor
- **Evidence signature**: correctly identifies Quadrant I and III cases; consistently swaps II and IV
- **Repair path**: "Quadrant II: go LEFT (x negative) and UP (y positive) — upper-left. Quadrant IV: go RIGHT (x positive) and DOWN (y negative) — lower-right. The direction of travel from the origin tells you the signs."

**MC-3: AXES-POINTS-PLACED-IN-QUADRANTS (Type 5, instruction-induced)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "The point (0, 5) is in Quadrant I because y is positive"
- **Mechanism**: the definition of "quadrant" is not strictly taught — students learn to associate positive y with Quadrant I and apply the rule to axis points, which technically lie on the boundary between quadrants and belong to none
- **Evidence signature**: consistently assigns axis-points to the quadrant of the nearest region
- **Repair path**: "A quadrant is one of the FOUR REGIONS inside the axes — the axes themselves are the boundaries, not the region. If x = 0 or y = 0, the point is ON an axis, not in any quadrant."

**MC-4: ARABIC-VS-ROMAN-NUMERAL-CONFUSION (Type 4, notation-induced)**
- **Characteristic phrase**: "Quadrant 2" (using 2 instead of II) or misidentifying "I, II, III, IV" as "1, 2, 3, 4" when reading standard labels
- **Mechanism**: Roman numerals are formally introduced but not deeply practised; under retrieval pressure, students revert to the more familiar Arabic system; when reading a standard diagram labelled I–IV, they may not recognise "IV" as "4"
- **Evidence signature**: uses Arabic numerals in solutions; cannot read a standard coordinate-plane diagram labelled with Roman numerals
- **Repair path**: "The standard labels are Roman numerals — I (one), II (two), III (three), IV (four). Write them each once with their equivalents, then practise reading a standard plane diagram."

## Analogies
1. **The four rooms of a house**: the x and y axes are the walls dividing the house into four rooms; the room in the upper-right corner is Room I (where you'd naturally go first); the labels go counter-clockwise around the house
2. **The plus-minus grid**: think of the four quadrants as cells in a "+/+" "+/−" "−/+" "−/−" grid — each cell's cell label (I, II, III, IV) is a name for that sign pattern

## Demonstrations
1. **Physical quadrant walk**: mark the x and y axes on the classroom floor with tape; students physically walk into each quadrant as called by its Roman numeral or sign pattern — kinesthetic reinforcement of the spatial layout
2. **Sign-pattern card drill**: four cards, each showing one sign pattern (++, −+, −−, +−); students hold up the correct Roman numeral card when a sign card is shown; then reverse (hold up the sign card for a named Roman numeral)

## Discovery Questions
1. In which quadrant is the point (4, −7)? How did you decide without plotting?
2. A point has a negative x-coordinate and a positive y-coordinate. Which quadrant is it in?
3. Is the point (0, −3) in any quadrant? Explain.
4. Name a point in each of the four quadrants using integer coordinates. Verify by plotting.
5. What is the sign of x in Quadrant III? What is the sign of y in Quadrant IV?

## Teaching Sequence
1. **Activation (2 min)**: Draw the coordinate plane with x and y axes; ask "how many regions do the axes create?" — establish the four regions visually before naming them
2. **Counter-clockwise convention (3 min)**: Label the four regions I, II, III, IV going counter-clockwise from the upper-right; state explicitly "the convention goes counter-clockwise from the upper-right"; address MC-1 by contrasting with the (wrong) clockwise labelling
3. **Sign patterns (4 min)**: Fill in the sign of x and y for each quadrant; draw the 2×2 sign table; confirm with one example point per quadrant
4. **Axis points (2 min)**: Plot (3, 0), (0, 5), (−2, 0), (0, −4); establish that these are on the axes, not in any quadrant — address MC-3
5. **Quick identification drill (5 min)**: 8 ordered pairs — students name the quadrant without plotting; check with sign-table; include one axis-point to keep MC-3 active

## Tutor Actions
- **Always ask "what is the sign of x? What is the sign of y?"** as the explicit two-step identification process — prevents the sign-swap errors of MC-2
- **Draw the sign table** on every session involving quadrants until the student can reproduce it from memory
- **Ask "is x zero or y zero?"** for every axis-point the student encounters — building the habit of checking for the no-quadrant case
- **Insist on Roman numerals** in all written answers from the start

## Voice Teaching Notes
- **Emphasis markers**: stress "COUNTER-clockwise, starting from upper-right" when introducing the labelling; stress "axes are boundaries — points ON axes are in no quadrant"
- **Hesitation-recovery moves**: if the student hesitates on a quadrant, ask "what is x? Positive or negative? What is y? Positive or negative? Now look at the sign table."
- **Load-bearing sentences**:
  - "Quadrant I: both positive; II: x negative, y positive; III: both negative; IV: x positive, y negative"
  - "Counter-clockwise from upper-right: I, II, III, IV"
  - "On an axis = no quadrant"
- **Register notes**: "quadrant" is the formal term; "region" is acceptable informally for activation; "room" for young learners

## Assessment Signals
- **Names the correct quadrant from ordered pair in all four cases, including axis-points** = AUTOMATIC
- **Consistently places II and IV in wrong positions** = MC-1 active (counter-clockwise walk; redraw the labelling)
- **Swaps II and IV sign patterns** = MC-2 active (direction-of-travel anchor: upper-left = II, lower-right = IV)
- **Places axis-point in a quadrant** = MC-3 active (boundary definition; "on the axis, not inside")
- **Uses Arabic numerals instead of Roman** = MC-4 active (Roman numeral practice drill)

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
**Immediate tier (right-now, same turn)**:
1. If MC-1 (clockwise labelling): "Put a finger on Quadrant I — upper-right. Now go counter-clockwise (the opposite of a clock): upper-left = II, lower-left = III, lower-right = IV. Counter-clockwise is the rule."
2. If MC-2 (II/IV swap): "Quadrant II is the upper-LEFT room — you went LEFT (negative x) and UP (positive y). Quadrant IV is the lower-RIGHT room — you went RIGHT (positive x) and DOWN (negative y). Upper-LEFT = II; lower-RIGHT = IV."
3. If MC-3 (axis-point placed in quadrant): "Is x = 0 or y = 0? Then the point is ON an axis — the axes are the walls between quadrants, not inside any room. No quadrant."
4. If MC-4 (Roman numeral confusion): "Write: I = 1, II = 2, III = 3, IV = 4. Now the standard plane always uses I–IV — never 1–4. Practise saying 'Quadrant II' (two I's) and 'Quadrant IV' (one I before V = 4)."

**Follow-up tier (consolidation)**:
- Rapid-fire drill: 20 ordered pairs (including 4 axis-points) in under 3 minutes — purely the sign-to-quadrant translation; no plotting needed; any error stops the drill for a one-sentence repair
- For MC-2: specifically drill pairs where one is in II and the nearby pair is in IV (e.g., (−3, 5) and (3, −5)) to force the discrimination

## Memory Hooks
- **"I: ++, II: −+, III: −−, IV: +−"**: the sign-pattern sequence in order (noticing the pattern: + → −, + → +, − → −, − → + flipping x alternately)
- **"Start upper-right, go counter-clockwise: I, II, III, IV"**: the labelling rule in one sentence
- **"On an axis = no quadrant"**: the axes-case rule

## Transfer Connections
1. **Coordinate plane** (`math.geom.coordinate-plane`): the quadrant vocabulary is a direct refinement of the coordinate-plane concept — the plane is established first, quadrants are the named sub-regions of it
2. **Slope and intercepts** (`math.geom.slope`, advanced): the sign of the slope depends on which quadrants the line passes through; a positive slope goes from III to I, a negative slope from II to IV — quadrant signs underlie slope interpretation
3. **Trigonometric functions** (advanced): the signs of sin and cos in each quadrant are determined by whether the y-coordinate and x-coordinate (respectively) are positive or negative on the unit circle — exactly the sign patterns memorised here

## Cross-Subject Connections
- **Physics (vectors)**: a vector in Quadrant II has a positive y-component and negative x-component — the sign pattern established here is the same pattern used to read vector components by direction
- **Data science (scatter plots)**: when analysing bivariate data, the four quadrants of a scatter plot centred at the mean represent combinations of "above/below mean in x" and "above/below mean in y" — the same structural logic as coordinate quadrants
