## Learning Objective
- The learner can state the formal definition $f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}\dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}$ and explain why the limit — not any single value of $h$ — is what makes it a derivative rather than an average rate.
- The learner can compute a derivative directly from the definition for a polynomial or simple rational function, by expanding, simplifying to cancel the $h$ in the denominator, and only THEN letting $h\to0$.
- The learner can state, correctly, that differentiability implies continuity but not the reverse, and can produce $|x|$ at $x=0$ as a standing counterexample to the converse.
- The learner can classify a point of non-differentiability as a corner, a cusp, a vertical tangent, or a discontinuity, from a description or graph of the function there.

## Core Understanding
`math.calc.derivative-intro` built the whole informal picture: a secant line's slope is an average rate of change; sliding the second point toward the first turns the secant into the tangent; the tangent's slope is the instantaneous rate. This concept's entire job is to make that picture COMPUTABLE. The formal definition $f'(x)=\lim_{h\to0}[f(x+h)-f(x)]/h$ is exactly the secant slope — $[f(x+h)-f(x)]/h$ is the rise over run between the points $x$ and $x+h$ — with the "sliding toward" step made precise as a limit. The reason the limit cannot be skipped, and $h$ cannot simply be set to $0$, is that setting $h=0$ directly gives $[f(x)-f(x)]/0 = 0/0$, an indeterminate form that carries no information at all; the entire computational skill of this concept is algebraic manipulation (expand, simplify, factor) that cancels the $h$ in the denominator BEFORE taking the limit, at which point substituting $h=0$ becomes legitimate. Differentiability and continuity are related but distinct: if a function has a well-defined tangent slope at a point, it cannot have a jump or hole there (a graph that tears apart has no single secant-slope limit to speak of), so differentiable implies continuous. But the converse fails — a function can be perfectly continuous (no gaps, drawable without lifting the pen) while still having no well-defined tangent slope at a point, because the secant slopes approaching from the left and from the right disagree (a corner, like $|x|$ at $0$), grow without bound (a vertical tangent), or oscillate without settling (a cusp).

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — plug into the formula.** The derivative is a recipe: write $f(x+h)$, write $f(x)$, subtract, divide by $h$, simplify, then "cross out the $h$'s that are left" and set $h=0$. *Upgrade trigger*: a function where the "cross out and set to zero" step isn't obviously algebra (e.g. $1/x$, which needs a common-denominator step first, not a simple factor-and-cancel). *Shelf life*: about one lesson — the moment a non-trivial algebra step is required, "plug in" alone stops working.
2. **Intermediate — the limit IS the point.** The difference quotient at any fixed $h\ne0$ is only an approximation; the derivative is what those approximations approach as $h\to0$, and no single value of $h$, however small, equals it. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to explain WHY differentiable functions must be continuous, which this model alone doesn't supply.
3. **Advanced — the derivative is what makes the tangent line the best LINEAR approximation.** Near $x$, $f(x+h)\approx f(x)+f'(x)h$, and this approximation's error shrinks faster than $h$ itself as $h\to0$ — differentiability is exactly the statement that such a linear approximation exists and is good enough, and continuity is the much weaker statement that $f(x+h)\to f(x)$ with no claim about the RATE of approach.
4. **Expert — differentiability is a regularity condition on a hierarchy, continuity is a weaker one.** Continuous ⊊ differentiable ⊊ continuously differentiable ⊊ ⋯, each a strictly stronger local smoothness condition than the last; $|x|$ separates the first two, and functions with a continuous but wildly oscillating derivative separate the second pair. *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the model that makes later distinctions (differentiable vs. $C^1$, analytic functions) feel like the same kind of question asked again.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant failure is algebraic, not conceptual: eight years of "evaluate the expression by substituting the number" collide with an expression that is genuinely $0/0$ at $h=0$, and the trained reflex is to substitute anyway and report the indeterminate result, or to stop the moment $0/0$ appears rather than recognizing it as a signal that more algebra (not less) is needed first (MC-3, DIRECT-SUBSTITUTION-INTO-QUOTIENT). A second failure is treating the difference quotient at some small but fixed $h$ (or the difference quotient's simplified symbolic form, before the limit is actually taken) as though it already were the derivative — the exact same "the pre-limit expression IS the answer" mechanism this campaign has already documented for `math.calc.limits`' own MC-1 and `math.calc.riemann-sums`' own MC-3 (MC-1, DIFFERENCE-QUOTIENT-IS-DERIVATIVE). The third failure is a logical-implication reversal dressed as common sense: "continuous" is informally described to learners as "smooth" or "no breaks," and a learner reasonably but wrongly concludes that "no breaks" already guarantees a well-defined tangent slope everywhere — missing that a graph can have no breaks at all and still have a sharp corner, an infinitely steep tangent, or an infinitely oscillating slope (MC-2, CONTINUITY-IMPLIES-DIFFERENTIABILITY).

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 2 registry (MC-1..MC-3). **The Blueprint's Misconception Registry carries no explicit birth-type column** (it uses the older Curriculum Production Pipeline document format, matching `math.calc.limits`, `math.calc.continuity`, and `math.calc.derivative-intro` — the 12th such gap found in this campaign); all three birth-type classifications below are independently assigned here, not Blueprint-adopted.

- **MC-1 — DIFFERENCE-QUOTIENT-IS-DERIVATIVE** (the Blueprint's own "Foundational Misconception")
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization. This is the identical mechanism already classified this way for `math.calc.limits`' MC-1 (LIMIT-IS-THE-FUNCTION-VALUE) and `math.calc.riemann-sums`' MC-3 (RIEMANN-SUM-IS-THE-INTEGRAL): a pre-limit finite quantity is treated as though it already equals the value the limit process produces. Here the finite quantity is the difference quotient at a fixed, nonzero $h$ (or its unsimplified symbolic form), mistaken for $f'(x)$ itself.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: computing $[f(x+h)-f(x)]/h$, simplifying it to some expression still containing $h$, and calling THAT the derivative — or evaluating it at one small numerical $h$ (say $h=0.01$) and reporting that number as $f'(x)$.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, from the Blueprint's A01 computation procedure): "Find $f'(2)$ for $f(x)=x^2$ using the definition." A learner holding MC-1 stops at $\dfrac{(2+h)^2-4}{h}=4+h$ and reports "$4+h$" or a single numeric approximation, never taking $h\to0$.
  - **Repair**: name the $h\to0$ step explicitly as the ONE step that turns an approximation into the exact answer; show $4+h$ evaluated at $h=1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001$ numerically approaching $4$, then state that the derivative IS the limit $4$, not any entry in that table — reusing the same "measurement improves, the true quantity is fixed" framing already used for `math.calc.riemann-sums`' MC-2/MC-3 repair.
  - **Verification of death**: given $f(x)=x^2+1$, the learner simplifies the difference quotient to $2x+h$ and explicitly states "now let $h\to0$" before writing $f'(x)=2x$, without being prompted to do so.

- **MC-2 — CONTINUITY-IMPLIES-DIFFERENTIABILITY**
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization of a true implication into its false converse — the same shape of error already classified this way for `math.calc.continuity-types`' MC-2 (LIMIT-EXISTS-MEANS-CONTINUOUS, there reversing "continuous ⟹ limit exists" into "limit exists ⟹ continuous"). Here the true implication "differentiable ⟹ continuous" is reversed into "continuous ⟹ differentiable."
  - **Characteristic phrase**: "the graph of $|x|$ doesn't have any gaps or breaks, so it must have a derivative everywhere."
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, from the Blueprint's A03 contrast): "Is $f(x)=|x|$ differentiable at $x=0$? It's continuous there, isn't it?"
  - **Repair**: Blueprint's A03 contrast pair — the smooth $x^2$ (differentiable everywhere, secant slopes from both sides agree and converge) directly against the corner $|x|$ at $0$ (continuous, yet the left-hand difference-quotient limit is $-1$ and the right-hand one is $+1$; they disagree, so no single two-sided limit exists, so $f'(0)$ does not exist). State the theorem precisely — differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous, proof sketch via the algebraic identity $f(x+h)-f(x) = \dfrac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}\cdot h$, whose right side $\to f'(x)\cdot0=0$ if the derivative exists — and name $|x|$ as the standing counterexample to the converse.
  - **Verification of death**: given a new corner example (e.g. $f(x)=|x-3|$ at $x=3$), the learner correctly identifies it as continuous but not differentiable there, and can state which of the two implications ("differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous" vs. its converse) is the true one.

- **MC-3 — DIRECT-SUBSTITUTION-INTO-QUOTIENT**
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralization of ordinary substitution-based evaluation (which works for continuous expressions everywhere else in a learner's prior experience) into a case genuinely producing an indeterminate $0/0$ form — the identical procedural-overreach pattern already classified this way for `math.calc.limit-laws`' MC-1 (QUOTIENT-LAW-APPLIED-DESPITE-ZERO-DENOMINATOR-LIMIT) and `math.calc.squeeze-theorem`'s MC-2.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: substituting $h=0$ directly into $[f(x+h)-f(x)]/h$, obtaining $0/0$, and either reporting "$0/0$" as the final answer or concluding "the derivative doesn't exist" without attempting to simplify first.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, from the Blueprint's A01 procedure): "Find $f'(x)$ for $f(x)=1/x$ using the definition." A learner holding MC-3 substitutes $h=0$ into the unsimplified quotient before combining the fractions in the numerator, hits $0/0$, and stops.
  - **Repair**: name $0/0$ explicitly as a SIGNAL that more algebra is needed, not a final answer — reuse the Blueprint's own A01 three-step procedure (substitute/expand $\to$ factor out $h$ $\to$ THEN take the limit) as a checklist, with $1/x$ worked fully: combine $\dfrac{1}{x+h}-\dfrac{1}{x}=\dfrac{x-(x+h)}{x(x+h)}=\dfrac{-h}{x(x+h)}$ FIRST, so that dividing by $h$ cancels it cleanly to $\dfrac{-1}{x(x+h)}$, which is safe to evaluate at $h=0$.
  - **Verification of death**: given a fresh rational function's difference quotient that lands on $0/0$ under naive substitution, the learner combines fractions or factors BEFORE taking the limit, without being told to.

## Analogies
- **Best — the speedometer.** An average speed over an hour (a secant slope) is a real, useful number, but it isn't "the speed you were going" at any one instant. The speedometer's instantaneous reading is what you get by shrinking that hour toward a single moment — exactly the $h\to0$ step, made physical and already familiar (the Blueprint's own primary representation).
- **Alternative — zooming into a graph.** Zoom into any smooth curve enough and it starts to look straight; the derivative is the slope of that straight line the zoomed-in picture converges to. Good for building the "linear approximation" advanced mental model; weak for explaining WHY the algebra needs a limit rather than a single small $h$.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "the derivative is just a really small slope."** This licenses MC-1 directly: it invites picking a very small $h$ and reporting that number, rather than recognizing the derivative as what an infinite sequence of ever-smaller-$h$ slopes converges to. Say "the derivative is the number those small-$h$ slopes are all trying to reach, never any one of them" instead.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "smooth means differentiable."** This is MC-2 wearing an everyday word. $|x|$ looks smooth enough to draw in one stroke, and drawability (continuity) is not the same claim as having one well-defined tangent direction everywhere (differentiability).

## Demonstrations
- **The numerical-limit table.** For $f(x)=x^2$ at $x=2$, compute $[f(2+h)-f(2)]/h$ at $h=1, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, -0.001$ side by side. *Predict the pattern first.* The values visibly close in on $4$ from both sides — the demonstration IS the limit, before any algebra is shown.
- **The corner collision.** Compute the LEFT-hand and RIGHT-hand difference-quotient limits for $f(x)=|x|$ at $x=0$ separately (getting $-1$ and $+1$). *Predict whether they'll agree first.* The disagreement is the demonstration that $f'(0)$ fails to exist, made concrete rather than asserted.
- **The $0/0$-is-a-signal rewrite.** Show a learner's own naive substitution into $[1/(x+h)-1/x]/h$ hitting $0/0$, then live-rewrite it via the combine-fractions-first step to $\dfrac{-1}{x(x+h)}$, safely evaluable at $h=0$. Running it on the learner's OWN stalled attempt, not a fresh example, is what makes MC-3's repair land.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction is the argued call for the CENTRAL formal definition itself (deriving $\lim_{h\to0}$ as the "right" formalization of "instantaneous rate" from first principles is not something a learner can be expected to reinvent), but guided discovery is used for the differentiability-vs-continuity DISTINCTION, which is genuinely discoverable from a picture.
1. **Need** — "Is $f(x)=x^2$ differentiable at every point? Is $f(x)=|x|$?" Compute both away from the suspicious point first — both look fine everywhere else.
2. **Playground** — at $x=0$ for $|x|$, compute the difference quotient using $h=0.1, 0.01$ from the right, and $h=-0.1,-0.01$ from the left, watching the two sequences settle at different values.
3. **Invention** — "What would it mean for a function to be continuous but still fail to have a single well-defined tangent slope at one point?" Let the learner propose an answer before naming "corner."
4. **Collision** — confront a learner who assumed "no gaps means differentiable everywhere" with the two disagreeing one-sided limits just computed.
5. **Formalisation** — differentiable $\Rightarrow$ continuous is a theorem (provable via the identity in MC-2's repair); the converse is false, with $|x|$ as the standing counterexample.
6. **Compression** — "Continuous means no tears. Differentiable means, additionally, no corners, no infinitely steep spots, and no infinite wiggling."

## Teaching Sequence
The algebraic computation (A01, this concept's central skill) must come BEFORE the differentiability-vs-continuity theorem (A03) — a learner who cannot yet reliably cancel $h$ out of a difference quotient has no working example to test the corner counterexample against, and the theorem becomes a fact to memorize rather than something demonstrated on cases the learner has already computed by hand. The $0/0$-is-a-signal framing (targeting MC-3) should be introduced the FIRST time a rational function's difference quotient is attempted, not retrofitted after a stalled attempt, since a learner's first encounter with genuine $0/0$ sets the template for every later one. The Blueprint's own A02 pattern-induction step (deriving $f'(x^n)=nx^{n-1}$ from computing $x, x^2, x^3, 1/x$ directly from the definition) should follow immediately after A01's polynomial computation is solid, since it is this concept's strongest evidence that the definition, however laborious, produces a genuine, generalizable PATTERN rather than a one-off answer per function — turn-level scripts for A01–A04 are owned by the Blueprint and not restated here.

## Tutor Actions
- **DO: Worked Example** — the numerical-limit table for $f(x)=x^2$ at $x=2$, run before any symbolic algebra. First action; anchors the "limit of approximations" picture concretely.
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — "Will the left-hand and right-hand difference-quotient limits for $|x|$ at $0$ agree?" asked BEFORE computing either. Surfaces MC-2 in one turn if the learner predicts "yes."
- **DO: Demonstration** — the $0/0$-is-a-signal rewrite, run on the learner's own stalled attempt at $1/x$'s difference quotient, per the repair above.
- **TEST-THINKING: Error Analysis** — "A student wrote $f'(2)=4+h$ for $f(x)=x^2$. What step did they skip?" targets MC-1 without needing a fresh worked example.
- **Does NOT fit: differentiation-rule shortcuts (power rule, product rule) before the definition is solid.** `math.calc.derivative-rules` owns those explicitly; introducing them early lets a learner bypass the definition entirely and never confront MC-1 or MC-3.
- **Does NOT fit: asserting "differentiable implies continuous, but not conversely" as a rule to memorize without the $|x|$ collision.** The theorem without the counterexample produces a slogan a learner can recite and still hold MC-2 underneath.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "we don't stop at $0/0$ — that means there's more algebra to do first, THEN we take the limit." Say it every single time $0/0$ appears, not just the first. Listen for a learner reading the difference quotient aloud and stopping their voice flat at "over $h$" without continuing to "as $h$ goes to zero" — that flattening is the tell that the limit clause is being treated as decorative rather than load-bearing (MC-1 in the making). Listen for confident, fast agreement when asked "so $|x|$ has a derivative everywhere, since it's continuous, right?" — fast confident agreement here, unlike a hesitant one, signals the CONVERSE (MC-2) is genuinely held as a rule, not a guess. Channel reality is owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Assessment Signals
- **Reports an expression still containing $h$, or a single numeric value at one fixed $h$, as "the derivative"** — MC-1. Route to the numerical-limit table, never to restating the definition's words.
- **Confidently affirms differentiability for a corner function based on continuity alone** — MC-2. Route to the left/right one-sided-limit computation on that exact function, not a lecture on the theorem.
- **Stops at $0/0$ and reports it as the answer, or concludes "no derivative exists" without attempting algebraic simplification** — MC-3. Check whether the learner attempted ANY algebra before substituting $h=0$; if none, the repair is procedural (the combine-first checklist), not conceptual.
- **Correctly simplifies to an expression in $x$ and $h$, then explicitly writes "$h\to0$" before the final answer** — the intended target state; do not rush past this explicit step even once fluent, since skipping the WRITTEN limit step is exactly how MC-1 regrows silently.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's A04 gate, MAMR 5/5 (⌈0.85×5⌉). The 4-item P77 set plus the P76 transfer probe (projectile motion, independence mode) must include at least one item requiring simplification through a genuine $0/0$ form before the limit — a gate made only of already-cancelled quotients certifies MC-1's shortcut rather than the full computation.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance here is "I don't get why we can't just plug in $h=0$" — a reasonable question about why ordinary algebra rules seem to be suspended. The concept-specific smaller question returns to the speedometer: **"If I tell you my average speed over the LAST HOUR, is that the same as my speed RIGHT NOW?"** The learner says no, correctly, on ground they already own from ordinary experience. Then connect: "plugging in $h=0$ is asking for the average rate over a time interval of ZERO LENGTH — and $[f(x)-f(x)]/0$ is $0/0$, which carries no information, exactly like asking 'what's your average speed over no time at all' carries none. We have to look at what the average speed APPROACHES as the interval shrinks, not jump straight to a zero-length interval." If the frustration is instead about the corner-function theorem feeling arbitrary, shrink to the bare comparison: **"Walk your finger along $x^2$ near zero, then along $|x|$ near zero. Does your finger's DIRECTION change smoothly, or does it suddenly reverse?"** Generic machinery is owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.
