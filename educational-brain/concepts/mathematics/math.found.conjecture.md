## Learning Objective

The learner can: define a conjecture as a statement believed true on
the basis of evidence or intuition but not yet proven or disproven;
correctly reject "extensive checking" as sufficient to promote a
conjecture to a theorem, directly extending `math.found.proof`'s own
proof-by-example lesson; recognize that a DISPROVEN conjecture (one
refuted by a single counterexample) is not a failure or waste, but a
genuine, often valuable, mathematical result; and distinguish a
conjecture from an axiom, which is assumed by choice rather than
believed pending proof.

## Core Understanding

A conjecture is a mathematical statement believed true on the basis of
evidence or intuition but not yet proven or disproven. Conjectures
occupy a genuinely distinct epistemic category from theorems: a
conjecture may be supported by extensive checking (many specific cases
verified), strong intuition, or partial results — but NONE of this
constitutes proof, directly extending `math.found.proof`'s own MC-1
(proof-by-example) to the level of what gets to be CALLED a theorem
versus a conjecture. A conjecture has exactly three possible fates: it
can eventually be PROVEN (becoming a theorem), DISPROVEN (refuted by a
single counterexample, becoming a known-false statement), or it can
remain OPEN indefinitely, neither proven nor disproven, sometimes for
centuries. A disproven conjecture is not a wasted or failed piece of
mathematics — finding the counterexample that refutes a conjecture is
itself a genuine mathematical achievement, often revealing exactly why
the conjectured pattern breaks down and pointing toward a more accurate
replacement conjecture. A conjecture is distinct from an axiom
(`math.found.axiom`): an axiom is ASSUMED by deliberate choice, as a
starting point requiring no justification within the system it defines;
a conjecture is BELIEVED, pending a proof that has not yet been found,
and is always, in principle, a candidate for either proof or refutation.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "a conjecture is basically a fact, just not fully
  written up yet"**: the learner treats a well-supported conjecture as
  functionally equivalent to a theorem, differing only in a technicality
  of paperwork. Shelf-life warning: this model fails the moment a
  conjecture that seemed extremely well-supported is eventually
  disproven by a single counterexample, which this model has no
  framework for.
- **Intermediate model — "a conjecture is genuinely uncertain until
  proven or disproven, no matter how much evidence supports it"**: the
  learner correctly withholds "theorem" status from unproven
  statements, but may still treat a disproven conjecture as a wasted
  effort rather than a genuine result. Upgrade trigger: being asked
  what value, if any, a disproven conjecture has.
- **Advanced model — "conjectures have three possible fates (proven,
  disproven, open), and disproof is itself valuable mathematics,
  distinct from an axiom's role as an unjustified starting assumption"**:
  the learner treats the conjecture/theorem boundary as absolute, values
  disproof as genuine progress, and cleanly distinguishes conjectures
  from axioms. Upgrade trigger: being asked to explain, precisely, how
  a conjecture differs from an axiom despite both being currently
  "unproven."
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner who still conflates well-
  supported conjectures with theorems (beginner model) should not be
  pushed into valuing disproof (advanced model) before the basic
  proven/unproven boundary is itself absolute and non-negotiable.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant failure — directly inheriting `math.found.proof`'s own
MC-1 (proof-by-example) at the level of what counts as established
mathematics — treats a conjecture supported by extensive checking or
strong intuition as functionally equivalent to a proven theorem,
missing that no amount of supporting evidence, however extensive,
substitutes for a completed proof; a single future counterexample can
still refute even a conjecture checked against millions of cases. A
second, independent failure treats a disproven conjecture as a wasted
or failed piece of mathematics, missing that finding the specific
counterexample that refutes a conjecture is itself a genuine
mathematical achievement, often more illuminating about the underlying
structure than continued unsuccessful attempts to prove the (false)
original claim would have been.

## Misconceptions

Applying the birth-taxonomy diagnostic procedure directly, since no
Blueprint exists yet.

**MC-1 — "A conjecture checked extensively is basically proven" (Type
1, overgeneralization — direct extension of `math.found.proof`'s own
MC-1, proof-by-example, to the terminology of what counts as
established)**
- *Why*: extensive checking (millions of verified cases) FEELS
  conclusive, and the psychological weight of that evidence is easy to
  mistake for the logical weight of a completed proof, especially when
  a conjecture has resisted disproof for a very long time.
- *Symptom*: describing a famously well-checked, long-standing
  conjecture as "basically true" or treating its eventual proof as a
  formality rather than a genuine, sometimes extremely difficult,
  outstanding achievement.
- *Detection probe*: ask the learner whether a conjecture checked
  against a billion cases, with no counterexample found, should be
  treated as proven.
- *Recovery*: "no amount of checking, however extensive, is a proof —
  a conjecture checked against a billion cases can still be disproven by
  the billion-and-first case. `math.found.proof`'s own lesson applies
  here exactly: 'consistent with the evidence so far' is never the
  same as 'proven for every case.'"
- *Verification*: the learner correctly labels an extensively-checked
  but unproven statement as a conjecture, not a theorem, without
  hesitation, regardless of how much evidence supports it.

**MC-2 — "A disproven conjecture was a waste — the mathematician was
just wrong" (Type 2, perceptual intuition — refutation feels like
failure rather than a genuine result)**
- *Why*: everyday intuition treats "being wrong" as an unproductive
  outcome, and this carries over into mathematics, where disproving a
  conjecture can feel like admitting the original idea was worthless
  rather than recognizing the disproof itself as a real, often valuable,
  mathematical contribution.
- *Symptom*: treating a famous disproven conjecture (or the
  mathematician who proposed it) dismissively, rather than recognizing
  the counterexample's own mathematical value.
- *Detection probe*: ask the learner what, if anything, is
  mathematically valuable about finding a counterexample that disproves
  a conjecture.
- *Recovery*: "finding the specific counterexample that breaks a
  conjecture is itself a genuine discovery — it often reveals EXACTLY
  why the pattern fails, pointing toward a more accurate replacement
  conjecture, or toward a genuinely deeper understanding of the
  structure involved. Disproof is progress, not failure."
- *Verification*: the learner articulates, unprompted, that a
  well-constructed counterexample disproving a conjecture is a genuine
  mathematical result in its own right.

## Analogies

**Primary — a scientific hunch under continued testing**: A conjecture
is like a detective's working theory of a case — strongly supported by
the evidence gathered so far, guiding further investigation, but not
yet a formal, courtroom-proven conclusion. If a single piece of
contradicting evidence surfaces later, the theory must be abandoned or
revised, no matter how much prior evidence supported it — and finding
that contradicting piece of evidence is itself valuable detective work,
not a wasted investigation.

**Anti-analogy to retire**: "A conjecture is a theorem that just hasn't
been 'finished' yet." "Finished" implies the conjecture's truth is
already settled and merely awaiting paperwork, directly inviting MC-1's
conflation of strong support with proof.

## Demonstrations

**Extensive-checking-is-not-proof**: present a conjectured pattern that
holds for a large, explicitly-checked range of cases (e.g. a claim
about a formula's behavior for n=1 through n=1000, all verified) and
ask whether this constitutes proof — directly reusing `math.found.
proof`'s own n²−n+41 counterexample-after-forty-confirmations pattern
to demonstrate that extensive verification is never sufficient.

**Valuable disproof**: present a scenario where a plausible-sounding
conjecture is refuted by a specific, carefully-constructed
counterexample, and walk through what the counterexample REVEALS about
why the original pattern fails — demonstrating that the refutation
itself constitutes new mathematical understanding, not merely a dead
end.

## Discovery Questions

Present a pattern that holds for the first several small cases the
learner checks themselves (e.g. a numeric sequence property) and ask
them to predict whether it holds universally — then, if feasible,
reveal or guide toward a later counterexample, letting the learner
experience directly that their own extensive checking did not
constitute proof. Recommendation: guided discovery for the checking-is-
not-proof lesson (directly experiential, reusing this program's
established discovery-through-collision pattern); direct instruction
for the conjecture-versus-axiom distinction, since it requires precise
definitional contrast not naturally rediscovered.

## Teaching Sequence

MC-1 (extensive checking mistaken for proof) is addressed first and
given the most weight, as the direct extension of `math.found.proof`'s
own foundational misconception to this concept's specific terminology
— a learner who conflates checking with proof cannot correctly use
"conjecture" versus "theorem" at all. MC-2 (disproof treated as wasted
effort) is addressed second, as a distinct attitudinal/valuation issue
that only becomes relevant once the proven/unproven boundary itself is
secure.

## Voice Teaching Notes

**Register**: Calibrated language — model epistemic humility explicitly
("this is strongly believed, but not yet proven") whenever discussing
any unproven claim, reinforcing the conjecture/theorem boundary through
consistent word choice, not just explicit rules.

**Wait-time**: After presenting a strongly-supported but unproven claim,
pause before stating whether it's "proven" — let the learner supply the
correct label themselves.

**Load-bearing sentences**:
- "No amount of checking is a proof — the next case could always be the
  counterexample."
- "Disproving a conjecture is a real result — it's progress, not
  failure."

## Assessment Signals

No item bank exists in a Blueprint for this concept; diagnostic

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Likeliest utterance: "but it's been checked so many times, isn't that
basically proof?" — the concept-specific smaller question: "has anyone
shown it MUST be true for every case, or just that no counterexample
has been found YET?" reframes the confusion from "extensive testing
equals certainty" to "absence of a found counterexample is not the same
as a proof that none exists" — directly isolating MC-1's evidentiary-
versus-logical conflation.

## Memory Hooks

**Type**: declarative (the conjecture/theorem/axiom three-way
distinction, plus the three-fates framework). Review form: fresh
examples of currently-open, historically-proven, and historically-
disproven conjectures, requiring the learner to correctly classify each
and explain the classification's basis. Interleaving partners:
`math.found.theorem` (the proven-status this concept explicitly is NOT
until earned) and `math.found.proof` (the standard whose absence
defines this concept).

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: `math.found.theorem` (a conjecture's proven fate),
  `math.found.axiom` (the distinct, deliberately-assumed category this
  concept must not be confused with).
- **Far**: scientific hypotheses under continued testing (a loose but
  instructive parallel — "supported by all evidence so far" is never
  logically equivalent to "proven," in science or mathematics alike,
  though the two fields' standards for what counts as resolution
  differ substantially).
- **Real-world**: any strongly-held but not-yet-verified belief in
  everyday reasoning, and the discipline of distinguishing "I'm very
  confident" from "I've verified this conclusively."
- **Expert transfer**: the learner, encountering an unfamiliar strongly-
  supported claim in any domain, automatically checks whether "strongly
  supported" has been conflated with "proven," applying this concept's
  own calibration standard beyond mathematics.
