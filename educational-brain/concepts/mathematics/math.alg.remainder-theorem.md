## Learning Objective
- The learner can find the remainder of p(x) ÷ (x − a) by directly evaluating p(a), with no division required, correctly identifying a by solving (divisor) = 0 rather than reading its sign off the divisor by inspection.
- The learner can apply the theorem regardless of whether the remainder turns out to be zero, understanding that a nonzero remainder is just as valid an outcome as a zero one.
- The learner can trust p(a) as the exact remainder — not an approximation requiring verification by actual division — because the substitution argument proves the two are algebraically identical.

## Core Understanding
The Remainder Theorem is not a new fact about polynomials to be memorised alongside division; it is a direct, two-line consequence of the Division Algorithm already established in `math.alg.polynomial-division`: p(x) = (x−a)q(x) + r, where r is a constant (since deg r < deg(x−a) = 1 forces r to be degree 0). Substituting x = a into this identity makes the first term vanish entirely — (a−a)·q(a) = 0·q(a) = 0 — leaving p(a) = r, exactly. This substitution is not an approximation, a shortcut that happens to usually work, or a pattern observed empirically; it is a direct algebraic consequence of an equation the learner has already accepted as true, which means the theorem deserves complete trust rather than habitual re-verification by long division. The theorem places no restriction on the value of r: it holds identically whether r turns out to be zero or not, and treating "the theorem gives a useful answer" as somehow conditional on r = 0 is a conflation with a separate, narrower statement (the Factor Theorem, this concept's own direct unlock) — the Remainder Theorem's job is to report *what the remainder is*, and a nonzero remainder is exactly as legitimate and exactly as fully answered a question as a zero one. The one place genuine care is required is identifying the correct value of a: it is not read directly off the divisor's visible sign, but derived by solving (divisor) = 0 for x — for (x−3), solving x−3=0 gives a = 3; for (x+5), rewritten as (x−(−5)), solving gives a = −5, so the sign of a is the *opposite* of the sign of the divisor's visible constant whenever that constant is being added rather than subtracted.

## Mental Models
1. **Beginner — plug the divisor's root into the polynomial, get the remainder, no division needed.** For (x−2), plug in 2; the number you get out is the remainder. *Upgrade trigger*: a divisor written with a plus sign, like (x+5), where "plug in the visible number" gives the wrong sign unless the learner has separately solved for the actual root. *Shelf life*: one session.
2. **Intermediate — always derive a by solving (divisor) = 0, never by reading its sign visually.** For (x+5) = (x−(−5)): solving x+5=0 gives x=−5, so a = −5, the opposite sign of the visible "+5." *Upgrade trigger*: needing to explain *why* substitution gives the exact remainder rather than merely trusting that it does — this model is reliable in practice but offers no justification on its own. *Shelf life*: durable and remains the correct procedure permanently.
3. **Advanced — the substitution is a direct, two-step algebraic proof, not an empirical pattern.** p(x)=(x−a)q(x)+r; substituting x=a makes (a−a)q(a)=0, leaving p(a)=r exactly — this is the entire proof, and it holds unconditionally for every polynomial and every value of a. *Upgrade trigger*: needing to recognise the theorem's corollary (the Factor Theorem) as a special case, not a separate rule — r=0 is simply what the theorem outputs when a happens to be a root, nothing more.
4. **Expert — the Remainder Theorem is the specific case of polynomial evaluation being identical to the "leftover" of a division, and this equivalence is what makes root-finding, factoring, and evaluation-by-substitution three views of a single underlying algebraic identity.** *Shelf life*: permanent, and it is the model that makes the Factor Theorem, synthetic division's own bottom-row entry, and later polynomial root-finding techniques all feel like restatements of one idea rather than three separate facts to memorise.

## Why Students Fail
The dominant and highest-frequency failure is substituting the wrong sign for a: facing a divisor of (x−2), the learner correctly reads off and uses x=2, but facing (x+3), the learner substitutes x=+3 rather than the correct x=−3, because the visible constant in the divisor (here, 3) is used directly rather than being derived by actually solving (x+3)=0 for x — the learner is pattern-matching on "the number that appears in the divisor" rather than performing the one-step algebra that determines its correct sign. The second failure is a conceptual conflation rather than a computational slip: the learner has absorbed that the theorem is *useful* for testing whether a value is a root (where the remainder comes out to zero), and over time this narrows into believing the theorem only genuinely "works" or "applies" in that special case — so when a computation correctly produces a nonzero remainder, the learner treats this as the theorem having failed to give a useful answer, rather than recognising that "the remainder is 7" is every bit as complete and correct an answer as "the remainder is 0." The third failure is one of trust rather than technique: having correctly computed p(a) and been told this equals the remainder, the learner performs the polynomial division anyway "to check," because the substitution feels like an unproven shortcut rather than a fact that has already been algebraically established — this failure does not corrupt any answer, but it defeats the entire efficiency purpose of the theorem and signals that the substitution argument's own logic was never genuinely internalised.

## Misconceptions
Reused by reference from the Blueprint's Component 2 registry (MC-1..MC-3) and its repair actions B01–B03, with birth-type classification added.

- **MC-1 — SUBSTITUTE-WRONG-SIGN** (FOUNDATIONAL)
  - **Birth type**: Type 4, notation-induced — the visible constant in the divisor (x±k) is pattern-matched directly as the value to substitute, rather than being run through the one-step algebra (solve divisor = 0) that actually determines a's correct sign.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: for divisor (x−2), substituting x=−2 rather than x=+2, or for (x+3), substituting x=+3 rather than x=−3.
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): "For divisor (x−3), which value do you substitute: x=−3 (the constant's sign), or x=+3 (solving x−3=0)?" — choosing the constant's raw sign confirms MC-1.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B01 — restate the derivation rule with no exceptions: solve (divisor) = 0 for x, every time, never read the sign off directly; for (x+5)=(x−(−5)), the sign of a is the opposite of the visible constant.
  - **Verification of death**: given a mixed set of divisors including both (x−k) and (x+k) forms, the learner derives the correct a for each by solving, not by pattern-matching, and gets every sign right.

- **MC-2 — THEOREM-IS-ONLY-FOR-ROOTS** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 5, instruction-induced — the Factor Theorem (a genuine special case, requiring r=0) is typically taught alongside or immediately after the Remainder Theorem, and without an explicit statement that the general theorem holds unconditionally, the two collapse into one "roots-only" rule in the learner's mind.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: "the remainder theorem tells you if something is a factor; if p(a)≠0, the theorem doesn't apply."
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): "p(x)=x²+1, divide by (x−1). Compute p(1). Is the theorem applicable here?" — a learner who says the nonzero result means the theorem "doesn't give useful information" confirms MC-2.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B02 — restate explicitly: the theorem states remainder = p(a), unconditionally; the Factor Theorem is the special-case corollary when that remainder happens to be zero, not a redefinition of when the general theorem applies.
  - **Verification of death**: given a value of a that produces a clearly nonzero p(a), the learner reports the nonzero remainder confidently as a complete, correct answer, with no hedging about the theorem "not applying."

- **MC-3 — DIVISION-STILL-REQUIRED** (moderate)
  - **Birth type**: Type 1, overgeneralisation of a healthy general habit ("verify your work") applied specifically to a step that is not actually uncertain — the substitution result is not a guess needing confirmation, but the learner has not yet internalised the two-line proof deeply enough to trust it on its own terms.
  - **Characteristic phrase**: "I got p(2)=7, but let me check by doing the long division to make sure."
  - **Detection probe** (verbatim, Blueprint P41): "p(x)=x³+2x+1 divided by (x−1). You compute p(1)=4. Is 4 the remainder, or should you verify with long division?" — choosing to verify confirms MC-3.
  - **Repair**: Blueprint Repair Action B03 — walk the two-line proof explicitly (p(x)=(x−a)q(x)+r; substitute x=a; (a−a)q(a)=0; p(a)=r), establishing that this is an algebraic identity holding for all polynomials and all values of a, with no exceptions, so long division would produce the identical number, not a more trustworthy one.
  - **Verification of death**: the learner reports a computed remainder from substitution alone, with no unprompted follow-up division, and can state why none is needed if asked.

## Analogies
- **Best — testing integer divisibility without performing the division.** Does 7 divide 371? Rather than long division, checking 371 = 7×53 + 0 confirms it directly — the Remainder Theorem is the polynomial version of exactly this shortcut, skipping the full division to get the remainder directly. This is the Blueprint's own P03 analogy and it directly grounds both the theorem's trustworthiness (MC-3's repair) and its unconditional applicability (MC-2's repair, since integer division's remainder is meaningful whether or not it happens to be zero).
- **Alternative — solving for the "off switch."** The value a is whatever makes the divisor (x−a) equal to zero — the "off switch" that silences the first term of the division identity entirely; finding it always means solving an equation, never reading a sign by inspection. This targets MC-1 directly.
- **Story analogy** — a translator who reports a message's meaning by direct interpretation rather than by physically walking through every intermediate step of a longer, equivalent process; both methods agree exactly, and trusting the shortcut is not a leap of faith once the equivalence is understood.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "the theorem tells you whether something is a factor."** This is precisely MC-2's collapse of two distinct facts into one; the correct, more general statement is "the theorem tells you the remainder, always — whether that remainder happens to be zero (making it a factor) is a separate, follow-on question."
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — "read the number in the divisor and use that."** This licenses MC-1 directly; the correct habit is always "solve the divisor equals zero," which merely *coincides* with the visible number when the divisor is written in (x−k) form and gives the opposite sign when written as (x+k).

## Demonstrations
- **The sign-derivation drill.** Present several divisors in mixed forms — (x−3), (x+5), (x−0), x itself — and require the learner to solve each one for its root explicitly before any evaluation is attempted. *Predict the sign of a before solving* — a learner who predicts incorrectly and then corrects via the actual algebra experiences the gap directly, operationalising Blueprint B01's repair.
- **The nonzero-is-fine demonstration.** Compute p(a) for a case with a genuinely nonzero result, then explicitly state the complete answer ("the remainder is 12") with no further qualification — followed by a side-by-side case where p(a) = 0, explicitly naming that case as the special one (a factor was found), not the other way around. This inverts the usual framing to prevent MC-2's collapse.
- **The proof walk-through, then a trust test.** Derive the two-line proof live (division identity, substitute x=a, first term vanishes, p(a)=r), then immediately present a fresh problem and ask the learner to report the remainder from substitution *alone*, resisting the urge to verify by division. *Predict, before computing, whether verification by division will be necessary* — this operationalises Blueprint B03's repair as a standing trust-building habit.

## Discovery Questions
Direct instruction wins for the theorem's own statement and proof — it is a two-line algebraic consequence of an already-established identity, and there is no genuine discovery path to it that improves on simply deriving it explicitly and then demonstrating its reliability. What is worth surfacing as a question, given how persistently MC-2 recurs, is the "does this always work, even when the remainder isn't zero" distinction: (1) **Need** — "Find the remainder when p(x)=x²+1 is divided by (x−1) using substitution." (2) **Playground** — apply the theorem to several divisors, some giving zero remainders and some not, recording the outcome each time. (3) **Invention** — "Does the theorem only work for the zero-remainder cases, or for all of them?" (4) **Collision** — offer a learner-proposed rule that the theorem "only applies" when p(a)=0, and test it directly against a case where p(a) is nonzero — the substitution still produces a correct, meaningful remainder either way. (5) **Formalisation** — the theorem states remainder = p(a) unconditionally; a zero result is the special case (Factor Theorem), not a precondition for the general statement being useful. (6) **Compression** — "The theorem always tells you the remainder. Zero is just one of the possible answers, not a requirement."

## Teaching Sequence
The sign-derivation rule (TA-A01, targeting MC-1) is established first, using integer divisibility testing as the analogy bridge, before any efficiency-focused worked examples are introduced — a learner who has not yet secured the correct-sign habit will compute a wrong remainder confidently, and no amount of subsequent practice on the theorem's *application* (finding remainders, testing factors) will surface or repair that if the sign error is baked into every example silently. The Factor Theorem sequencing follows the Blueprint's own explicit Teaching Notes guidance: the Remainder Theorem is applied successfully to several nonzero-remainder cases first (TA-A02's own worked example 1), establishing the general "always gives an answer" pattern, before the zero-remainder/factor-testing case is introduced (TA-A02's worked example 2) — presenting both simultaneously, per the Blueprint's own stated reasoning, is what most reliably causes MC-2's merger of the two theorems into a single "roots-only" rule. MC-3 (the division-still-required over-verification habit) is addressed throughout rather than as an isolated stage, since it can recur at any point where a learner's confidence in the substitution result wavers, and the proof itself (walked through explicitly at TA-A01's own analogy bridge) is the standing reference point for repairing it wherever it appears. The Blueprint's own Component 4 sequence (TA-A01 analogy bridge with proof sketch and sign rule, TA-A02 worked-example pair covering both nonzero and zero-remainder cases, TA-A03 mastery gate) is reused by reference and not restated turn-by-turn here.

## Tutor Actions
- **TEST-THINKING: Prediction** — the sign-derivation drill, required before every substitution until deriving a by solving (never by inspection) is automatic.
- **DO: Demonstration** — the nonzero-is-fine demonstration, deliberately leading with a nonzero-remainder case rather than a factor-testing case, to establish the general pattern before the special case is introduced.
- **DO: Worked example, the proof walk-through** — performed explicitly at least once, live, rather than merely stated as a given fact, since the Blueprint's own Teaching Notes identify seeing the proof as the direct antidote to MC-3's persistent over-verification habit.
- **Does NOT fit: introducing the Factor Theorem in the same session as the first Remainder Theorem examples.** Per the Blueprint's own explicit sequencing guidance, this reliably produces MC-2's merger of the two theorems.
- **Does NOT fit: skipping the proof and presenting the substitution rule as a given fact to be trusted on authority.** A learner who has not seen *why* p(a) equals the remainder has no basis for resisting the urge to re-verify by division (MC-3), and "trust me" is a weaker foundation than a two-line derivation the learner can re-derive themselves.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The load-bearing sentence is "solve the divisor equals zero — don't just read the sign." Slow down on "solve," making clear this is an action to perform, not a value to read off. Listen for a learner stating a substitution value immediately upon seeing the divisor, with no audible pause for the one-step algebra — the immediate, unpaused response is the signature of MC-1's pattern-matching rather than genuine derivation. For MC-2, listen for a rising, uncertain tone or a qualifying phrase ("...but that doesn't really tell us anything, right?") immediately after reporting a nonzero remainder — the uncertainty itself, even when the arithmetic was correct, signals the theorem's general applicability has not landed. A confident, unhesitating "the remainder is [value]" with no follow-up verification is the strongest positive signal this concept produces. ## Assessment Signals
- **A substitution using the divisor's visible sign directly rather than its derived root (e.g. p(−2) for divisor (x−2))** — MC-1; route to the sign-derivation drill, never to a bare restatement of "use the root."
- **A correctly computed nonzero remainder described as the theorem "not applying" or "not being useful"** — MC-2, cleanly diagnostic; route to the nonzero-is-fine demonstration.
- **A correct substitution result followed by an unprompted attempt at full polynomial division "to check"** — MC-3; route to the proof walk-through, emphasising that the two methods are algebraically guaranteed to agree, not merely usually.
- **A confidently reported nonzero remainder treated as a complete, final answer with no hedging** — the strongest positive signal for MC-2's repair; do not require any further "checking" once this appears reliably.
- **Mastery trigger**: the Blueprint's TA-A03 gate, MAMR ⌈0.85×5⌉ = 5/5, including its explicit True/False item testing whether the theorem is believed to require p(a)=0 and its P76 transfer probe (both a nonzero-remainder case and a factor-test case on the same polynomial) — a gate passed without correctly handling both item types does not certify that MC-2's conflation has genuinely been resolved.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
The likely utterance is "wait, which number do I plug in?" facing a divisor written with a plus sign. The concept-specific smaller question isolates the one-step algebra directly: **"What value of x makes (x+5) equal to zero? Just solve that — forget the polynomial for a second."** — separating the sign-derivation judgment from the (unrelated) evaluation arithmetic that follows. If the freeze is specifically about trusting a nonzero result, shrink to the proof's own core step: **"If x equals a, what does (x−a) become? And anything times zero is...?"** — walking the single algebraic fact (the first term vanishes) that the entire theorem rests on, in isolation from any specific polynomial's numbers. ## Memory Hooks
- Concept type: **fact-plus-procedure** — the theorem's statement (remainder = p(a)) is a fact to be recalled instantly, while deriving the correct value of a is a short, mandatory procedure attached to every application. Review the sign-derivation step via rapid mixed-divisor drilling (no full polynomial attached) and the theorem's application via full worked problems, separately, so a decay in the sign rule is diagnosable independent of a decay in the substitution-evaluation skill.
- Concept-specific deviation: keep at least one nonzero-remainder item and one zero-remainder (factor-test) item in every review cycle, deliberately paired, to keep MC-2's distinction alive rather than letting practice drift toward one case type.
