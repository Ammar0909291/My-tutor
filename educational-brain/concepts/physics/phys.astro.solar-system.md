# The Earth-Moon-Sun System: Seasons, Phases, Eclipses and Tides — `phys.astro.solar-system`

## Learning Objective

After this concept, the learner can:

1. Explain the seasons by axial tilt, not distance.
2. Explain the Moon's phases as our changing view of its sunlit half.
3. Explain when and why solar and lunar eclipses happen.
4. Explain two tides a day and why the Moon dominates the tides.

## Core Understanding

Earth's axis is tilted 23.4° to the plane of its orbit and keeps pointing the same way in space all year. In June the northern hemisphere leans towards the Sun: the noon Sun climbs high and the days are long. At latitude 28.6°N the noon Sun stands 84.8° above the horizon in June but only 38.0° in December. A slanting beam spreads the same sunlight over more ground — about 1.6 times more here in December — so each square metre is heated less, and the short days add to the effect. That is winter. Southern countries have their summer in December, when the southern hemisphere leans towards the Sun. Distance is not the cause: Earth is actually closest to the Sun in early January (147 million km) and farthest in July (152 million km), a change of only about 3%.

The Moon shines by reflected sunlight, so one half of it is always lit. As it orbits Earth every 29.5 days we see different amounts of that lit half: none at new moon (Moon between Earth and Sun), half of it at the quarters (Moon at right angles), all of it at full moon (Moon opposite the Sun). Earth's shadow plays no part in the phases. A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes exactly between Sun and Earth, so its shadow falls on Earth — only possible at new moon; a lunar eclipse happens when the Moon passes through Earth's shadow — only possible at full moon. They do not happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to Earth's, so at most new and full moons it passes above or below the Sun–Earth line. Total solar eclipses are possible because the Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away, so both appear about half a degree across.

The Moon's gravity pulls the near side of Earth slightly more than Earth's centre, and the centre slightly more than the far side. That difference stretches Earth's oceans into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one on the opposite side. As Earth rotates under them, most coasts get two high tides roughly every 12 hours 25 minutes. The Sun pulls Earth about 180 times harder than the Moon does, but tides depend on the difference in pull across Earth, which falls off as 1/d³; the Moon is so much closer that its tidal effect is about 2.2 times the Sun's. When Sun and Moon line up at new and full moon, their tides add — spring tides; at the quarters they partly cancel — neap tides.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner (arriving)**: summer when nearer the Sun; phases from Earth's shadow; the
  Moon "pulls the sea up" on one side.
- **Intermediate**: tilt → Sun height and day length; phases from the lit half seen at
  different angles; eclipses need alignment at new/full moon; two tidal bulges from
  differential gravity.
- **Advanced**: precession of the axis; the eclipse nodes and eclipse seasons; tidal
  friction slowing Earth's rotation and moving the Moon outward.
- **Expert**: tidal locking (why we always see the same face of the Moon); Milankovitch
  cycles and ice ages.
- **Versioning note**: install the intermediate model; mention tidal locking as the
  reason the Moon keeps one face towards us.

## Why Students Fail

"Nearer the fire is warmer" is a strong everyday rule. Diagrams of phases look like
shadows. And "the Moon pulls the water" predicts one tide, not two.

## Misconceptions

**M1 — Summer happens because Earth is closer to the Sun**
- *Why*: everyday heat-source intuition (type 4).
- *Symptom / phrases*: "we're nearer the Sun in summer".
- *Detection probe (verbatim)*: "Why is it warmer in summer than in winter?"
- *Recovery*: opposite hemispheres; perihelion in January.
- *Verification*: noon-Sun calculations for two seasons.

**M2 — The Moon's phases are caused by Earth's shadow**
- *Why*: shadow pictures (type 4).
- *Symptom*: "the dark part is Earth's shadow".
- *Detection probe*: "What causes the Moon's phases?"
- *Recovery*: Earth's shadow can only reach the full moon — that's a lunar eclipse.
- *Verification*: place the Moon for each phase in a diagram.

**M3 — There is only one tidal bulge, facing the Moon**
- *Why*: "the Moon pulls the water towards it" (type 5).
- *Symptom*: predicts one high tide a day.
- *Detection probe*: "How many high tides does a beach get in a day, and why?"
- *Recovery*: the far side is pulled least — it is "left behind", forming a second
  bulge.
- *Verification*: explain spring and neap tides.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy**: a torch beam on a table — straight down, a small bright patch;
  slanted, a large dim one.
  *Breaking point*: the atmosphere also matters; the torch ignores it.
- **Alternative (tides)**: three runners tied by elastic, with the front one pulled
  hardest — the line stretches out at both ends.
  *Breaking point*: illustrative only.
- **Anti-analogy to avoid**: "the Moon is partly hidden behind Earth." It installs M2.

## Voice Teaching Notes

*How it sounds when taught well*: the tutor always places Sun, Earth and Moon before
naming a phase; says "tilt, not distance"; says "the difference in pull" for tides.

*Load-bearing sentence to slow down on*: "Half the Moon is always lit — the phases are
just how much of that lit half faces us."

*What to listen for*: "closer to the Sun" → M1; "Earth's shadow" → M2; "one bulge"
→ M3.

## Assessment Signals

**Diagnostic — golden probe**: "Why is it warmer in summer?" Correct: the tilt makes
the Sun higher and days longer.

**Distractor-mapped items**:
- "Cause of the seasons?" Options: axial tilt; distance from the Sun; Earth's
  rotation speed; sunspots. Answer: axial tilt. "Distance" targets M1.
- "Cause of the phases?" Options: our changing view of the sunlit half; Earth's
  shadow; clouds; the Moon's own rotation. Answer: the first. "Earth's shadow" targets
  M2.
