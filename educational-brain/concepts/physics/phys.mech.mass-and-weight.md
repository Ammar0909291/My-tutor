# Mass, Weight and Free Fall — `phys.mech.mass-and-weight`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `phys.mech.mass-and-weight`
- **Curriculum location**: physics / mechanics
- **Prerequisites** (from KG `requires`, with the load-bearing part):
  - `phys.mech.force` — the load-bearing part is that a force is a push or pull,
    measured in newtons, exerted by one body on another. Weight IS a force — the
    Earth's pull on a body — so without force as an interaction, "weight in
    newtons" has no meaning.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none listed. In practice W = mg is used in every
  mechanics problem that involves gravity: free-body diagrams, normal force,
  inclined planes, projectile motion, potential energy mgh, and it is the
  surface version of `phys.mech.universal-gravitation`.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery threshold**: 0.70 ·
  **Est. hours**: 2 · **References**: NCERT Science Class 9 Ch. 10; Halliday Resnick Ch. 5

## Learning Objective

After this concept, the learner can:

1. Distinguish mass (amount of matter, kg, the same everywhere) from weight (the
     gravitational force on a body, N, which depends on g).
2. Compute weight with W = mg on Earth (g ≈ 9.8 N/kg), the Moon (≈1.6 N/kg) and
     other bodies, and find g from a weight and a mass.
3. Explain that without air resistance all bodies fall with the same acceleration
     g, whatever their mass.
4. Explain why, in air, a light spread-out object such as a feather falls slowly.

## Core Understanding

Mass and weight are different quantities. Mass is the amount of matter in a body, measured in kilograms; it is the same on Earth, on the Moon and in deep space. Weight is the force with which gravity pulls on that body, measured in newtons; it depends on where the body is. A 5 kg bag of rice is 5 kg everywhere, but the Moon pulls on it about six times more weakly than the Earth does, so on a spring balance it stretches the spring much less.

The link is W = mg. Here g is the gravitational field strength — the pull on each kilogram. Near Earth's surface g ≈ 9.8 N/kg; on the Moon g ≈ 1.6 N/kg. A 60 kg person weighs about 60 × 9.8 ≈ 590 N on Earth and about 60 × 1.6 ≈ 97 N on the Moon, and is 60 kg on both. A beam balance, which compares one mass with another, reads the same on the Moon; a spring balance, which measures the pull, does not. A bathroom scale measures the force and is marked in kg by dividing by Earth's g.

Free fall has a surprise: without air resistance, every body falls with the same acceleration. A 10 kg ball is pulled ten times harder than a 1 kg ball, but it is also ten times harder to speed up, so the two effects cancel exactly. Near Earth that common acceleration is g ≈ 9.8 m/s² — the same number as the field strength, because 1 N/kg is the same unit as 1 m/s². On the Moon a hammer and a feather dropped together land together. In air, a feather falls slowly not because it is light but because air resistance is large compared with its small weight.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner (arriving)**: "weight" means kilograms; heavier things fall faster;
  astronauts float because they have no weight and maybe no mass.
- **Intermediate**: mass is the amount of matter, fixed; weight is a force, W = mg,
  which changes with g. All bodies fall together without air.
- **Advanced**: g is both the field strength (N/kg) and the free-fall acceleration
  (m/s²); the equality of the two is why mass cancels in free fall. "Weightless"
  astronauts in orbit still have their weight acting — they and their station
  are falling together, so nothing presses on them.
- **Expert**: the mass that gravity pulls on (gravitational mass) and the mass
  that resists acceleration (inertial mass) are measured to be equal to better
  than one part in 10¹⁴ — the equivalence principle, the foundation of general
  relativity. g varies slightly with height and latitude (`phys.mech.gravitational-field`).
- **Versioning note**: install the intermediate model. Name the "pulled harder but
  harder to speed up" cancellation; defer orbital weightlessness to gravitation.

## Why Students Fail

Everyday language uses "weight" for kilograms ("my weight is 60 kg"), so mass and
weight blur into one quantity, and anything that changes weight seems to change
mass. Second, everyday experience shows heavy things falling faster through air —
a stone beats a leaf — and learners generalise to "heavier falls faster" in
general. Third, "weightless" astronauts suggest that mass itself can vanish.

## Misconceptions

**M1 — Mass and weight are the same thing**
- *Why*: everyday language ("weight 60 kg") (type 2, everyday language).
- *Symptom / phrases*: "her mass is less on the Moon"; weight given in kg as a
  physics answer.
- *Detection probe (verbatim)*: "An astronaut has a mass of 60 kg on Earth. What
  is her mass on the Moon, and is her weight the same there?"
- *Recovery*: the beam balance still balances 5 kg against 5 kg on the Moon —
  the amount of matter did not change. Only the pull did.
- *Verification*: three items asking separately for mass and weight on two worlds.

**M2 — Heavier objects fall faster**
- *Why*: everyday falls happen in air, where shape and weight together decide
  air resistance (type 1, perceptual).
- *Symptom*: "the 10 kg ball lands first" even with no air.
- *Detection probe*: "A 10 kg ball and a 1 kg ball are dropped together where
  there is no air. Which lands first?"
- *Recovery*: the hammer and feather on the Moon. Then the reason: ten times the
  pull, ten times harder to speed up — same acceleration.
- *Verification*: predict vacuum falls for three pairs; explain the feather in air.

**M3 — Weightless means massless**
- *Why*: floating astronauts look as if they have lost their "heaviness"
  altogether (type 1, perceptual).
- *Symptom*: "in space you have no mass"; "you could push the space station with
  a finger because nothing has mass there".
- *Detection probe*: "A 60 kg astronaut floats in deep space. Is she still hard to
  push into motion?"
- *Recovery*: mass still resists changes in motion everywhere — pushing a
  floating astronaut still takes a real push. Only the gravitational pull (weight)
  is small or unfelt.
- *Verification*: one item contrasting "floating" with "easy to accelerate".

**M4 — g is the force of gravity**
- *Why*: "g" is introduced as "gravity" and treated as a force (type 5,
  instructional compression).
- *Symptom*: says "the force of gravity on the stone is 9.8"; omits the mass.
- *Detection probe*: "Is the force of gravity on a 2 kg stone 9.8 N?"
- *Recovery*: g is the pull on EACH kilogram; the force on the whole stone is
  2 × 9.8 = 19.6 N.
- *Verification*: two weight calculations with units stated.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy**: a team pulling a cart. A cart twice as heavy is pulled by
  twice as many people — but it is also twice as hard to get moving. It speeds
  up at the same rate. That is free fall: more pull, proportionally more to move.
  *Breaking point*: the team is a choice; gravity's pull is automatically
  proportional to mass. Don't extend to "gravity decides how hard to pull".
- **Alternative**: a sack of rice and a measuring cup — the rice in the sack (mass)
  does not change when you carry it to the Moon; how heavy it feels in your hand
  (weight) does.
  *Breaking point*: "feels heavy" also includes inertia when shaking the sack;
  keep the analogy to holding it still.
- **Anti-analogy to avoid**: "weight is how much stuff you have." This installs M1.

## Demonstrations

- **Home, no equipment**: a book with a sheet of paper resting flat on top —
  drop them and they fall together (the book shields the paper from air). Then
  drop the sheet alone, flat and then crumpled.
- **Teacher demo**: a coin and feather in a tube; pump out the air and they fall
  together.
- **Video**: the hammer-and-feather drop on the Moon (1971).
- **Spring vs beam balance**: hang the same object on both; discuss which reading
  would change on the Moon.
- **Prediction before demo**: "a tennis ball and a bowling ball dropped from the
  same height — which lands first?"

## Discovery Questions

Guided discovery for free fall, direct instruction for W = mg.

**Structure**:
1. *Need*: "If you went to the Moon, would you be lighter? Would there be less of
   you?" → separate the two questions.
2. *Discovery*: drop pairs (book and paper, crumpled and flat paper) and look for
   what actually decides the order.
3. *Direct instruction*: W = mg with g values.
4. *Practice*: weights on different worlds; free-fall predictions in air and vacuum.

## Tutor Actions

Priority dispatch (in order):

1. **WORKED-EXAMPLE** — three traces:
   (a) 60 kg person: W = 60 × 9.8 ≈ 590 N on Earth; 60 × 1.6 ≈ 97 N on the Moon;
       mass 60 kg on both.
   (b) 900 kg rover on Mars, g ≈ 3.7 N/kg: W ≈ 3330 N.
   (c) Finding g: a 50 kg student weighs 250 N on a planet → g = 250/50 = 5 N/kg.

2. **ERROR-ANALYSIS** — a student writes "on the Moon the astronaut's mass is
   10 kg". Ask the learner what a beam balance would show.

3. **PREDICTION-BEFORE-DEMO** — before the Moon video, ask which lands first.

4. **RETRIEVAL-SCHEDULE-PROMPT** — next three sessions:
   "Weight of a 2 kg bag on Earth and Moon" → "which balance reads the same on
   the Moon?" → "why does a parachutist fall slower than a stone?"

## Voice Teaching Notes

*How it sounds when taught well*: the tutor always pairs "mass, in kilograms"
and "weight, a force, in newtons"; says "pull on each kilogram" for g; separates
"in a vacuum" from "in air" every time falling is discussed.

*Load-bearing sentence to slow down on*: "The heavier body is pulled harder, but
it is harder to speed up by exactly the same factor — so everything falls with
the same acceleration when there is no air."

*What to listen for*: "my weight is 60 kg" used as physics → M1; "the heavy one
lands first" (vacuum) → M2; "no mass in space" → M3; "gravity is 9.8 newtons"
→ M4.

## Assessment Signals

**Diagnostic — golden probe**: "An astronaut has a mass of 60 kg on Earth. What is
her mass on the Moon, and is her weight the same there?" Correct: 60 kg; weight
smaller, about 97 N instead of about 590 N.

**Distractor-mapped items**:
- "Mass of a 60 kg astronaut on the Moon?" Options: 60 kg, 10 kg, 97 kg, 0 kg.
  Answer: 60 kg. Distractor 10 kg targets M1; 0 kg targets M3.
- "10 kg and 1 kg balls dropped in a vacuum?" Options: 10 kg first, 1 kg first,
  together, depends on height. Answer: together. Distractor "10 kg first"
  targets M2.

**Guided practice → independent practice fading ladder**:
1. W = mg on Earth (3 problems, g = 9.8 N/kg).
2. W on other worlds (3 problems).
3. Finding g or m from W (3 problems).
4. Free-fall predictions in air and in a vacuum (4 items).
5. (Unscaffolded) explain a parachutist and a feather.

**Mastery gate set** (per assessment/05):
- *Production*: 4 weight calculations on different worlds.
- *New surface*: a fictional planet with given g.
- *Mixed*: mass/weight items interleaved with free-fall predictions.
- *Delayed*: one-week check — the floating astronaut.

**Calibration note**: W = mg is easy, so learners feel done. The check that reveals
miscalibration is the vacuum fall: many who compute weights perfectly still say
the heavier ball lands first.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

*Likeliest utterance*: "but heavy things DO fall faster" (M2); "isn't weight just
kilograms?" (M1).

*Concept-specific smaller question*: "Drop a book and a sheet of paper resting on
it — what happens?" (Together.) "So when air can't slow the paper, does being
lighter make it slower?"

*M1 recovery*: "If you flew to the Moon, would there be less of you — fewer atoms?"
(No.) "So which quantity stays the same?"

## Transfer Connections

- *Near*: `phys.mech.newtons-second-law` — free fall is F = ma with F = mg, so a = g.
- *Near*: `phys.mech.kinematics-1d` — falling bodies with a = g.
- *Far*: `phys.mech.universal-gravitation` — g = GM/R² explains why the Moon's g is
  smaller.
- *Real-world*: payload limits for rockets (weight vs mass), weighing scales,
  parachutes and skydivers.
- *Expert transfer*: the equivalence of gravitational and inertial mass in general
  relativity (`phys.rel` domain).

## Cross-Subject Connections

- **Chemistry**: the mass of reactants is conserved, not their weight — a reaction
  run on the Moon balances by mass in grams exactly as on Earth.
- **Biology**: bone and muscle loss in astronauts — the body adapts to reduced
  weight, not reduced mass.
- **Mathematics**: W = mg is a direct proportion; g is the constant of
  proportionality and the slope of a W–m graph.
- **Geography**: g varies slightly between the poles and the equator, which
  matters for precise surveying and gravimetry.
