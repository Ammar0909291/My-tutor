## Learning Objective

After this concept, the learner can:

1. Classify energy sources as renewable or non-renewable and trace each to its
     origin.
2. Explain that energy is transformed, not used up, and that what is lost is
     usefulness.
3. Compute efficiency and use the kilowatt-hour (1 kWh = 3.6 MJ).
4. Compare sources on availability, cost, reliability and environmental impact.

## Core Understanding

Electricity is a carrier, not a source: it has to be generated from something. In a coal power station, chemical energy in coal becomes heat, which turns water into steam, which spins a turbine, which drives a generator. Follow almost any source back far enough and you reach the Sun: coal, oil and gas are the remains of ancient plants and plankton that stored sunlight; wind is driven by uneven solar heating; hydroelectric power uses water lifted by evaporation. The exceptions are tidal power (the Moon's gravity), geothermal power (heat from the Earth's interior) and nuclear power (energy stored in heavy nuclei). Sources are renewable if they are replaced naturally on a human timescale — solar, wind, hydro, tidal, geothermal, biomass — and non-renewable if they took millions of years to form or cannot be replaced — coal, oil, natural gas and nuclear fuel.

Energy is never used up. After a car journey the petrol's chemical energy has become motion and then heat in the engine, the road and the air — all of it still exists, but spread out and much harder to use. What we really lose is energy in a useful, concentrated form. Efficiency measures how much of the input ends up as the output we want: efficiency = useful energy out ÷ energy in. A coal station that turns 1000 MJ of fuel energy into 350 MJ of electrical energy is 35 % efficient; the other 650 MJ leave as heat in the cooling water and air. No real device reaches 100 %, and a claim of more than 100 % breaks conservation of energy.

Electricity is sold by the kilowatt-hour, the energy delivered by 1 kW in 1 hour: 1 kWh = 1000 W × 3600 s = 3.6 MJ. A 2 kW heater running for 3 hours uses 6 kWh. Choosing sources is never a matter of "good" and "bad": solar and wind need no fuel and emit nothing while running, but they stop at night or in calm weather and need storage or backup; hydro is steady but floods valleys and blocks fish; fossil fuels are reliable but finite and emit carbon dioxide; nuclear emits little carbon but leaves long-lived waste.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner (arriving)**: energy comes from the socket and gets used up; renewable
  means good, fossil means bad.
- **Intermediate**: every source has an origin and a chain of transformations;
  energy is conserved but degraded; efficiency = useful out ÷ in; kWh is energy.
- **Advanced**: Sankey diagrams; the grid balances steady (baseload) and variable
  supply; the trade-offs are multi-dimensional.
- **Expert**: the second law sets a ceiling on heat-engine efficiency (Carnot);
  life-cycle analysis counts the energy and emissions of building the plant too.
- **Versioning note**: install the intermediate model; signal Carnot's limit as
  the reason power stations sit near 35–45 %.

## Why Students Fail

Everyday language says we "use up" or "consume" energy, which flatly contradicts
conservation. "Renewable" is heard as a moral label rather than a statement about
replacement timescale, so trade-offs disappear. And the kWh looks like a power
unit because it contains "kW".

## Misconceptions

**M1 — Energy is used up and disappears**
- *Why*: "consumption" language (type 2, everyday language).
- *Symptom / phrases*: "the energy was used up"; "the battery's energy is gone".
- *Detection probe (verbatim)*: "After a car journey the petrol is gone. Where did
  its energy go?"
- *Recovery*: the power station's 650 MJ are in the warm cooling water — energy is
  transformed and spread out, not destroyed.
- *Verification*: three energy-flow accounts that must balance.

**M2 — Renewable sources have no environmental or practical drawbacks**
- *Why*: renewable is presented as the "good" category (type 5, instructional
  framing).
- *Symptom*: "dams and wind farms don't affect anything"; "solar works all the time".
- *Detection probe*: "Does a hydroelectric dam have any environmental cost?"
- *Recovery*: a dam floods a valley; solar stops at night. Renewable means
  replenished, not cost-free.
- *Verification*: an advantage and a drawback for each of four sources.

**M3 — The kilowatt-hour is a unit of power**
- *Why*: "kW" appears in it (type 4, surface feature).
- *Symptom*: "my bill says I used 200 kW".
- *Detection probe*: "A 2 kW heater runs for 3 hours. How much energy, in kWh and
  in MJ?"
- *Recovery*: kW × hours = energy; 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ.
- *Verification*: two bill calculations.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy**: money spent at a market — the money isn't destroyed; it moves to
  many stall-holders and is hard to collect back. Energy spreads out the same way.
  *Breaking point*: money can be gathered back easily; dispersed heat cannot.
- **Alternative**: a waterfall — water (energy) is never lost at the bottom, but
  it can't drive a wheel again without being lifted.
  *Breaking point*: water can be pumped back with energy; that costs more than it
  returns.
- **Anti-analogy to avoid**: "energy is like food — once eaten, it's gone." It
  installs M1.

## Demonstrations

- **Home**: read the household electricity bill and the meter; compute one
  appliance's kWh from its rating.
- **Teacher demo**: a hand-cranked generator lighting a bulb — the cranker feels
  the effort, the bulb gets warm.
- **Sankey diagram**: draw the power station's 1000 → 350 + 650 MJ flow.
- **Prediction before demo**: "LED or filament bulb — which one warms your hand?"

## Discovery Questions

**Structure**:
1. *Need*: "Where does the energy in your phone charger come from?"
2. *Discovery*: trace three sources back to their origin; look for the Sun.
3. *Direct instruction*: efficiency, kWh, the conservation account.
4. *Apply*: compare sources for a real town.

## Tutor Actions

Priority dispatch (in order):

1. **WORKED-EXAMPLE** — three traces:
   (a) Efficiency: 350/1000 = 35 %; 650 MJ to heat.
   (b) kWh: 2 kW × 3 h = 6 kWh = 21.6 MJ.
   (c) Cost: 1.5 kW × 8 h = 12 kWh; at ₹8/kWh, ₹96.

2. **ERROR-ANALYSIS** — a student writes "the power station destroys 650 MJ". Ask
   where the warm water in the cooling towers comes from.

3. **PREDICTION-BEFORE-DEMO** — before touching the bulbs, ask which wastes more.

4. **RETRIEVAL-SCHEDULE-PROMPT** — next three sessions:
   "classify six sources" → "heater kWh" → "where does the 'lost' energy go?"

## Voice Teaching Notes

*How it sounds when taught well*: the tutor says "transformed" or "spread out",
never "used up"; pairs every source with one advantage and one drawback; says
"kilowatt-hours of energy".

*Load-bearing sentence to slow down on*: "Energy is never used up — it ends up
spread out as heat, which is why what runs out is useful energy, not energy."

*What to listen for*: "used up / gone" → M1; "renewable so no problem" → M2;
"I used 200 kW" → M3.

## Assessment Signals

**Diagnostic — golden probe**: "After a car journey the petrol is gone. Where did its
energy go?" Correct: into motion and then heat in the engine, road and air — it
still exists, spread out.

**Distractor-mapped items**:
- "Station: 1000 MJ in, 350 MJ out — where are the 650 MJ?" Options: destroyed;
  heat in the cooling water and air; stored in the turbine; turned into coal.
  Answer: heat. "Destroyed" targets M1.
- "A 2 kW heater for 3 h?" Options: 6 kW, 6 kWh, 6 MJ, 0.67 kWh. Answer: 6 kWh.
  "6 kW" targets M3.

**Guided practice → independent practice fading ladder**:
1. Classify and trace sources (6 items).
2. Efficiency from energy flows (3 items).
3. kWh and cost (3 items).
4. Advantage/drawback comparisons (3 sources).
5. (Unscaffolded) design an energy mix for a town.

**Mastery gate set** (per assessment/05):
- *Production*: one efficiency and one bill calculation.
- *New surface*: an appliance not seen before.
- *Mixed*: classification interleaved with calculation.
- *Delayed*: one-week check — the "energy crisis" question.

**Calibration note**: classification is easy; the check that reveals
miscalibration is "where did the energy go?" — the "used up" answer survives
correct efficiency calculations.
