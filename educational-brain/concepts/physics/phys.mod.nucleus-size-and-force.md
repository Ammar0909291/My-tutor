## Learning Objective

After this concept, the learner can:

1. Use Z, A and N = A − Z, and identify isotopes.
2. Compute nuclear radii from R = R₀A^(1/3).
3. Explain why all nuclei have about the same density, ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³.
4. Describe the nuclear force: strong, attractive, short-range, charge-independent.

## Core Understanding

A nucleus is made of protons and neutrons, together called nucleons. The atomic number Z counts the protons and fixes the element; the mass number A counts all the nucleons; so a nucleus has N = A − Z neutrons. Uranium-238 (²³⁸₉₂U) has 92 protons and 146 neutrons. Nuclei with the same Z but different N are isotopes of one element — chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 both have 17 protons, with 18 and 20 neutrons — and behave identically in chemistry.

Scattering experiments show that nucleons pack together like marbles in a bag, each taking about the same volume. So a nucleus's volume is proportional to A and its radius to the cube root of A: R = R₀A^(1/3), with R₀ ≈ 1.2 fm (1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m). Iron-56 has R ≈ 1.2 × 3.83 ≈ 4.6 fm; uranium-238 has R ≈ 7.4 fm, only about 6.2 times the radius of a single proton even though it holds 238 nucleons. Because the volume grows exactly as fast as the mass, A cancels and every nucleus has nearly the same density: ρ = A × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg / ((4/3)πR₀³A) ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³, about 10¹⁴ times the density of water. A teaspoon of nuclear matter would weigh about a billion tonnes, and neutron stars are made of matter this dense. Heavier nuclei are bigger, not denser.

Something must hold the nucleus together. The protons repel each other electrically, and at nuclear distances that repulsion is enormous. Gravity between two protons is about 10³⁶ times weaker than their electric repulsion at any distance, and the electrons are far outside the nucleus. The binding comes from the nuclear (strong) force. It acts between any two nucleons — proton–proton, proton–neutron and neutron–neutron alike — and is much stronger than the electric repulsion at separations of about 1–2 fm. It is very short-range, dying away to almost nothing beyond a few femtometres, which is why it holds a nucleus together without pulling neighbouring atoms' nuclei together. At still shorter distances it becomes repulsive, which keeps nucleons from collapsing into each other and explains the constant packing density.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner (arriving)**: the nucleus is a dot; bigger means denser; gravity or
  electrons hold it.
- **Intermediate**: Z, A, N and isotopes; R = R₀A^(1/3); constant nuclear density;
  the nuclear force — strong, short-range, charge-independent.
- **Advanced**: the liquid-drop picture; why heavy nuclei need extra neutrons (the
  long-range Coulomb repulsion grows faster than the short-range binding).
- **Expert**: the nuclear force as a residual of the strong interaction between
  quarks (meson exchange); the nuclear shell model.
- **Versioning note**: install the intermediate model; mention the neutron excess of
  heavy nuclei as the bridge to stability and radioactivity.

## Why Students Fail

"Heavier" is equated with "denser". Only gravity and electromagnetism are familiar, so
learners reach for them. And A^(1/3) is an unfamiliar operation, so the constant
density result is never derived.

## Misconceptions

**M1 — Heavier nuclei are denser**
- *Why*: heavier-means-denser intuition from everyday objects (type 4).
- *Symptom / phrases*: "uranium's nucleus is much denser".
- *Detection probe (verbatim)*: "A uranium-238 nucleus has 238 nucleons; a hydrogen
  nucleus has 1. Is the uranium nucleus denser?"
- *Recovery*: radius ratio 6.2, volume ratio 238.
- *Verification*: compute the density for two nuclei.

**M2 — Gravity (or the electrons) holds the nucleus together**
- *Why*: only familiar forces considered (type 2).
- *Symptom*: "gravity pulls the protons together".
- *Detection probe*: "Protons repel each other strongly. What holds a nucleus
  together?"
- *Recovery*: the 10³⁶ ratio; electrons are outside the nucleus.
- *Verification*: state three properties of the nuclear force.

**M3 — The nuclear force acts only on protons**
- *Why*: forces associated with charge (type 2).
- *Symptom*: "neutrons are just filler".
- *Detection probe*: "Does the nuclear force act on neutrons?"
- *Recovery*: it acts between all nucleons, charge-independent; neutrons add binding
  without adding repulsion.
- *Verification*: explain why heavy nuclei have more neutrons than protons.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy**: marbles in a bag — more marbles make a bigger bag, but the
  packing, and so the density, stays the same.
  *Breaking point*: marbles are rigid; nucleons are quantum particles.
- **Alternative (force)**: Velcro — extremely strong grip on contact, nothing at all
  from a few millimetres away.
  *Breaking point*: Velcro has no repulsive core.
- **Anti-analogy to avoid**: "the nucleus is held together by gravity like a planet."
  It installs M2.

## Demonstrations

- **Home**: compare a bag of 10 marbles and a bag of 100 — weigh them and estimate
  the volume; the density is the same.
- **Teacher demo**: magnetic balls that snap together only on contact, illustrating
  short range.
- **Prediction before demo**: "with ten times the marbles, how much bigger will the
  bag be across?"
