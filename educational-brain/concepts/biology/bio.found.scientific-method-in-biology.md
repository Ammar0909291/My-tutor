## Learning Objective

The learner can: state the components of a controlled experiment
(independent variable — what is deliberately changed; dependent
variable — what is measured; control variables — everything else held
constant; a control group — a baseline for comparison); explain why a
hypothesis that turns out to be FALSE is still a genuinely valuable
scientific result, not a failure; and explain why replication (repeating
a study, ideally by independent researchers) and peer review are
necessary for a finding to be trusted, rather than accepting a single
study's result at face value.

## Core Understanding

The scientific method in biology is a disciplined process for testing
explanations against evidence rather than accepting or rejecting them by
intuition or authority: an observation prompts a hypothesis (a
specific, testable proposed explanation), which is tested via a
controlled experiment that deliberately varies exactly one factor (the
independent variable) while holding all other relevant factors
constant (control variables), measuring the resulting effect (the
dependent variable) against a control group that receives no
intervention, to isolate whether the independent variable — and not
some other, uncontrolled factor — actually caused the observed effect.
Because chance, small sample sizes, and unrecognised confounding
factors can all produce a misleading result from a single experiment,
a finding is only provisionally trusted after sufficient sample size,
statistical evaluation of whether the result could plausibly be due to
chance, and — critically — independent replication by other researchers
obtaining the same result; peer review (independent expert scrutiny of
a study's methods and conclusions before publication) is a further,
necessary check on quality and validity, not a formality. A hypothesis
that is tested and found FALSE is not a failed experiment — it is a
genuine, valuable scientific result that narrows the space of possible
explanations exactly as much as a confirmed hypothesis does, and
progress in biology (as in all science) depends on hypotheses being
falsifiable — capable, in principle, of being shown wrong by evidence.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "the scientific method is a fixed five-step
  recipe to follow in order"**: the learner can recite steps (question,
  hypothesis, experiment, results, conclusion) as a rigid sequence
  without yet understanding WHY each step matters or that real research
  often loops back and revises earlier steps.
- **Intermediate model — "a 'wrong' hypothesis means the experiment
  failed"**: the direct substrate of M2 below — the learner treats a
  disconfirmed hypothesis as a bad outcome rather than a valid result.
  Upgrade trigger: a real historical case where a disproven hypothesis
  was itself scientifically valuable (e.g. ruling out spontaneous
  generation, itself connected to `bio.cell.cell-theory`'s own third
  tenet).
- **Advanced model — "hypotheses are tested, not proven; disconfirmation
  is real progress; a single study is provisional until replicated"**:
  the learner correctly evaluates a described study for controls,
  sample size, and whether it has been replicated before trusting its
  conclusion.
- **Expert model — "the scientific method as an ongoing, self-
  correcting community process, not an individual recipe"**: the
  learner understands that no single study, however well-designed,
  settles a question alone — scientific knowledge is provisional and
  strengthens through accumulated, independently replicated, peer-
  reviewed evidence over time, and can be revised when new evidence
  warrants it.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner who still treats a disproven
  hypothesis as a failure (intermediate model, unrepaired M2) should not
  be pushed toward evaluating real, messy research literature — they
  will misjudge a field with several disconfirmed hypotheses as a field
  in trouble, rather than recognising normal, healthy scientific
  progress.

## Why Students Fail

M1 (the "it's just a theory" confusion) arises because the English word
"theory" carries a strong everyday sense — an unproven guess, a hunch —
that is essentially the OPPOSITE of its precise scientific sense (a
well-substantiated explanatory framework, supported by extensive
evidence, as in the theory of evolution or cell theory); a learner
encountering "theory" in a biology class inevitably brings the everyday
sense along, and nothing about the word itself signals the two meanings
differ. M2 arises because school science instruction — and more broadly,
school assessment generally — frequently and implicitly rewards getting
the "right" predicted answer, so a learner reasonably imports "wrong
guess = bad outcome" from ordinary schoolwork into a domain where a
disconfirmed hypothesis is actually a valid, useful result.

- **M1 — "It's just a theory" — treating a scientific theory as an
  unproven guess (Type 3, language contamination)**: this is the
  taxonomy document's own canonical biology example — the everyday sense
  of "theory" (an unproven guess) collides directly with the scientific
  sense (a well-substantiated explanatory framework backed by extensive
  evidence). Matches Type 3's cleanest diagnostic test: rephrasing away
  from the word "theory" (e.g. "a well-supported explanation confirmed
  by many independent lines of evidence") typically resolves the
  confusion, since the underlying content is not what's hard.
  Characteristic phrase: "it's just a theory, not a fact." Recovery
  path: name the collision explicitly as a word problem — "this is one
  of those words that means something completely different in science
  than at home" — and contrast "theory" with the scientific sense of
  "hypothesis" (a single, as-yet-untested proposed explanation) to show
  scientific vocabulary distinguishes exactly the confidence levels
  everyday "theory" collapses together. Verification-of-death: the
  learner, given a scientific theory (e.g. cell theory, the theory of
  evolution) and a genuine hypothesis side by side, correctly identifies
  which has extensive supporting evidence and which is still being
  tested, using the words correctly rather than as synonyms.
- **M2 — "A hypothesis that turns out to be false means the experiment
  failed" (Type 5, instruction-induced)**: born from ordinary school
  assessment culture, where "wrong answer" is near-universally treated
  as a bad outcome across nearly every subject a learner encounters
  before this concept — a curriculum-wide, near-universal pattern rather
  than an individual misunderstanding, matching Type 5's signature
  exactly. Characteristic phrase: "the experiment didn't work because my
  hypothesis was wrong." Recovery path: this is the one birth type
  entirely preventable at first teaching (per Type 5's own repair
  implication) — name the shelf-life explicitly the first time a
  hypothesis is introduced: "a hypothesis being disproven is not a
  failed experiment — it IS the result, and it's just as useful as a
  confirmed one." Reinforce with a concrete historical case (e.g. testing
  and ruling out spontaneous generation was a genuinely valuable
  scientific result, directly connected to `bio.cell.cell-theory`'s
  third tenet). Verification-of-death: the learner, given a described
  experiment whose hypothesis was disconfirmed, correctly evaluates the
  experiment's DESIGN quality (was it well-controlled?) independently of
  whether the hypothesis turned out true or false.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — a detective testing, not assuming, a suspect's
  guilt**: a hypothesis is a suspect to test against evidence, not a
  belief to defend; a detective who rules out a suspect via solid
  evidence has made real progress, not failed the case — directly
  supports M2's repair.
- **Alternative — "theory" vs. "hunch" as two different confidence
  levels on the same scale**: a hunch (everyday "theory") sits at low
  confidence with little evidence; a scientific theory sits at very high
  confidence with extensive evidence — placing both on one visual scale
  makes the word-collision (M1) concrete rather than abstract.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT say "an experiment is like a court case that
  proves guilt or innocence"**: "proves" overstates what a single
  experiment can establish — science accumulates supporting evidence
  through replication rather than "proving" a hypothesis outright in one
  trial, and this framing risks reinforcing the idea that one
  experiment should settle a question definitively.

## Demonstrations

- **Teacher-demo — spontaneous generation, historically**: present the
  historical hypothesis that living things could arise spontaneously
  from non-living matter, and the controlled experiments (e.g. sealed
  vs. open containers) that disproved it — a genuine case where a
  disconfirmed hypothesis was scientifically valuable, directly
  reinforcing M2's repair and connecting forward to `bio.cell.cell-
  theory`'s third tenet.
- **Discrimination demonstration — identify the variables**: present a
  simple described experiment (e.g. testing whether a fertiliser
  increases plant growth) and have the learner identify the independent
  variable, dependent variable, control variables, and control group
  before being told.

## Discovery Questions

A genuine discovery design fits: **Need** — "how would you actually TEST
whether a fertiliser makes plants grow faster, rather than just
guessing?" **Playground** — the learner proposes an experimental setup.
**Invention** — the learner identifies, largely unprompted, that
comparing fertilised vs. non-fertilised plants under otherwise identical
conditions is needed. **Collision** — pushed on what happens if the
fertilised plants get more sunlight too (confounding variable), forcing
the learner to recognise the need to control ALL other factors, not just
add the fertiliser. **Formalization** — independent/dependent/control
variables and the control group are named precisely. **Compression** —
the learner designs a controlled experiment for a new, unrelated
question.

## Teaching Sequence

M1 (the "it's just a theory" language collision) should be addressed
early and separately from the controlled-experiment mechanics, since it
is a vocabulary issue independent of experimental design; M2 (disproven
hypothesis = failure) should be addressed AFTER the learner has designed
or evaluated at least one controlled experiment themselves, since the
repair depends on the learner appreciating that a well-designed
experiment's VALUE lies in its design and rigour, not in which way its
result comes out — a point best made concrete only once the learner has
experienced designing or checking an experiment's controls.

## Tutor Actions

From `../../teaching-actions/`: **Error Analysis** (the "it's just a
theory" misconception, addressed as a vocabulary collision) →
**Discovery/guided-invention** (the fertiliser experiment design) →
**Worked Example** (the spontaneous-generation historical case) →
**Discrimination** (variable identification in a new scenario). **What
doesn't fit**: presenting the scientific method as a fixed, linear
five-step list to memorise before any hands-on design exercise — real
research loops and revises, and a purely linear presentation reinforces
the "recipe" beginner model past its useful shelf-life.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Listen for "it's just a theory" or "that's just a theory, not proven"
applied to an established scientific theory (evolution, cell theory) —
M1's clearest verbal signature. Listen for "the experiment failed"
applied to a study whose hypothesis was disconfirmed but whose design
was otherwise sound — M2's signature, distinguishable from a genuinely
poorly-designed experiment by asking a follow-up about the controls
used. The load-bearing sentence: "a hypothesis proven wrong is still a
real result — the failure would be not testing it carefully at all" —
delivered slowly, particularly "still a real result." Channel-reality
limits owned by `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Likeliest utterance: "so the experiment didn't work?" after a
disconfirmed-hypothesis scenario, or "well it's just a theory" as a
dismissal (not distress-shaped — foundational, low-stakes concept).
Concept-specific smaller question for M1: "when scientists say
'theory,' do they mean a guess, or something that's been tested and
supported again and again?" Concept-specific smaller question for M2:
"was the experiment well-designed and carefully controlled? Does that
change depending on which way the result came out?" Generic recovery
machinery owned by `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: a new experimental scenario, correctly analysed for its
  variables and control group.
- **Far**: recognising the same "isolate one variable, control the
  rest, don't over-trust a single trial" structure in non-biological
  contexts (e.g. debugging software by changing one thing at a time, or
  evaluating a single anecdotal product review against many independent
  reviews).
- **Real-world**: reading a health or nutrition headline ("Study finds
  X causes Y") and asking whether it was a controlled study, whether it
  has been replicated, and whether it was peer reviewed before trusting
  the claim.
- **Expert transfer**: on meeting any scientific claim in the media, the
  learner spontaneously asks about study design, sample size, and
  replication before accepting or rejecting it.
