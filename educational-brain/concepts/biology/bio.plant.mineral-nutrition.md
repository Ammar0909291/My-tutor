# bio.plant.mineral-nutrition — Mineral Nutrition in Plants

## Learning Objective
The student can correctly explain plant mass as coming primarily from CO2/water (not
soil), correctly distinguish mobile nutrient deficiency patterns (older leaves affected)
from immobile ones (younger leaves affected), and correctly explain over-fertilization's
osmotic-stress mechanism.

## Core Understanding
Plants need mineral ions from the soil for essential biochemical functions — this is
categorically distinct from the carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen they obtain from CO2 and
water. **Macronutrients** (needed in large amounts): **nitrogen** — a component of amino
acids, proteins, nucleic acids, and chlorophyll; **phosphorus** — a component of nucleic
acids, ATP, and cell membranes; **potassium** — activates enzymes and is required for
stomatal guard cell function; **magnesium** — the central atom in the chlorophyll
molecule itself. **Micronutrients** (needed only in trace amounts): iron (as an enzyme
cofactor), manganese, zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum. Plants absorb these mineral ions
AGAINST concentration gradients, via active transport (requiring ATP) through root hair
cells.

Deficiency symptoms differ by nutrient in a specific, diagnostically useful way tied
directly to nutrient **mobility** within the plant. **Nitrogen** is mobile — when
nitrogen is scarce, the plant relocates its existing nitrogen from older leaves to
younger, actively growing tissue, so nitrogen deficiency's characteristic symptom
(chlorosis, yellowing) appears in OLDER leaves first. **Magnesium** deficiency, by
contrast, shows the OPPOSITE pattern: because magnesium is not relocated from older
leaves the way nitrogen is, its deficiency symptom appears in YOUNGER leaves first —
despite magnesium also being essential for chlorophyll. **Phosphorus** deficiency
produces purple discolouration (anthocyanin accumulation); **potassium** deficiency
produces brown leaf margins.

The concept's first central corrective claim: **plants do not "feed from soil" in the
sense of getting the bulk of their mass from it.** Plants obtain the BULK of their mass
from CO2 and water via photosynthesis (the carbon that builds a tree's wood comes from
atmospheric CO2). Soil provides mineral ions in comparatively very small quantities —
essential for building specific molecules (proteins, DNA) but contributing minimal total
mass. A directly testable consequence follows: a plant grown in mineral-deficient but
otherwise optimal conditions will be stunted (unable to build proteins properly) but WILL
STILL PHOTOSYNTHESIZE — demonstrating that mass accumulation (from CO2/water) and mineral
sufficiency (from soil) are genuinely separate requirements, not the same thing measured
two ways.

The second central corrective claim: **"more fertiliser always helps plants grow faster"
is false beyond a certain point.** Excess mineral concentration in soil causes osmotic
stress: when the soil solution becomes MORE concentrated than the solution inside root
cells, the normal water-potential gradient reverses, and water moves OUT of root cells
(via osmosis) rather than in — a process called plasmolysis — wilting the plant even in
objectively wet soil. Over-fertilizing does not simply provide unused surplus nutrients;
it can actively harm the plant through this specific osmotic mechanism.

## Mental Models
- **Mass from air and water, ingredients from soil**: a plant's bulk structural material
  (mostly carbon) comes from CO2 fixed via photosynthesis; soil minerals are more like
  specific, small-quantity ingredients (for proteins, chlorophyll, DNA) than bulk
  building material — confusing "essential" with "the source of mass" is the core error
  to avoid.
- **Mobility determines WHERE deficiency symptoms show up**: a mobile nutrient's
  deficiency symptom appears in older leaves (because the plant actively relocates its
  existing supply to newer growth, sacrificing the older leaves); an immobile nutrient's
  deficiency symptom appears in younger leaves (because there is no relocation
  mechanism to protect new growth) — this single mobility property predicts which leaves
  will show symptoms first.
- **Reversing the normal water-potential gradient is genuinely harmful, not just wasteful**:
  excess fertilizer isn't simply "unused" — it can actively invert the normal
  soil-to-root water flow direction, actively pulling water OUT of the plant.

## Why Students Fail
1. They interpret "plants need soil nutrients" as implying plants get most of their
   substance FROM soil, conflating "essential in small quantities" with "the primary
   mass source."
2. They apply a single, undifferentiated "yellowing = nutrient deficiency" diagnostic
   without tracking which specific leaves are affected, missing that leaf position
   (older vs. younger) is itself diagnostic information tied to nutrient mobility.
3. They assume more of a beneficial input (fertilizer) is always better, without
   considering a specific mechanism (osmotic stress) by which excess input becomes
   actively harmful rather than simply unused.

## Misconceptions

### M1 — "Plants get most of their mass from soil nutrients" (Type 1: Overgeneralization)
**Statement**: Since plants absorb nutrients from soil and these nutrients are described
as essential, most of a plant's physical mass (its bulk, its wood, its bulk tissue) must
come from soil.
**Origin**: Overgeneralizing "soil nutrients are essential for plant function" (true)
into "soil nutrients are the primary source of plant mass" (false), without separately
tracking that mass accumulation happens via photosynthesis (CO2 + water), while soil
nutrients serve a smaller-quantity, specific-molecule-building role.
**Why it persists**: Both claims involve "things a plant needs from its environment,"
and without an explicit mass-accounting distinction (bulk carbon from air/water vs. trace
minerals from soil), the two categories of environmental input blend together.
**Repair**: Present the mineral-deficient-but-otherwise-optimal scenario directly: such a
plant is stunted (protein-building fails) but STILL photosynthesizes and still
accumulates mass from CO2/water — demonstrating that mass accumulation and mineral
sufficiency are separately testable, separate requirements.
**Diagnostic probe**: reinforced by the concept's own misconception_repair content
directly contrasting mass source (CO2/water) against mineral role (building specific
molecules in small quantities).

### M2 — "All nutrient deficiency chlorosis patterns look the same, or nitrogen deficiency shows the same pattern as magnesium deficiency" (Type 4: Notation/mechanism-induced)
**Statement**: Since both nitrogen and magnesium deficiencies cause chlorosis (yellowing)
and both nutrients are needed for chlorophyll-related function, their deficiency symptoms
should appear in the same leaves (either both in older, or both in younger, leaves).
**Origin**: Conflating "both nutrients relate to chlorophyll/greenness" with "both
nutrients behave identically within the plant," without tracking the specific,
distinguishing variable (nutrient mobility) that determines WHERE each deficiency's
symptom actually appears.
**Why it persists**: Without explicitly naming nitrogen as mobile and magnesium as
immobile, and explaining how that specific property determines symptom location, the
shared "causes yellowing" surface similarity obscures a real, diagnostically important
difference.
**Repair**: State the mobility distinction explicitly: nitrogen is relocated by the
plant from older to younger tissue (so deficiency symptoms appear in OLDER leaves, which
are sacrificed); magnesium is not relocated this way (so deficiency symptoms appear in
YOUNGER leaves, since there's no mechanism protecting new growth) — this single property
correctly predicts the location difference.
**Diagnostic probe**: the existing probe-depth short_answer directly presenting a
youngest-leaves-yellow scenario and asking whether it's more consistent with nitrogen or
magnesium deficiency, with distractors directly testing the mobility confusion.

## Analogies
- The bricks-vs-mortar-additives model: CO2 and water are the bulk bricks building a
  plant's structure; soil minerals are more like small, specific additives (rebar,
  fasteners) needed for specific structural functions but contributing negligible total
  material.
- The relocate-or-strand model: a mobile nutrient (nitrogen) is like a company
  relocating its best resources from a closing branch (older leaves) to a growing one
  (younger leaves) when supply runs short; an immobile nutrient (magnesium) is like a
  resource that's stuck at each branch once delivered — the growing branch (younger
  leaves) simply gets none if the overall supply runs short.

## Demonstrations
- Present two chlorosis scenarios side by side (older leaves affected; younger leaves
  affected) and have students assign each to nitrogen or magnesium deficiency using only
  the mobility principle, before revealing the correct assignment.
- Walk the over-fertilization osmotic-stress mechanism explicitly: soil solution
  concentration rises above root cell concentration → water potential gradient reverses
  → water moves out of root cells → wilting, despite wet soil.

## Discovery Questions
- "If a plant's dry mass is measured before and after several weeks of growth in
  mineral-deficient (but otherwise optimal) conditions, would you expect it to gain mass
  from photosynthesis even while showing deficiency symptoms? What does that suggest
  about where plant mass actually comes from?"
- "Nitrogen and magnesium are both needed for chlorophyll-related function, yet their
  deficiencies show up in different leaves. What specific property of each nutrient
  explains this difference?"
- "If more fertiliser is always better, why would a gardener's plant wilt after they
  doubled the fertiliser dose, even though the soil is still wet?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Introduce the macronutrient/micronutrient distinction and each nutrient's specific
   biochemical role before discussing deficiency symptoms.
2. Present the plants-don't-get-mass-from-soil correction directly, using the
   mineral-deficient-but-photosynthesizing scenario as concrete evidence.
3. Introduce nutrient mobility (nitrogen mobile, magnesium immobile) as the specific
   variable determining WHERE deficiency symptoms appear, using the two-scenario sorting
   exercise.
4. Present the over-fertilization osmotic-stress mechanism explicitly, tracing the
   soil-solution-concentration-reversal logic step by step.
5. Close by connecting active transport (ATP-dependent mineral uptake against a gradient)
   back to the general active-transport principles established in
   `bio.cell.cell-membrane-transport`.

## Tutor Actions
- If a student says plants get most of their mass from soil: ask them to predict what
  would happen to a mineral-deficient plant's mass over time if it can still
  photosynthesize, testing whether they can separate the two requirements.
- If a student cannot distinguish nitrogen and magnesium deficiency patterns: ask them
  to state which nutrient is mobile and which is immobile before assigning a symptom
  location.
- If a student assumes more fertilizer is always better: ask them to trace what happens
  to the soil-solution/root-cell water-potential relationship as fertilizer concentration
  increases.

## Voice Teaching Notes
Say "mass from air and water, ingredients from soil" whenever plant nutrition comes up,
to keep the mass-source distinction explicit. Say "which nutrient is mobile?" as the
standing diagnostic question whenever a chlorosis-pattern scenario is presented.

## Assessment Signals
- **Early recovery**: after the two-scenario sorting exercise, correctly assigns a novel
  deficiency scenario to the correct nutrient using the mobility principle, without
  needing this restated.
- **Fragile**: can state "plants get mass mainly from CO2 and water" as a memorized fact
  but still describes soil as the primary nutrient/mass source when discussing plant
  growth in their own words.
- **Deep gap**: continues to conflate nitrogen and magnesium deficiency patterns after
  the mobility distinction has been explicitly taught — indicates the mobility-based
  reasoning was never actually adopted, only "different nutrients, different symptoms"
  was retained without the specific mechanism.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
For M1, do not just restate "mass comes from CO2 and water" — ask the student to predict
the outcome of the mineral-deficient-but-optimal-CO2/water scenario themselves (stunted
but still photosynthesizing) before revealing the actual result. For M2, present a
DIFFERENT pair of nutrients (not nitrogen/magnesium) and ask the student to predict
which one's deficiency would show in older vs. younger leaves, given only each
nutrient's mobility, testing whether the underlying principle (not the specific
nitrogen/magnesium example) has transferred.

## Memory Hooks
- "Mass from air and water. Minerals build specific molecules, not bulk."
- "Mobile nutrient (nitrogen): symptoms in older leaves. Immobile (magnesium): symptoms
  in younger leaves."
- "Too much fertiliser reverses the water gradient — wilting even in wet soil."
