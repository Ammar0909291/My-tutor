## Learning Objective
The student can correctly classify a given ecosystem service into ONE of the FOUR
categories (provisioning, regulating, supporting, cultural), correctly explain WHY
non-market valuation approaches are needed specifically for services with no direct
market price (rather than assuming all ecosystem value can be captured by market
transactions), and correctly describe environmental impact assessment as the APPLIED
framework connecting ecological science to actual policy decisions.

## Core Understanding
**Ecosystem services** — the benefits humans obtain from ecosystems — are classified
into FOUR distinct categories, and correctly assigning a given service to its category
requires attending to its SPECIFIC functional nature. **Provisioning services** are
DIRECT, tangible products extracted from ecosystems (food, timber, fresh water,
medicinal compounds). **Regulating services** are benefits from an ecosystem's
REGULATION of natural processes (climate regulation via carbon sequestration, flood
control via wetland water absorption, pollination of crops, water purification).
**Supporting services** are the UNDERLYING ecological processes NECESSARY for
producing all other service categories, but not directly consumed by humans themselves
(nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary production) — the essential distinguishing
feature of supporting services is that they enable the OTHER three categories rather
than providing a benefit humans experience directly. **Cultural services** are
NON-MATERIAL benefits (recreational, aesthetic, spiritual, educational value people
derive from ecosystems). The classification task requires checking which SPECIFIC
functional role a given service plays, since services that seem superficially similar
can belong to different categories depending on this functional distinction.

Many ecosystem services have NO direct market price (nobody pays a market rate
specifically for "pollination service" or "flood control"), which creates a specific
ECONOMIC PROBLEM: standard market mechanisms systematically UNDERVALUE (or assign
literally zero measured value to) these services, even when their actual contribution
to human wellbeing is substantial. This is precisely WHY **non-market valuation
approaches** (methods such as estimating the cost of an artificial replacement for a
lost service, or surveying what people would be willing to pay to preserve it) are
NEEDED specifically for services lacking a market price — the goal of these
approaches is to make the ECONOMIC magnitude of these otherwise-invisible services
visible to decision-makers who would otherwise implicitly treat them as worth zero.
Alongside these economic valuation approaches, students must also recognise
NON-ECONOMIC valuation (ethical, cultural, intrinsic-value arguments for ecosystem
protection that do not attempt to express value in monetary terms at all) as a
genuinely separate, complementary approach, not merely a weaker substitute for
economic valuation.

**Environmental impact assessment (EIA)** is the APPLIED framework that CONNECTS
ecological science to actual policy and development decisions — it is the formal,
structured process by which the likely ecological consequences of a proposed
development or policy action are systematically evaluated BEFORE the decision is
made, so that decision-makers have concrete ecological evidence (rather than
assumption or advocacy alone) to weigh alongside economic and social considerations.
The essential point students must grasp: EIA is not itself a scientific research
programme discovering new ecological knowledge — it is the practical MECHANISM by
which existing ecological science and ecosystem-service valuation get systematically
applied to a specific, concrete real-world decision.

## Mental Models
- **The four-drawers filing system for ecosystem services**: provisioning (things you
  can hold), regulating (processes that keep conditions stable), supporting
  (foundational processes enabling everything else), cultural (non-material,
  experiential value) — every service goes into exactly one drawer based on its
  specific functional role.
- **The invisible-price-tag model for non-market valuation**: services with no market
  price are like goods with an invisible price tag — non-market valuation methods are
  specifically designed to make that invisible tag visible to decision-makers who
  would otherwise treat the good as free.
- **The bridge-not-the-lab model for environmental impact assessment**: EIA is the
  bridge connecting ecological science (the lab) to an actual policy decision (the
  other side), not a place where new ecological science is generated.

## Why Students Fail
- They confuse supporting services with the other three categories, missing that
  supporting services specifically ENABLE the other categories rather than providing
  a benefit humans experience directly themselves.
- They assume a service with no market price has no real economic value, missing that
  non-market valuation approaches exist precisely to make this otherwise-invisible
  value measurable.
- They treat environmental impact assessment as itself a scientific discovery process
  rather than as the APPLIED mechanism connecting existing ecological science to
  actual policy decisions.

### M1 — "Supporting services are just another kind of provisioning or regulating service" (Type 1: Overgeneralization)
**Statement**: Supporting services (nutrient cycling, soil formation, primary
production) are classified as provisioning or regulating services, without
recognising their SPECIFIC distinguishing feature: they enable the OTHER three
service categories rather than providing a benefit humans experience directly.
**Origin**: Overgeneralizing from the shared broad category ("ecosystems provide
useful things") to an incorrect inference that all four categories work the same way,
without separately tracking that supporting services occupy a functionally different,
FOUNDATIONAL role (enabling other services) rather than being a directly-experienced
benefit category themselves.
**Why it persists**: Without an explicit check for "does this service benefit humans
directly, or does it enable other services that then benefit humans," a supporting
service's foundational, indirect role can be missed.
**Repair**: State the classification test explicitly: provisioning, regulating, and
cultural services all provide some DIRECT benefit humans experience; supporting
services instead ENABLE those other three categories (nutrient cycling underlies the
soil fertility that makes provisioning of food possible) without themselves being
directly experienced as a benefit — classification requires checking which of these
two functional roles a given service plays.
**Verification-of-death**: given a specific ecosystem process (e.g., nitrogen
fixation) and asked to classify it, the learner correctly identifies it as a
supporting service by checking whether it directly benefits humans or instead enables
other services.

### M2 — "A service with no market price has no real economic value" (Type 4: Notation-Induced)
**Statement**: An ecosystem service with no direct market price (nobody buys or sells
it directly) is treated as having no real economic value or as being economically
"free," rather than recognising that non-market valuation approaches exist
specifically because such services CAN have substantial real economic value that
standard markets simply fail to price.
**Origin**: The absence of an observable market PRICE is mistaken for the absence of
economic VALUE, conflating "has no price tag" with "is worth nothing," without
separately tracking that price and value can diverge when no market mechanism exists
to set the price.
**Why it persists**: Without an explicit distinction between price (a market
artifact) and value (the actual contribution to wellbeing), "no price" can seem to
mean "no value."
**Repair**: State explicitly that non-market valuation approaches (replacement-cost
estimation, willingness-to-pay surveys) exist SPECIFICALLY to measure the real
economic value of services lacking a market price — a wetland's flood-control
service has no market price, but its economic value can be substantial when measured
by the cost of an artificial flood-control alternative it replaces.
**Verification-of-death**: given a described ecosystem service with no market price
(e.g., natural pollination of a crop), the learner correctly proposes a non-market
valuation approach (e.g., the cost of manual/artificial pollination as a replacement)
to estimate its real economic value.

## Analogies
- The four-drawers filing system for ecosystem services (see Mental Models):

  functional role.
- The invisible-price-tag model for non-market valuation (see Mental Models): making

- The bridge-not-the-lab model for environmental impact assessment (see Mental

  science.

## Demonstrations
- Present the nitrogen-fixation classification exercise and ask the student to
  determine which of the four categories it belongs to, applying the direct-benefit-
  versus-enabling-role test.
- Present the natural-pollination-no-market-price scenario and ask the student to
  propose a non-market valuation approach to estimate its economic value.

## Discovery Questions
- "Does nutrient cycling directly benefit YOU the way eating a fish (provisioning) or
  visiting a park (cultural) does? If not, what role is it playing instead?"
- "If nobody buys or sells 'natural pollination' directly, does that mean it has no
  real economic value? How could you estimate what it's actually worth?"
- "Does an environmental impact assessment discover new ecological facts, or does it
  apply facts we already know to a specific real-world decision?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Introduce the four ecosystem service categories together with the classification
   test, directly correcting the supporting-services misconception using the
   nitrogen-fixation exercise.
2. Introduce economic and non-economic valuation approaches, directly correcting the
   no-price-means-no-value misconception using the natural-pollination scenario.
3. Introduce environmental impact assessment as the applied bridge from ecological
   science to policy decisions.
4. Close by connecting all three themes back to the general principle that applied
   ecology translates ecological understanding into decision-relevant, actionable
   terms.

## Tutor Actions
- If a student misclassifies a supporting service: ask them whether the service
  directly benefits humans or instead enables other services.
- If a student treats an unpriced service as worthless: ask them to propose a
  non-market valuation method for it.
- If a student treats EIA as generating new science: ask them what specific existing
  ecological knowledge it is applying to a specific decision.

## Voice Teaching Notes
Say "direct benefit or enabling role?" whenever ecosystem service classification
comes up, to keep the supporting-services distinction explicit. Say "priceless isn't
valueless" whenever non-market valuation is discussed.
