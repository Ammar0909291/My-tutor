## Learning Objective

The learner can: state that fungal cell walls are made of chitin, not
cellulose; explain absorptive heterotrophy (secreting digestive enzymes
externally, then absorbing the products) as fungi's mode of nutrition,
distinct from a plant's autotrophy; describe the mycelium/hyphae body
structure and why it maximises surface area for absorption; and
correctly state that fungi are phylogenetically closer to animals than
to plants, despite superficial plant-like growth habits.

## Core Understanding

Fungi are eukaryotes with cell walls built from chitin — the same
tough polysaccharide found in arthropod exoskeletons — rather than the
cellulose that defines plant cell walls, and this single chemical
difference underlies fungi's entire distinct biology. Fungi are
heterotrophs, but not in the animal sense of ingesting solid food
internally: they digest EXTERNALLY, secreting enzymes into their
surrounding substrate and then absorbing the resulting broken-down
products directly through their cell walls — absorptive heterotrophy.
The fungal body, the mycelium, is a branching network of thread-like
filaments called hyphae, a structure that maximises surface area for
this external absorption, much as a root system maximises a plant's
absorptive surface. Fungi reproduce via spores, sexually or asexually,
and occupy three major ecological roles: decomposers (breaking down
dead organic matter and recycling its nutrients back into ecosystems —
a direct link to nutrient cycling), pathogens (causing disease in
plants, animals, or other fungi), and mycorrhizal partners (forming
symbiotic associations with plant roots, extending the plant's
effective root surface area in exchange for sugars). Despite their
historically plant-adjacent classification (both are stationary and
grow upward), molecular phylogenetics places fungi as the sister group
to animals — together called Opisthokonta — reflecting shared ancestry
that has nothing to do with either group's superficial resemblance to
plants.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "fungi are non-green plants"**: the arriving,
  appearance-driven intuition — stationary, upward-growing, therefore
  plant-like.
- **Intermediate model — "fungi differ from plants somehow, but the
  exact biological reason is unclear"**: the learner senses fungi are
  classified separately without yet having a specific criterion (cell
  wall chemistry, nutrition mode) to point to.
- **Advanced model — "fungi defined by chitin walls and absorptive
  heterotrophy, genuinely distinct from plants on both counts"**: the
  learner correctly states both defining differences and applies them
  to classify an unfamiliar organism.
- **Expert model — "fungi as evolutionarily closer to animals than
  plants, despite superficial appearance"**: the learner holds the
  molecular-phylogenetic relationship (Opisthokonta) as the deeper truth
  underlying the surface-level classification, connecting this to the
  general principle (from `bio.found.classification-need`) that shared
  ancestry, not appearance, is classification's actual basis.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner who still calls fungi "non-green
  plants" (beginner model, unrepaired) should not be pushed toward the
  fungi-closer-to-animals expert fact — without first accepting fungi
  are a genuinely distinct kingdom by mechanism (cell wall, nutrition),
  the animal-relatedness claim will seem like an arbitrary, disconnected
  trivia fact rather than a natural consequence of already-understood
  biology.

## Why Students Fail

Fungi share the two most visually salient features of plants —
staying in one place and growing upward — while their actual defining
differences (chitin vs. cellulose cell walls; absorptive vs.
photosynthetic nutrition) are invisible to casual observation and
require either chemical testing or explicit instruction to notice; a
learner reasonably classifies by the visible, available evidence
(stationary growth habit) rather than the underlying, unobservable
mechanism.

## Misconceptions

- **M1 — "Fungi are a type of plant" (Type 2, perceptual intuition)**:
  built from a lifetime of casual visual observation (mushrooms, moulds
  appear stationary and grow upward like plants), with no explicit rule
  ever taught that says "grows upward and stays put = plant" — the
  belief forms from raw, unlabelled perceptual experience rather than a
  statable rule, matching Type 2's signature: it can persist even in a
  learner who can correctly state the chitin/cellulose distinction when
  asked directly, because the perceptual pull competes with, rather than
  is simply replaced by, the correct model. Characteristic phrase:
  "mushrooms are non-green plants" or "fungi are just plants without
  chlorophyll." Verbatim detection probes (seed corpus): "What
  distinguishes the fungal cell wall from the plant cell wall?" and
  "Based on molecular evidence, fungi are most closely related to which
  kingdom?" Recovery path: per Type 2's repair implication, verbal
  restatement of the cell-wall/nutrition distinction is insufficient on
  its own — pair it with the concrete, checkable fact that fungi store
  glycogen (like animals do), not starch (like plants do), giving the
  learner a second, independent, checkable data point beyond the
  cell-wall chemistry alone. Verification-of-death: given a new fungal
  example, the learner correctly explains its nutrition mode
  (absorptive heterotrophy) without appearance (stationary, upward
  growth) entering the justification — and, per Type 2's documented
  regrowth pattern, this should be re-checked periodically rather than
  assumed permanently repaired after one correct answer.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — a food delivery service that "cooks" outside, then
  brings in only the finished meal**: a fungus secretes digestive
  "cooking" enzymes outside its own body, into its surroundings, and
  only absorbs the finished, broken-down product — unlike an animal,
  which brings whole food inside first and digests it internally.
- **Alternative — a root system, extended and thread-like**: mycelium's
  branching hyphae maximise absorptive surface area much as a plant's
  root system does, but for absorbing digested organic matter rather
  than water and minerals.
- **Story analogy — the glycogen/starch storage contrast**: fungi store
  glycogen, exactly as animals do, while plants store starch — a single,
  checkable biochemical fact that cuts cleanly against the "fungi are
  plants" intuition.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT say "fungi are like plants that lost their
  chlorophyll"**: this framing implies fungi are a degraded or
  incomplete version of a plant, reinforcing rather than countering the
  "fungi are plants" misconception — fungi are not former plants; they
  are a distinct kingdom with their own evolutionary history, closer to
  animals.

## Demonstrations

- **Discrimination demonstration — glycogen vs. starch**: present the
  storage-molecule contrast (fungi: glycogen, like animals; plants:
  starch) as a direct, checkable test distinguishing the two kingdoms,
  independent of appearance.
- **Teacher-demo — external digestion visualised**: describe (or show a
  diagram of) a fungus secreting enzymes into a piece of dead wood,
  breaking it down externally, before any nutrient enters the fungal
  cells — contrasted explicitly with an animal eating first, digesting
  second.

## Discovery Questions

A genuine discovery design fits: **Need** — "a mushroom doesn't move,
grows upward, and has no green colour. Is it a strange kind of plant?"
**Playground** — the learner examines mushroom features against known
plant and animal features. **Invention** — the learner proposes their
own classification, likely leaning toward "plant" from appearance
alone. **Collision** — presented with the storage-molecule fact
(glycogen, like animals, not starch) — a genuinely surprising piece of
evidence pointing the opposite direction from appearance. **Formalization**
— chitin cell walls and absorptive heterotrophy are introduced as the
actual defining criteria, and the Opisthokonta (fungi-animal) grouping
is named. **Compression** — the learner classifies a new, unfamiliar
fungal example (e.g. a mould) using the criteria rather than appearance.

## Teaching Sequence

The chitin/cellulose and nutrition-mode distinctions (M1's primary
repair path) should be established before the fungi-closer-to-animals
fact is introduced — the animal-relatedness claim only makes sense as a
consequence of the concept's other, mechanism-based facts (shared
glycogen storage, shared heterotrophy), not as an isolated trivia
statement to memorise first.

## Tutor Actions

From `../../teaching-actions/`: **Definition/Orientation** (chitin
walls, absorptive heterotrophy, mycelium structure) → **Discrimination**
(glycogen vs. starch storage) → **Error Analysis** (both misconception
