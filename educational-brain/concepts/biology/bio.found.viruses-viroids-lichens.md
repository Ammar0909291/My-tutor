# Viruses, Viroids and Lichens — `bio.found.viruses-viroids-lichens`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `bio.found.viruses-viroids-lichens` (canonical
  biology KG)
- **Curriculum location**: biology / foundations
- **Prerequisites**: `bio.found.five-kingdom` — the load-bearing part is
  the settled five-kingdom system, since this concept's whole point is
  examining three entities that sit deliberately OUTSIDE that
  classification.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `bio.micro.microbial-diversity`,
  `bio.micro.viral-replication` (both currently unauthored in this
  Educational Brain).
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.70 · **Est. hours**: 3

## Learning Objective

The learner can: state why viruses are acellular and therefore not
placed in any of the five kingdoms; describe a typical virus's minimal
structure (nucleic acid genome enclosed in a protein capsid); explain
why viroids are structurally even simpler than viruses (naked RNA, no
capsid); and correctly identify a lichen as a mutualistic symbiosis
between a fungus and a photosynthetic partner, not a virus or any
single organism.

## Core Understanding

Viruses, viroids, and lichens are three biologically distinct entities
that share only the property of sitting outside the standard
five-kingdom classification, and conflating them is a category error.
Viruses are acellular — not built from cells at all — consisting
minimally of a nucleic acid genome (DNA or RNA, but never both in the
same virus, and either single- or double-stranded) enclosed in a
protein coat called a capsid; outside a host cell, a virus is
essentially an inert particle with no metabolism of its own, and it
only becomes active by hijacking a host cell's machinery to replicate.
Viroids are structurally simpler still — a naked, circular RNA strand
with no protein coat at all — and cause disease exclusively in plants.
Lichens are an entirely different kind of thing: not acellular, not
viral, but a genuine mutualistic symbiosis between a fungus (providing
structure and water retention) and a photosynthetic partner, either an
alga or a cyanobacterium (providing sugars from photosynthesis) — two
full, independently-classifiable organisms living together as one
functional unit.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "three unusual, hard-to-classify things lumped
  together"**: the learner accepts the three are grouped in this
  concept without yet understanding each is genuinely different from
  the others in kind, not just in degree of oddness.
- **Intermediate model — "since they're grouped together, they must be
  similar kinds of things"**: the direct substrate of the misconception
  below — treating co-occurrence in one lesson as evidence of shared
  biological nature. Upgrade trigger: the lichen case specifically,
  which shares essentially nothing structurally with a virus.
- **Advanced model — "three genuinely distinct categories: acellular
  infectious particles (viruses/viroids) vs. a two-organism symbiosis
  (lichens)"**: the learner correctly separates all three and states
  what makes each unique.
- **Expert model — "the five-kingdom system's boundary cases as evidence
  the classification is itself a human tool, not a perfect map of
  nature"**: the learner understands that entities like viruses
  genuinely resist clean categorisation, and that this is informative
  about the limits of any classification system rather than a defect
  to be argued away.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner who still assumes lichens are
  virus-like (intermediate model, unrepaired) should not be pushed
  toward reasoning about classification-system limits generally — they
  will import the wrong specific example (lichens) into that broader
  reasoning.

## Why Students Fail

All three entities are introduced together specifically because none
fits the five-kingdom system, and a learner reasonably (but incorrectly)
infers that shared exclusion from a system implies shared internal
similarity — nothing about the grouping itself signals that the three
are excluded for entirely different reasons (acellularity for two of
them, being a composite of two full organisms for the third).

## Misconceptions

No Blueprint exists yet for this concept; both misconceptions classified
directly against the concept's own seed content using the birth-
taxonomy diagnostic procedure (`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

- **M1 — "Viruses are alive because they can reproduce" (Type 1,
  overgeneralization)**: the learner correctly knows reproduction is
  one of the characteristics of life and overgeneralizes "can
  reproduce" into "reproduces," when in fact a virus is REPLICATED
  entirely by a host cell's own machinery, energy, and enzymes — the
  virus contributes only its genetic instructions, with no metabolism
  of its own. Matches Type 1's signature: the underlying association
  (reproduction ↔ life) is genuinely true elsewhere, extended past a
  case where the "reproduction" observed is not self-directed at all.
  Characteristic phrase: "viruses can reproduce, so they must be alive."
  Verbatim detection probe (seed corpus, `misconception_probe`): "A
  student argues 'viruses are alive because they can reproduce.' What
  is the flaw in this reasoning?" Recovery path: the concept's own
  photocopier analogy — a photocopier can copy itself if fed the right
  program and materials, but the photocopier is not alive; the same
  logic applies to a virus copied by a host cell's machinery. Reinforce
  with the "edge of life" framing: biologists deliberately treat
  viruses as neither clearly alive nor clearly non-living, rather than
  forcing a binary answer. Verification-of-death: the learner, asked
  whether a virus is alive, spontaneously distinguishes "is replicated
  by something else" from "actively reproduces itself," rather than
  answering from the single fact "it multiplies."
- **M2 — "A lichen is a type of virus, since both are described as
  simple/atypical" (Type 3, language contamination)**: the word
  "simple" or "atypical," used loosely to describe both viruses and
  lichens in casual conversation about "unusual living things," collides
  with the fact that they are structurally unrelated — a lichen is not
  simple at all in the relevant sense; it is a composite of two full,
  independently-classifiable organisms. Matches Type 3's diagnostic
  test: rephrasing away from "simple/unusual" toward "what is it made
  of" resolves the confusion, since the underlying content (two organisms
  living together) is not itself hard to grasp once separated from the
  vague "simple" label. Characteristic phrase: calling a lichen "a type
  of virus" or "basically a virus, but for plants/rocks." Recovery path:
  name the collision explicitly — "'simple' doesn't mean 'the same kind
  of thing'" — and state plainly what a lichen actually is (two
  organisms: a fungus and an alga or cyanobacterium, working together).
  Verification-of-death: the learner, given a new grouping of "unusual"
  biological entities, checks each one's actual composition rather than
  assuming shared category membership from a shared "unusual" label.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — a photocopier copying itself with someone else
  operating it (the concept's own worked example)**: the copy happens,
  but the copier isn't doing the work or providing the energy — directly
  parallels viral replication by a host cell.
- **Alternative — a business partnership, for lichens**: two separate
  companies (a fungus, an alga) each bring something the other lacks
  (structure/water retention; food from photosynthesis) and function
  together as one enterprise while remaining two distinct entities —
  useful for the "two organisms, one functional unit" structure.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT say "viruses, viroids, and lichens are all
  simple, primitive life forms"**: "primitive" and "simple" applied
  uniformly across all three obscures that they are simple (or not) in
  completely different senses, and directly feeds M2.

## Demonstrations

- **Discrimination demonstration — sort the three**: present short
  descriptions of a virus, a viroid, and a lichen (scrambled) and have
  the learner match each to its correct defining feature (capsid + a
  nucleic acid; naked RNA only; two organisms in symbiosis) before being
  told.
- **Teacher-demo — the edge-of-life framing**: present the seven
  characteristics of life (from `bio.found.characteristics-of-life`,
  already authored) and walk through which a virus has and lacks OUTSIDE
  a host cell, demonstrating why biologists resist a simple yes/no
  answer.

## Discovery Questions

A genuine discovery design fits: **Need** — "a virus can multiply
inside your cells. Does that make it alive?" **Playground** — the
learner checks the virus against the seven characteristics of life one
by one. **Invention** — the learner notices the virus fails several
characteristics (no independent metabolism, no homeostasis) outside a
host. **Collision** — presented with the "but it CAN reproduce" counter-
argument, forcing a closer look at WHO is doing the reproducing.
**Formalization** — the "edge of life" framing is introduced explicitly.
**Compression** — the learner applies the same characteristics-of-life
check to a viroid and to a lichen, discovering each requires a
DIFFERENT verdict for different reasons.

## Teaching Sequence

M1 (virus alive-because-reproduces) should be addressed using the
already-secure seven-characteristics-of-life framework from
`bio.found.characteristics-of-life` before M2 (lichen-as-virus) is
introduced — M1's repair reinforces "check the whole picture, not one
convenient fact," which directly supports M2's repair (checking a
lichen's actual composition rather than a loose "simple/unusual" label).

## Tutor Actions

From `../../teaching-actions/`: **Definition/Orientation** (three
distinct entities, defining features each) → **Error Analysis** (both
misconception probes) → **Classification/Sorting** (the sort-the-three
discrimination demonstration). **What doesn't fit**: introducing all
three under one umbrella term ("unusual life forms") without immediately
distinguishing their structurally unrelated natures — this framing is
exactly what produces M2.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Listen for "viruses are alive because they reproduce" stated
unprompted, with no qualification about WHO does the reproducing — M1's
clearest verbal signature. Listen for "lichen" and "virus" used in the
same breath as if related — M2's signature. The load-bearing sentence:
"a virus is copied, not reproducing itself — and a lichen is actually
TWO organisms, not one simple thing" — delivered as one connected
statement tying both repairs together. Channel-reality limits owned by
`../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Likeliest utterance: "but it multiplies, doesn't that mean it's alive?"
or confusing a lichen with a virus (not distress-shaped — foundational,
low-stakes concept). Concept-specific smaller question for M1: "when
the virus is copied, whose ribosomes and energy are actually doing the
work — the virus's, or the host cell's?" Concept-specific smaller
question for M2: "a lichen is made of two things living together — a
fungus and something else. What is that something else, and does a
virus have anything like it?" Generic recovery machinery owned by
`../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: a new "is it alive" borderline case, correctly reasoned
  through the seven characteristics.
- **Far**: recognising the same "shared unusual status doesn't imply
  shared kind" structure elsewhere (e.g. two rare medical conditions
  grouped in a textbook chapter for being uncommon are not necessarily
  related in mechanism).
- **Real-world**: understanding why antiviral drugs work completely
  differently from antibiotics (a consequence of viruses being acellular
  and dependent on host machinery, unlike bacteria).
- **Expert transfer**: on meeting any newly discovered biological
  entity resisting easy classification, the learner spontaneously
  checks its actual structure and mechanism rather than forcing it into
  an existing category by surface resemblance.
