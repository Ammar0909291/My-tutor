## Learning Objective
The student can correctly identify the contraceptive pill's primary mechanism as
ovulation suppression (not implantation prevention), correctly identify condoms as the
only contraceptive method also protecting against STIs, and correctly apply the
12-month clinical threshold for infertility rather than a shorter, intuitive timeframe.

## Core Understanding
Reproductive health encompasses the full physical, mental, and social well-being related
to the reproductive system — explicitly NOT merely the absence of disease. Contraceptive
methods work through several distinct mechanisms. **Barrier methods** (condoms,
diaphragm) physically prevent sperm from reaching the egg. **Hormonal methods** (the
pill, patch, injection, implant) use synthetic oestrogen/progesterone to prevent
ovulation, alter cervical mucus, and thin the endometrium. **IUDs** work via either
copper (toxic to sperm) or hormonal mechanisms. **Emergency contraception** uses
high-dose progesterone specifically to DELAY ovulation.

**Sexually transmitted infections (STIs)** divide into two categories with different
treatment implications: bacterial STIs (chlamydia, gonorrhoea) are antibiotic-treatable;
viral STIs (HIV, herpes, HPV) are manageable but NOT curable. A single fact deserves
emphasis for its practical significance: **condoms provide the only STI protection among
contraceptive methods** — every other contraceptive method listed prevents pregnancy
through mechanisms entirely unrelated to pathogen transmission, and provides no STI
protection at all.

**Infertility** has a precise clinical definition: failure to conceive after **12 months**
of unprotected intercourse — not a shorter, more casual timeframe. Its causes include low
sperm count, blocked Fallopian tubes, ovulatory dysfunction, and endometriosis. **IVF**
(in-vitro fertilisation) addresses some causes of infertility: eggs are harvested after
hormonal stimulation, fertilised in a laboratory setting, and the resulting embryo is
implanted.

The concept's central corrective claim, precisely stated: **the contraceptive pill's
PRIMARY mechanism is preventing ovulation, not preventing implantation.** A common
misconception holds that the pill works mainly by preventing a fertilised egg from
implanting — this gets the primary mechanism wrong. The pill's main effect is: no egg is
released (ovulation suppressed), so fertilisation cannot occur in the first place; it
also has secondary effects (thickening cervical mucus to reduce sperm penetration,
thinning the endometrium to further reduce implantation probability if fertilisation
somehow still occurred), but these are secondary mechanisms layered on top of the
primary one. When taken perfectly, the pill is over 99% effective, PRIMARILY through
ovulation suppression specifically.

A closely related, second corrective claim addresses emergency contraception directly:
**"the morning-after pill is an abortifacient" is false.** Plan B's primary mechanism is
identical in kind to the regular pill's: delaying or inhibiting ovulation, thereby
preventing fertilisation — it does NOT terminate an already-established pregnancy. This
also explains a specific, otherwise-puzzling clinical fact: if ovulation has already
occurred by the time emergency contraception is taken, its effectiveness drops
dramatically — not because it failed to terminate an existing pregnancy, but because it
missed its actual prevention window (ovulation had already happened, so there was no
ovulation left to delay or prevent).

## Mental Models
- **Prevent release, not remove the result**: both the regular pill and emergency
  contraception work "upstream" — preventing an egg from being released in the first
  place — rather than "downstream," by acting on a fertilised egg or established
  pregnancy; this upstream framing explains both their primary mechanism and their
  failure mode (missing the ovulation window).
- **STI protection is a completely separate property from pregnancy prevention**: a
  contraceptive method's pregnancy-prevention effectiveness and its STI-prevention
  effectiveness are two independent properties — a highly effective contraceptive
  (like the pill) can simultaneously offer ZERO STI protection, and this is not a
  contradiction.
- **Twelve months, not an intuitive shorter period**: the clinical infertility threshold
  is a specific, defined number (12 months) precisely because natural, healthy variation
  in conception timing is wide enough that shorter periods without success are not yet
  diagnostically meaningful.

## Why Students Fail
1. They assume the pill's mechanism must be "stopping the fertilised egg" (an
   intuitively vivid, action-oriented picture) rather than "preventing the egg from ever
   being released" (a less visually dramatic but actually primary mechanism).
2. They assume any effective contraceptive method should also protect against
   infections, since "protection" feels like it should be a single undifferentiated
   property, rather than tracking that pregnancy prevention and pathogen-transmission
   prevention are mechanistically unrelated.
3. They apply an intuitive, shorter timeframe (weeks or a few months) to judge whether
   a couple's conception difficulty is clinically concerning, rather than the specific,
   defined 12-month threshold.

## Misconceptions

### M1 — "The contraceptive pill prevents pregnancy primarily by preventing implantation" (Type 4: Notation/mechanism-induced)
**Statement**: The pill's main way of preventing pregnancy is stopping a fertilised egg
from implanting in the uterine wall, rather than preventing ovulation from occurring at
all.
**Origin**: The pill is introduced alongside several distinct effects (ovulation
suppression, cervical mucus thickening, endometrial thinning) without a clear
PRIMARY/secondary hierarchy stated, allowing the more dramatic-sounding "blocks
implantation" effect to be mistaken for the main mechanism.
**Why it persists**: Without an explicit statement that no egg is released in the first
place under normal correct use, "preventing implantation" can feel like the most direct,
final-step explanation for how pregnancy is avoided.
**Repair**: State the mechanism hierarchy explicitly: primary mechanism is ovulation
suppression (no egg released, so fertilisation cannot occur at all); the cervical-mucus
and endometrial effects are secondary, additional safeguards layered on top, relevant
only in the rare case ovulation still occurs.
**Diagnostic probe**: the existing misconception_probe asking for the pill's primary
mechanism, with the prevents-implantation distractor flagged to this misconception.

### M2 — "Emergency contraception (the morning-after pill) is an abortifacient, or works after fertilisation has already occurred" (Type 1: Overgeneralization)
**Statement**: Since emergency contraception is taken after unprotected sex has already
occurred, it must work by terminating a pregnancy that has already begun, rather than by
preventing conception from occurring at all.
**Origin**: Overgeneralizing from the TIMING of emergency contraception's use (after
sex has already happened) to an incorrect inference about its MECHANISM (must therefore
act on an already-fertilised egg or established pregnancy), without registering that its
actual mechanism (delaying/inhibiting ovulation) can still act BEFORE fertilisation even
though the drug is taken after intercourse.
**Why it persists**: The "after the fact" timing of taking the pill relative to
intercourse can be conflated with acting "after the fact" relative to fertilisation or
implantation, when in fact ovulation itself may not yet have occurred by the time the
pill is taken.
**Repair**: State explicitly that emergency contraception's mechanism is the same in
kind as the regular pill's (delaying/inhibiting ovulation) — its effectiveness depends
specifically on whether ovulation has ALREADY occurred by the time it's taken; if
ovulation already happened, effectiveness drops because the prevention window was
missed, not because the drug failed to terminate an existing pregnancy.
**Diagnostic probe**: reinforced by the existing MCQ's broader mechanism-hierarchy
content (discussed above under M1) and the concept's own misconception_repair content
directly addressing the abortifacient framing.

## Analogies
- The locked-door-vs-eviction model: the pill (and emergency contraception) work like
  locking the door before anyone tries to enter (preventing ovulation/fertilisation),
  not like evicting someone who has already moved in (terminating an established
  pregnancy) — the timing of the intervention relative to the whole process matters more
  than the timing relative to the specific act of intercourse.
- The umbrella-vs-vaccine model: a contraceptive method (like the pill) is like an
  umbrella that keeps you dry from rain (pregnancy) but does nothing about a completely
  different hazard (infection) — being dry and being germ-free are unrelated properties
  an umbrella was never designed to address.

## Demonstrations
- Present the pill's three effects (ovulation suppression, cervical mucus thickening,
  endometrial thinning) with an explicit primary/secondary hierarchy diagram, rather than
  a flat list, to make the mechanism ranking concrete.
- Walk the emergency-contraception timing scenario explicitly: taken before ovulation
  (delays/prevents it, high effectiveness) versus taken after ovulation has already
  occurred (missed the window, effectiveness drops) — as two distinct timeline branches.

## Discovery Questions
- "If the pill's main job were to stop a fertilised egg from implanting, would ovulation
  still need to be suppressed at all? What does the fact that ovulation IS suppressed
  suggest about which mechanism is actually primary?"
- "Emergency contraception is taken after sex has already happened. Does that necessarily
  mean it acts after FERTILISATION has already happened? What would need to be true
  about ovulation timing for it to still work?"
- "If a couple has been trying to conceive for 8 months, does that already meet the
  clinical definition of infertility? What's the actual defined threshold?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Introduce the contraceptive method categories (barrier, hormonal, IUD, emergency)
   with their general mechanisms before detailing any single method's specific mechanism
   hierarchy.
2. Present the pill's mechanism hierarchy explicitly (primary: ovulation suppression;
   secondary: cervical mucus, endometrial effects), directly correcting the
   implantation-prevention misconception.
3. Present emergency contraception's mechanism as the same in kind as the regular pill's,
   using the before/after-ovulation timeline branches to directly address the
   abortifacient misconception.
4. Introduce STI categories (bacterial, treatable; viral, manageable not curable) and the
   condom-only-STI-protection fact explicitly.
5. Close with the 12-month infertility threshold, using the 8-month scenario to test
   whether the specific defined number (not an intuitive shorter period) has been
   adopted.

## Tutor Actions
- If a student says the pill primarily prevents implantation: ask them whether ovulation
  is suppressed under the pill, and what that suppression would be for if implantation
  prevention were the primary mechanism.
- If a student describes emergency contraception as terminating a pregnancy: ask them
  what specifically would need to be true about ovulation timing for the drug's
  mechanism (delaying ovulation) to still be relevant after intercourse but before
  fertilisation.
- If a student assumes any effective contraceptive also prevents STIs: ask them to name
  the specific mechanism by which their chosen method would need to block pathogen
  transmission, testing whether that mechanism actually exists for that method.

## Voice Teaching Notes
Say "no egg, no fertilisation — that's the main event" whenever the pill's mechanism
comes up, to keep ovulation suppression as the primary, first-mentioned mechanism. Say
"pregnancy prevention and infection prevention are different jobs" whenever
contraceptive method effectiveness is discussed, to keep the two properties
distinguished.

## Assessment Signals
- **Early recovery**: after the mechanism-hierarchy diagram, correctly ranks a novel
  hormonal contraceptive method's effects by primary/secondary mechanism, without
  needing this restated.
- **Fragile**: can state "the pill mainly stops ovulation" as a memorized correction but
  still describes emergency contraception as terminating a pregnancy when discussing it
  in their own words.
- **Deep gap**: continues to apply an intuitive, shorter infertility timeframe (e.g., 3
  months) after the 12-month clinical threshold has been explicitly taught — indicates
  the specific defined number was never actually adopted.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
For M1, do not just restate "ovulation suppression is primary" — ask the student to
explain what purpose ovulation suppression would even serve if implantation prevention
were truly the main mechanism, forcing them to reason through the logical inconsistency
themselves. For M2, present a specific timing scenario (emergency contraception taken
before vs. after ovulation) and ask the student to predict effectiveness in each case,
testing whether the underlying before/after-ovulation mechanism (not just "it's not an
abortifacient") has been adopted.

## Memory Hooks
- "No egg released — that's the pill's main job. Everything else is backup."
- "Condoms: the only method that also blocks infection, not just pregnancy."
- "Twelve months, not a feeling — that's the actual infertility threshold."
