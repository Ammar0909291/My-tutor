## Learning Objective
The student can correctly apply behavioural-ecology principles (kin selection,
reciprocal altruism, parental investment) as HYPOTHESES to be tested against human
behaviour rather than as automatically-confirmed explanations, correctly evaluate
claimed human behavioural universals with appropriate attention to evidence and
controversy, and correctly explain cultural evolution as a SECOND, FASTER inheritance
system operating ALONGSIDE (not replacing) genetic evolution.

## Core Understanding
**Human behavioural ecology and evolutionary psychology** apply the SAME behavioural-
ecology principles already established for other species — kin selection, reciprocal
altruism, parental investment — to HUMAN behaviour, but the essential methodological
point students must grasp is that applying these principles to humans generates
HYPOTHESES that must be tested against evidence, not conclusions that are automatically
TRUE simply because the underlying evolutionary logic is sound elsewhere in biology.
Human behaviour is shaped by a far more complex mix of genetic predisposition,
individual learning, and cultural transmission than most other species' behaviour, so
demonstrating that a proposed evolutionary explanation is LOGICALLY coherent is NOT the
same as demonstrating that it is the ACTUAL explanation for a specific observed human
behaviour — each specific claim requires its own supporting evidence.

Proposed **human behavioural universals** (patterns claimed to appear across virtually
all human cultures, such as certain facial expressions of emotion, kin-based
altruism patterns, or aspects of mate preference) are an active area of genuine
scientific EVIDENCE-GATHERING and CONTROVERSY, not settled fact. Some claimed
universals have substantial cross-cultural evidentiary support; others are contested,
with critics pointing to cross-cultural variation that the claimed universal fails to
account for, or to alternative (non-evolutionary, e.g. purely cultural/economic)
explanations for the same pattern. The essential critical-thinking point: evaluating
any specific universal-behaviour claim requires examining its SPECIFIC supporting
evidence and its SPECIFIC criticisms, rather than accepting or rejecting the entire
category of "evolutionary explanations for human behaviour" wholesale.

**Cultural evolution** is a SECOND system of inheritance operating in humans ALONGSIDE
(not as a replacement for) genetic evolution — ideas, behaviours, and knowledge can be
transmitted from one individual to another (and across generations) through
TEACHING, IMITATION, and communication, entirely independent of genetic inheritance.
The critical distinguishing feature students must grasp is SPEED: cultural evolution
can operate MUCH FASTER than genetic evolution, because a beneficial idea or practice
can spread through an entire population within a single generation via social
transmission, whereas a beneficial genetic variant requires MULTIPLE generations of
differential reproduction to spread. This means significant, rapid changes in human
behaviour can occur WITHOUT any corresponding change in the human gene pool — the two
inheritance systems operate on genuinely different timescales and through genuinely
different mechanisms, and both can be operating on the SAME behaviour simultaneously.

## Mental Models
- **The hypothesis-not-conclusion model**: an evolutionary explanation for a human
  behaviour is a HYPOTHESIS awaiting its own specific evidence, exactly like any other
  scientific hypothesis — sound underlying logic elsewhere in biology does not exempt a
  specific human-behaviour claim from needing its own supporting data.
- **The two-lanes-different-speeds model for genetic vs. cultural evolution**: genetic
  evolution is a slow-moving lane (multi-generational differential reproduction);
  cultural evolution is a fast-moving lane (single-generation social transmission) —
  both lanes run alongside each other, and a behaviour can be shaped by traffic in
  either or both.

## Why Students Fail
- They treat an evolutionary explanation for a human behaviour as automatically true
  once the underlying principle (kin selection, parental investment) is understood,
  missing that EACH specific claim about human behaviour still requires its own
  supporting evidence.
- They treat proposed human behavioural universals as either uniformly well-
  established or uniformly discredited, missing that different specific universal-
  behaviour claims have DIFFERENT levels of evidentiary support and require individual
  evaluation.
- They treat cultural evolution as simply another name for or a byproduct of genetic
  evolution, missing that it is a DISTINCT, MUCH FASTER inheritance system that can
  drive rapid behavioural change with no corresponding genetic change at all.

### M1 — "An evolutionary explanation for human behaviour is automatically correct" (Type 1: Overgeneralization)
**Statement**: A proposed evolutionary explanation for a specific human behaviour
(e.g., a claimed adaptive origin for a particular preference or habit) is accepted as
correct simply because the underlying evolutionary PRINCIPLE (kin selection,
reciprocal altruism, parental investment) is well-supported in biology generally,
without requiring SPECIFIC supporting evidence for that particular human-behaviour
claim.
**Origin**: Overgeneralizing from the correct general validity of behavioural-ecology
principles (demonstrated across many non-human species) to the incorrect inference
that ANY specific human application of these principles must also be correct, without
separately tracking that each specific claim needs its OWN evidentiary support given
humans' additional complexity (culture, individual learning).
**Why it persists**: Without an explicit requirement to check for SPECIFIC supporting
evidence, a logically coherent evolutionary story can feel sufficiently convincing on
its own.
**Repair**: State explicitly that applying behavioural-ecology principles to humans
generates a HYPOTHESIS, not an automatic conclusion — the hypothesis must be tested
against actual evidence (cross-cultural data, controlled studies) specific to that
claim, precisely because human behaviour is also shaped by individual learning and
culture in ways that could produce the SAME observed pattern through a non-genetic
route.
**Verification-of-death**: given two competing explanations for the same human
behaviour (one evolutionary, one purely cultural), the learner correctly states that
determining which is correct (or whether both contribute) requires evidence, rather
than assuming the evolutionary explanation wins by default.

### M2 — "Cultural evolution is just a form of or byproduct of genetic evolution" (Type 3: Language Contamination)
**Statement**: Cultural evolution (the transmission of ideas and behaviours via
teaching/imitation) is understood as a byproduct or slower-acting extension of genetic
evolution, rather than as a DISTINCT inheritance system that can produce rapid
behavioural change entirely independent of, and much faster than, genetic change.
**Origin**: The shared word "evolution" in both terms suggests a single underlying
process to students, importing genetic evolution's slow, multi-generational timescale
onto cultural evolution as well.
**Why it persists**: Without an explicit contrast naming the DIFFERENT transmission
mechanism (social learning vs. differential reproduction) and DIFFERENT timescale
(single-generation vs. multi-generational) of each system, the shared terminology can
obscure the genuine mechanistic difference.
**Repair**: State explicitly that cultural evolution transmits information via
teaching, imitation, and communication — operating potentially within a SINGLE
generation — while genetic evolution transmits information via differential
reproduction across MULTIPLE generations; both systems can act on human behaviour
SIMULTANEOUSLY and INDEPENDENTLY, and a rapid behavioural shift with no time for
genetic change to occur is strong evidence that cultural (not genetic) evolution is
the operating mechanism.
**Verification-of-death**: given a scenario describing a behaviour spreading through
an entire population within a single generation, the learner correctly attributes this
to cultural (not genetic) evolution, citing the mismatch between the observed
timescale and genetic evolution's multi-generational requirement.

## Analogies
- The hypothesis-not-conclusion model (see Mental Models): treating an evolutionary
  story about human behaviour with the same evidentiary skepticism as any other
  scientific hypothesis.
- The two-lanes-different-speeds model for genetic vs. cultural evolution (see Mental
  Models): a slow lane and a fast lane running alongside each other.

## Demonstrations
- Present the two-competing-explanations scenario (evolutionary vs. purely cultural)
  for the same behaviour and ask the student to state what would be needed to
  distinguish between them.
- Present the single-generation behaviour-spread scenario and ask the student to
  attribute it to cultural or genetic evolution, justifying with the timescale
  mismatch.

## Discovery Questions
- "If an evolutionary story for a human behaviour sounds logically coherent, does that
  alone make it TRUE? What else would you need to check?"
- "Could a behaviour spread through an entire population in a single generation via
  genetic evolution alone? Why or why not — and what does that tell you about which
  system produced a rapid change you observe?"
- "Why might different researchers disagree about whether a specific 'human universal'
  is really universal?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Introduce the application of behavioural-ecology principles to human behaviour as
   hypothesis-generating, directly correcting the automatically-correct misconception
   using the two-competing-explanations scenario.
2. Introduce human behavioural universals as a genuinely contested evidentiary domain,
   requiring case-by-case evaluation rather than blanket acceptance or rejection.
3. Introduce cultural evolution as a distinct, faster inheritance system, directly
   correcting the byproduct-of-genetic-evolution misconception using the single-
   generation behaviour-spread scenario.
4. Close by connecting all three themes back to the general principle that human
   behaviour requires more careful, evidence-based, multi-causal analysis than
   behaviour in most other species.

## Tutor Actions
- If a student accepts an evolutionary story for a human behaviour without evidence:
  ask them what specific evidence would distinguish it from a purely cultural
  explanation.
- If a student treats a claimed human universal as settled fact: ask them to consider
  what cross-cultural evidence would be needed to support or challenge the claim.
- If a student conflates cultural and genetic evolution: ask them to compare the
  timescale of the observed change to genetic evolution's multi-generational
  requirement.

## Voice Teaching Notes
Say "hypothesis, not conclusion" whenever an evolutionary explanation for human
behaviour comes up, to keep the evidentiary requirement explicit. Say "which lane,
which speed?" whenever cultural and genetic evolution are discussed together, to keep
the two-systems distinction active.
