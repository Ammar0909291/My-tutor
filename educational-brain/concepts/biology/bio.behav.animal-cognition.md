## Learning Objective
The student can correctly explain WHY problem-solving and tool use are treated as
evidence of FLEXIBLE, non-instinctive behaviour (rather than fixed action patterns),
correctly identify the specific methodological CHALLENGES in comparative cognition
research (particularly the risk of over-interpreting behaviour as evidence of complex
cognition when simpler explanations exist), and correctly evaluate claims about
theory of mind in non-human animals as CONTESTED, requiring careful attention to
alternative, simpler explanations for the same observed behaviour.

## Core Understanding
**Problem-solving and tool use** are treated as evidence of FLEXIBLE, non-instinctive
behaviour specifically because they involve a NOVEL solution to a NOVEL problem — a
fixed action pattern or simple instinct, by definition, is a STEREOTYPED response
triggered reliably by a specific sign stimulus, and cannot by itself account for an
animal producing a genuinely novel, situationally-appropriate solution it has never
encountered or been trained on before (such as bending a wire into a hook to retrieve
an out-of-reach object). The key evidentiary logic: demonstrating flexible, generalised
problem-solving REQUIRES showing that the animal can adapt its behaviour to genuinely
NEW situations, not merely that it can perform a well-learned or instinctive behaviour
reliably.

**Comparative cognition** is the methodology of studying and comparing cognitive
abilities ACROSS different species, and it faces a specific, recurring METHODOLOGICAL
CHALLENGE that students must understand precisely: **Morgan's Canon**, the principle
that a behaviour should NOT be explained by invoking a HIGHER, more complex cognitive
process (e.g., "the animal understood the causal relationship") if a LOWER, simpler
process (e.g., "the animal learned this specific action sequence through trial-and-
error reinforcement") can adequately explain the same observed behaviour. This
principle exists because researchers face a persistent temptation to OVER-INTERPRET
animal behaviour anthropomorphically, attributing sophisticated understanding where a
simpler learning or instinctive mechanism would suffice — rigorous comparative
cognition research must actively rule out simpler explanations before concluding that
a more complex cognitive process is genuinely operating.

The evidence for **theory of mind** in non-human animals (the capacity to attribute
mental states — beliefs, desires, intentions, knowledge — to OTHER individuals, and to
recognise that another individual's mental state may differ from one's own) is
GENUINELY CONTESTED in the scientific literature, and students must understand WHY:
behaviour that superficially appears to reflect an understanding of another
individual's mental state can often ALSO be explained by simpler mechanisms —
learned behavioural rules, associative learning, or the animal simply attending to
the OTHER individual's observable behavioural cues (like gaze direction or body
posture) WITHOUT genuinely representing that individual's internal mental state at
all. This is precisely the same methodological challenge Morgan's Canon addresses:
each specific claimed instance of theory-of-mind-like behaviour must be evaluated
against whether a simpler, non-mentalistic explanation can account for the same
observed pattern, which is why the overall evidentiary picture remains contested
rather than settled.

## Mental Models
- **The novel-key-for-a-novel-lock model for tool use as flexibility evidence**: a
  fixed instinct is a single key that only opens ONE specific lock (a specific sign
  stimulus); genuine problem-solving is fashioning a NEW key for a lock the animal has
  never encountered before — this is why novelty of the SITUATION is the critical
  evidentiary requirement.
- **Morgan's Canon as the default-to-simpler-explanation rule**: like a scientific
  version of "don't assume the fancier explanation without first ruling out the
  boring one" — always check whether simple learning/instinct explains the behaviour
  before concluding sophisticated cognition is at work.

## Why Students Fail
- They treat any successful problem-solving or tool-use behaviour as automatic
  evidence of sophisticated cognition, without checking whether the SITUATION was
  genuinely novel to the animal or simply a well-learned/instinctive response.
- They are unaware of Morgan's Canon and the risk of over-interpreting animal
  behaviour anthropomorphically, missing why comparative cognition researchers must
  actively rule out simpler explanations before accepting a complex-cognition
  interpretation.
- They treat theory-of-mind evidence in animals as either settled-yes or settled-no,
  missing that the genuine scientific controversy centres on whether simpler,
  non-mentalistic mechanisms can explain the SAME observed behaviours.

### M1 — "Any successful tool use demonstrates sophisticated cognitive understanding" (Type 1: Overgeneralization)
**Statement**: An animal successfully using a tool to solve a problem is taken as
automatic evidence of sophisticated, human-like cognitive understanding, without
checking whether the problem/situation was genuinely NOVEL to the animal (evidence of
flexibility) or whether the specific behaviour could instead be a well-learned or
instinctive response to a familiar situation.
**Origin**: Overgeneralizing from the correct general principle that novel
problem-solving CAN indicate flexible cognition to the incorrect specific inference
that ANY instance of successful tool use automatically qualifies, without separately
verifying the novelty condition.
**Why it persists**: Without explicitly checking whether the animal had prior
experience with a similar problem, a single impressive-looking success can seem
self-evidently to demonstrate flexible cognition.
**Repair**: State explicitly that flexible problem-solving evidence specifically
requires the SITUATION to be genuinely NOVEL to the animal (not previously
encountered or trained) — a familiar, well-practiced tool-use behaviour does not, by
itself, distinguish flexible cognition from a highly-refined learned or instinctive
routine; verifying novelty is a necessary step before drawing the flexible-cognition
conclusion.
**Verification-of-death**: given a scenario contrasting an animal solving a genuinely
novel problem versus repeating a previously-trained solution to a familiar problem,
the learner correctly identifies only the former as evidence of flexible,
non-instinctive problem-solving.

### M2 — "Theory-of-mind-like behaviour in animals proves they understand others' mental states" (Type 6: Analogy Overextension)
**Statement**: An animal's behaviour that superficially resembles a human
theory-of-mind response (e.g., appearing to account for what another individual can
or cannot see) is taken as proof that the animal genuinely represents that
individual's internal mental state, without considering whether a simpler explanation
(learned behavioural rules, or attending only to observable cues like gaze direction)
could account for the same behaviour.
**Origin**: Overextending the human theory-of-mind analogy to animal behaviour that
merely resembles the human pattern superficially, without applying Morgan's Canon to
check whether a simpler, non-mentalistic mechanism explains the SAME observations
equally well.
**Why it persists**: Without explicitly considering and ruling out simpler
alternative explanations, a behaviour that "looks like" understanding another's mind
can seem sufficient evidence on its own.
**Repair**: State Morgan's Canon explicitly and require it to be applied: before
concluding an animal has theory of mind, actively check whether the SAME observed
behaviour could instead be explained by simpler mechanisms (learned association
between another's gaze/posture and specific outcomes, without any actual
representation of that individual's internal mental state) — genuine theory-of-mind
evidence must specifically rule OUT these simpler alternatives, which is precisely why
the overall evidentiary picture remains contested.
**Verification-of-death**: given a described animal behaviour that appears to track
another individual's visual perspective, the learner correctly proposes at least one
simpler, non-mentalistic alternative explanation (e.g., a learned association with
observable gaze direction) before accepting the theory-of-mind interpretation.

## Analogies
- The novel-key-for-a-novel-lock model for tool use as flexibility evidence (see
  Mental Models): a single-purpose instinct key versus fashioning a new key for an
  unfamiliar lock.
- Morgan's Canon as the default-to-simpler-explanation rule (see Mental Models): check
  the boring explanation before accepting the fancy one.

## Demonstrations
- Present the novel-problem-vs-familiar-repeated-solution scenario and ask the
  student to identify which case provides genuine evidence of flexible,
  non-instinctive problem-solving.
- Present a described animal behaviour resembling theory of mind and ask the student
  to propose a simpler, non-mentalistic alternative explanation before evaluating the
  theory-of-mind interpretation.

## Discovery Questions
- "If an animal performs a tool-use behaviour it has practiced hundreds of times
  before, does that tell you as much about its flexible problem-solving ability as a
  genuinely NEW problem would?"
- "Before concluding an animal 'understands what another animal can see,' what
  simpler explanation should you rule out first?"
- "Why might two very different explanations — one involving genuine understanding of
  another's mind, one involving simple learned association with observable cues —
  both predict the exact same observed behaviour?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Introduce problem-solving and tool use as evidence of flexibility, directly
   correcting the any-success-proves-cognition misconception using the novel-vs-
   familiar scenario.
2. Introduce comparative cognition methodology and Morgan's Canon as the governing
   principle for interpreting cross-species behavioural evidence.
3. Introduce the contested theory-of-mind evidence, directly correcting the
   behaviour-proves-understanding misconception using the simpler-alternative
   proposal exercise.
4. Close by connecting all three themes back to the shared methodological principle:
   rule out simpler explanations before accepting a complex-cognition interpretation.

## Tutor Actions
- If a student accepts any successful tool use as sophisticated cognition: ask them
  whether the situation was genuinely novel to the animal.
- If a student accepts theory-of-mind-like behaviour at face value: ask them to
  propose a simpler, non-mentalistic explanation for the same behaviour.
- If a student cannot articulate Morgan's Canon: ask them which of two competing
  explanations (simpler vs. more complex) should be checked first.

## Voice Teaching Notes
Say "was it genuinely novel?" whenever tool use or problem-solving is discussed, to
keep the novelty-requirement explicit. Say "rule out the simpler explanation first"
whenever comparative cognition or theory of mind comes up, to keep Morgan's Canon
active.
