# Amines — `chem.nitro.amines`

## Identity
- **KG ID**: chem.nitro.amines
- **Subject**: Chemistry
- **Domain**: Nitrogen Compounds (chem.nitro)
- **Prerequisites**: chem.alc.alcohols, chem.org.mechanisms
- **Difficulty**: advanced
- **Bloom level**: analyse
- **Estimated hours**: 2.5

## Learning Objective
Classify amines as primary, secondary, or tertiary and explain their basicity relative to ammonia, predict the products of amine reactions (alkylation, acylation, Hinsberg test), describe the preparation of primary aromatic amines from nitrobenzene, explain diazonium salt formation and its synthetic utility (Sandmeyer reaction, phenol synthesis), and contrast arylamine and aliphatic amine basicity using resonance.

## Core Understanding
**Structure and classification**: amines are derivatives of ammonia (NH₃) in which one, two, or three H atoms are replaced by alkyl or aryl groups; 1° amine (RNH₂), 2° amine (R₂NH), 3° amine (R₃N); the nitrogen is sp³-hybridised with a lone pair; amines are PYRAMIDAL (not planar) — rapid inversion prevents isolation of enantiomers in most cases. **Basicity**: amines are bases (lone pair on N accepts a proton); Kb for aliphatic amines: methylamine > ammonia > aniline. WHY aliphatic amines are MORE BASIC than ammonia: alkyl groups are electron-donating (+I induction), increasing electron density on N → better proton acceptor. WHY aromatic amines (aniline) are LESS BASIC than ammonia: the lone pair on N is delocalized into the benzene ring (resonance donation into ring → lone pair partially unavailable for protonation); phenyl ring withdraws electron density → N is a POORER base; Kb of aniline ≈ 4×10⁻¹⁰ vs. Kb of methylamine ≈ 4×10⁻⁴. **Basicity order (aliphatic)**: 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃ > ArNH₂ (in the gas phase; in water, 2° > 3° due to solvation of N–H). **Reactions of amines**: (1) **Reaction with acids**: amine + HX → ammonium salt (RNH₃⁺X⁻); reversible with base; (2) **Alkylation** (R–X): amine + alkyl halide → progressive alkylation through 1°, 2°, 3°, to quaternary ammonium salt R₄N⁺X⁻ (not further basic); difficult to stop at one stage — Gabriel synthesis avoids over-alkylation for 1° amines; (3) **Acylation** (acyl chloride or anhydride): amine + RCOCl → amide + HCl; useful for protecting amines; 3° amines cannot form amides (no N–H to eliminate); (4) **Hinsberg test**: distinguishes 1°, 2°, 3° amines: add benzenesulfonyl chloride (PhSO₂Cl) + NaOH; 1° amine → sulfonamide (soluble in NaOH = has N–H, acidic — can deprotonate); 2° amine → N-substituted sulfonamide (INSOLUBLE in NaOH = no N–H, neutral); 3° amine → no reaction (or solid ammonium salt, dissolves in acid). **Preparation of primary aromatic amines**: nitrobenzene + Sn/HCl (or Fe/HCl) → aniline; or catalytic H₂/Pd → aniline. This is the reduction of a nitro group to amino (covered in chem.nitro.nitro-compounds). **Diazonium salts**: primary aromatic amine + NaNO₂ + HCl at 0–5°C → aryl diazonium chloride (ArN₂⁺Cl⁻); the N≡N (diazonium) group is an excellent leaving group; diazonium salts are UNSTABLE above 5°C (decompose to phenol + N₂ in water) and must be used in situ; reactions of diazonium: (a) **Sandmeyer reaction** — Cu(I)X catalyst + ArN₂⁺ → ArX (X = Cl, Br, CN); gives aryl halides or nitriles NOT accessible by direct EAS (e.g., DEACTIVATED arenes do not undergo direct FAS); (b) **Balz-Schiemann reaction**: ArN₂⁺ + HBF₄ → ArN₂⁺BF₄⁻ → pyrolysis → ArF (aryl fluoride, rare direct method); (c) **Phenol** (warm aqueous workup): ArN₂⁺ + H₂O → ArOH + N₂ + H⁺; (d) **Azo coupling**: ArN₂⁺ + activated aromatic (phenol or aniline) → azo dye (Ar–N=N–Ar', ORANGE-RED); intensely coloured, used as dyes; the coupling is an EAS on the activated ring (activated by OH or NR₂). **Synthetic utility of diazonium**: starting from arenes, one can install NH₂ via nitration + reduction, then use the diazonium to install any of: Cl, Br, I, F, CN, OH, H (dediazotiation with H₃PO₂) — greatly expanding the substitution pattern accessible via EAS alone.

## Mental Models
- **Amine basicity = lone pair availability**: anything that reduces electron density on N reduces basicity; resonance donation into an aromatic ring removes the lone pair from the basic site → aniline << aliphatic amines. Alkyl groups push electrons toward N → greater lone pair density → stronger base.
- **Diazonium as a "nitrogen cargo ship"**: the diazonium group (N₂⁺) is attached to the ring like a cargo container; when you dock at the right port (Cu₂Cl₂ → Cl port; Cu₂Br₂ → Br port; warm water → OH port), the cargo swaps with the port's contents and N₂ leaves as gas — a perfect driving force (stable N₂, −ΔG).
- **Hinsberg test decision tree**: can it react? → 3° cannot; 1° forms soluble sulfonamide (N–H present, acidic); 2° forms insoluble sulfonamide (no N–H, neutral). The NaOH solubility step distinguishes 1° from 2°.

## Why Students Fail
- Predicting aniline as MORE basic than aliphatic amines — inverting the resonance argument.
- Forgetting that the Sandmeyer reaction requires Cu(I) catalyst (not just warming in water); writing "warm diazonium + HCl → chlorobenzene" without a catalyst is wrong.
- Over-alkylation in direct amine + alkyl halide reactions — not recognising that the product amine is itself a nucleophile and continues to react unless the Gabriel synthesis or another selective method is used.

## Misconceptions
1. **"Aniline is more basic than methylamine because the lone pair is more exposed in aniline"** (Type 2 — perceptual intuition: students see the lone pair drawn on the nitrogen and assume any visible lone pair is equally available for protonation; they do not consider that resonance donation into the ring removes the lone pair's availability).
   - Probe: "Which is the stronger base: aniline or methylamine? Explain."
   - Characteristic phrase: "aniline is more basic because the benzene ring is big and N sticks out more"
   - Intervention: draw the resonance structures of aniline — the lone pair on N delocalizes into the ring (ortho/para positions carry partial negative charge; N carries partial positive); N's lone pair is SHARED with the ring and thus LESS available for protonation. pKb: aniline ≈ 9.4 (weak base); methylamine ≈ 3.4 (strong base). Lower pKb = stronger base.

2. **"The Sandmeyer reaction replaces –N₂⁺ with Cl without any special reagent"** (Type 5 — instruction-induced: the reaction is sometimes written in a simplified form without emphasising the role of Cu(I); students think warming in HCl gives the aryl chloride).
   - Probe: "Write the conditions for converting benzenediazonium chloride to chlorobenzene."
   - Characteristic phrase: "just heat in HCl" / "diazonium + HCl → chlorobenzene + N₂"
   - Intervention: the Sandmeyer reaction requires CuCl (copper(I) chloride) catalyst; Cu(I) acts as a single-electron transfer agent, generating an aryl radical, which then reacts with Cl. Simply heating in HCl gives phenol + N₂ (hydrolysis) or dediazotiation products, NOT the aryl halide. The copper catalyst is ESSENTIAL for Sandmeyer.

3. **"Direct alkylation of ammonia or a primary amine gives a clean primary or secondary amine product"** (Type 1 — overgeneralization: students treat the first alkylation as the end of the story, not recognising that the mono-alkylated product has a lone pair that is as nucleophilic as the starting material and will compete for the next alkyl halide).
   - Probe: "What is the main problem with trying to prepare methylamine by reacting NH₃ with CH₃Br?"
   - Characteristic phrase: "NH₃ + CH₃Br → CH₃NH₂, done"
   - Intervention: CH₃NH₂ formed in the first step has a lone pair on N just as nucleophilic as NH₃; it reacts with CH₃Br → (CH₃)₂NH → (CH₃)₃N → (CH₃)₄N⁺Br⁻. The product is a mixture of all four species. To prepare a pure 1° amine, use the Gabriel synthesis (potassium phthalimide + RX → N-alkylphthalimide → hydrazinolysis → pure RNH₂).

## Analogies
- **Good**: the diazonium group on an aromatic ring is like a universal adapter on a USB hub — whatever plug you need (Cl, Br, CN, OH, F), you connect the right receptor (CuCl, CuBr, CuCN, H₂O, BF₄⁻) and the adapter swaps out with N₂ as the release gas. Without the adapter (diazonium), many of these connections would be impossible by direct EAS.
- **Anti-analogy**: Do NOT say "amines behave just like ammonia in all reactions" — aromatic amines (anilines) are profoundly different from aliphatic amines in basicity, reactivity toward acylation, and susceptibility to diazotisation; and all amines differ from ammonia in the alkylation pattern. "More like ammonia" is a useful starting point but must be immediately qualified.

## Demonstrations
- **Amine odour and basicity**: ammonia versus a dilute aliphatic amine solution (e.g., ethylamine) — both pungent; add HCl (fuming) → white ammonium/alkylammonium salt cloud forms; then add NaOH → free amine regenerated (odour returns). Confirms: amine + acid → salt; salt + base → amine.
- **Azo dye synthesis**: diazotise aniline (NaNO₂/HCl at 0°C) → couple with β-naphthol in NaOH → brilliant orange-red azo dye precipitates. Dramatic colour change; shows the coupling reaction and the synthetic utility of diazonium chemistry in one step.

## Discovery Questions
1. Rank the following in order of increasing basicity: NH₃, aniline, methylamine, dimethylamine. Explain each ranking step.
2. You need to prepare 4-bromoaniline from aniline. Why can't you directly brominate aniline with Br₂/FeBr₃? (Hint: where would the Br go?) What route would you use instead?
3. Design a synthesis of p-iodotoluene from toluene using diazonium chemistry. (Nitration → reduction → diazotisation → Sandmeyer with KI/Cu.)
4. Why must diazonium salt preparation be conducted below 5°C?

## Teaching Sequence
1. **Structure and classification**: 1°, 2°, 3°; sp³ N; lone pair; pyramidal; physical properties (bp, solubility).
2. **Basicity**: compare NH₃, aliphatic amines, aniline; +I effect vs. resonance donation; pKb values.
3. **Reactions**: acid–base (ammonium salt); alkylation (over-alkylation problem; Gabriel synthesis mention); acylation (amide formation).
4. **Hinsberg test**: mechanism; solubility distinction of sulfonamide with NaOH → distinguishes 1°, 2°, 3°.
5. **Preparation of aromatic amines**: nitrobenzene → aniline (reduction); EAS on aniline (activated ring, ortho/para directing).
6. **Diazonium salts**: preparation conditions (low temperature, NaNO₂/HCl); stability; reactions: Sandmeyer (Cl, Br, CN), Balz-Schiemann (F), phenol synthesis, azo coupling.
7. **Synthetic strategy**: how diazonium unlocks substitution patterns inaccessible to EAS alone.

## Tutor Actions
- **If aniline basicity over-rated**: draw the five resonance structures of aniline showing lone pair into ring; ask "is the lone pair still localised on N for protonation?" — no; confirm with pKb values.
- **If Sandmeyer catalyst omitted**: ask "what happens if you just warm the diazonium in HCl without copper?" → phenol (hydrolysis) or decomposition; confirm Cu(I) is the catalyst for halide substitution.
- **If over-alkylation not flagged**: trace the reaction step-by-step: NH₃ → RNH₂ → R₂NH → R₃N → R₄N⁺; each product is nucleophilic; ask "can CH₃NH₂ also react with CH₃Br?" — yes, at similar or faster rate.

## Voice Teaching Notes
- "Aniline is a WEAK base because the lone pair is delocalised into the ring. Aliphatic amines are STRONGER bases because the alkyl groups push electrons TO the nitrogen."
- "Diazonium reactions: cold = stable; warm + water = phenol; Cu₂Cl₂ = chlorobenzene; Cu₂Br₂ = bromobenzene; Cu₂(CN)₂ = nitrile; BF₄⁻ + heat = fluorobenzene."
- "Gabriel synthesis = the clean route to a pure primary amine. Direct alkylation gives a mixture."

## Assessment Signals
- **Green**: correctly ranks aniline < NH₃ < aliphatic amines in basicity with resonance/inductive reasoning; correctly states Sandmeyer requires Cu(I); predicts over-alkylation problem in direct synthesis and proposes Gabriel synthesis; writes diazonium reactions with correct conditions.
- **Amber**: correct basicity order but weak explanation; knows Sandmeyer but forgets the catalyst; can diazotise but not use the product in Sandmeyer.
- **Red**: ranks aniline as more basic than methylamine; writes Sandmeyer without Cu(I); says direct alkylation of NH₃ gives a pure primary amine.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
- Basicity inversion: give three pKb values (aniline 9.4, NH₃ 4.7, methylamine 3.4); ask "which pKb is smallest?" — methylamine; "smaller pKb = stronger or weaker base?" — stronger; "now explain WHY using resonance." Force the reasoning after establishing the empirical order.
- Sandmeyer catalyst omission: ask "what is a radical mechanism doing in an ionic-looking reaction?" — Cu(I) transfers a single electron to the aryl diazonium, generating an aryl radical + N₂; the radical then abstracts Cl from CuCl₂ to give ArCl. The Cu is the essential single-electron mediator.
- Over-alkylation: run a mini kinetics thought experiment — "is CH₃NH₂ a better or worse nucleophile than NH₃?" — equally good (both N lone pair); therefore over-alkylation is statistically inevitable with direct methods.

## Memory Hooks
- **Basicity: aliphatic > NH₃ > ArNH₂ (aniline last, resonance drains N lone pair)**
- **Hinsberg: 1° = soluble sulfonamide; 2° = insoluble sulfonamide; 3° = no reaction**
- **Diazonium: < 5°C to prepare; Sandmeyer (Cu₂X₂) = ArX; warm water = ArOH; azo coupling = dye**
- **Gabriel synthesis = pure 1° amine (phthalimide + RX → NH₂R)**
- **Aniline EAS = ortho/para activation (lone pair into ring = activating substituent)**

## Transfer Connections
- **Nitro compounds** (chem.nitro.nitro-compounds): reduction of Ar–NO₂ → Ar–NH₂ (aniline) is the entry point for all aromatic amine chemistry; the diazonium route can also re-introduce NO₂ via the Sandmeyer with sodium nitrite; nitro and amino groups are connected via a redox ladder.
- **Phenols** (chem.alc.phenols): ArN₂⁺ + H₂O → ArOH + N₂ is a key route to substituted phenols (complementary to direct phenol synthesis) when the position is set by prior nitration/reduction rather than direct OH introduction.
- **Carbonyl chemistry** (chem.carb.aldehydes/amides): acylation of amines gives amides; amide chemistry (Nylon, peptide bonds) builds directly on amine + acyl chemistry.
- **EAS** (chem.hyd.arenes): –NH₂ is the most powerfully activating, ortho/para-directing substituent; understanding aniline's EAS reactivity requires integrating resonance donation (+M effect dominates −I) with knowledge of EAS mechanism.

## Cross-Subject Connections
- **Biology**: all 20 amino acids have an α-amino group (primary amine); the peptide bond is an amide formed from amine + carboxyl; neurotransmitters (dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline) are all aromatic amines; histamine (an imidazole amine) mediates allergic response.
- **Pharmacology**: amines are the most common functional group in drug molecules; they are protonated at physiological pH (salt form) for solubility but must cross membranes as the free base; pKa of the protonated amine controls distribution, half-life, and receptor binding.
- **Dye industry**: azo dyes (made by diazonium coupling) constitute ~60–70% of all synthetic dyes; the N=N chromophore absorbs visible light (400–700 nm range); Congo Red, methyl orange, and sunset yellow are azo dyes. Their synthesis is direct classroom diazonium chemistry.

## Curriculum Feedback
- The diazonium salt reactions (Sandmeyer, Balz-Schiemann, phenol synthesis, azo coupling) collectively represent some of the most versatile synthetic tools in aromatic chemistry; they deserve their own assessment set that tests which reagent gives which product rather than just definitions.
