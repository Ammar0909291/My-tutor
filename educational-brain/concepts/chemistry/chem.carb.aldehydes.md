# Aldehydes — `chem.carb.aldehydes`

## Identity
- **KG ID**: chem.carb.aldehydes
- **Subject**: Chemistry
- **Domain**: Carbonyl Chemistry (chem.carb)
- **Prerequisites**: chem.alc.alcohols, chem.org.mechanisms
- **Difficulty**: advanced
- **Bloom level**: apply
- **Estimated hours**: 2.5

## Learning Objective
Describe the structure and reactivity of aldehydes (sp² carbonyl carbon, nucleophilic addition mechanism), predict products of reactions with common nucleophiles (H⁻, RMgX, HCN, alcohols, Tollens'/Fehling's), explain why aldehydes are more reactive than ketones toward nucleophilic addition, and connect the aldehyde group to the oxidation state ladder for primary carbons.

## Core Understanding
**Structure**: the aldehyde group (–CHO) consists of a carbonyl (C=O) directly attached to a hydrogen and one carbon chain (or H in formaldehyde, HCHO). The carbonyl carbon is sp²-hybridised; the three groups are coplanar (~120° bond angles); the π-bond is polarised C(δ+)=O(δ−) (oxygen more electronegative). The carbonyl carbon is the reactive site for nucleophilic attack. **Nomenclature**: IUPAC — replace terminal "-e" of parent alkane with "-al" (ethanal, propanal); the carbonyl carbon is always C-1 (so no position number is needed). Common names: formaldehyde (methanal, HCHO), acetaldehyde (ethanal, CH₃CHO), benzaldehyde (PhCHO). **Why aldehydes are more reactive than ketones toward nucleophilic addition**: (a) steric effect — the aldehyde has only ONE carbon substituent on the carbonyl carbon (the H is essentially no steric barrier), while the ketone has TWO; less steric crowding → nucleophile approaches more easily; (b) electronic effect — alkyl groups are weakly electron-donating by induction, which partially neutralises the δ+ on the ketone carbonyl carbon; aldehydes have fewer alkyl groups → carbonyl carbon is MORE electrophilic → more reactive. **General nucleophilic addition mechanism**: nucleophile attacks the C(δ+); the π-bond electrons move to O (O becomes the alkoxide); protonation of O by workup gives the product alcohol (tetrahedral product). **Specific reactions**: (1) **Reduction with NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄** — delivers H⁻ to the carbonyl carbon; aldehyde → primary alcohol; (2) **Grignard addition** (RMgX) — carbanion (R−) attacks carbonyl C → after workup → secondary alcohol from aldehyde (primary alcohol from formaldehyde, HCHO); (3) **HCN addition** — CN⁻ attacks the carbonyl C; after protonation → cyanohydrin (OH + CN on same carbon); the extra C from CN allows chain extension by one carbon; industrially important; (4) **Acetal formation** (ROH, acid catalyst) — two equivalents of alcohol; hemiacetal → acetal (R–CH(OR')₂) + H₂O; reversible (acid-catalysed); acetals are STABLE to base and nucleophiles → used as PROTECTING GROUPS in synthesis; (5) **Aldol reaction** (base, e.g. NaOH): one aldehyde acts as nucleophile (via its α-carbanion = enolate), one acts as electrophile (the carbonyl); product = β-hydroxyaldehyde (aldol); on heating → dehydration → α,β-unsaturated aldehyde (aldol condensation); cross-aldol is possible with a ketone as the enolate donor; (6) **Tollens' test** (Ag(NH₃)₂⁺ in ammonia): oxidises aldehyde to carboxylate; Ag⁺ is reduced to silver metal → silver mirror on test tube wall; POSITIVE for aldehydes and reducing sugars; NEGATIVE for ketones; (7) **Fehling's test** (Cu²⁺ in alkaline tartrate): Cu²⁺ (blue) oxidised to Cu₂O (brick-red precipitate); POSITIVE for aliphatic aldehydes and reducing sugars; NEGATIVE for aromatic aldehydes (benzaldehyde) and ketones; (8) **Cannizzaro reaction** (aldehydes without α-H, conc. NaOH): one aldehyde is oxidised to carboxylate, one reduced to alcohol; a disproportionation (50% alcohol, 50% carboxylate); requires aldehydes with no α-H (HCHO, PhCHO, ClCHO) — if α-H is present, aldol happens instead.

## Mental Models
- **The carbonyl carbon as a "electron-poor target"**: the C=O polarisation leaves a permanent partial positive on carbon; any species with a lone pair or a negative charge is drawn to it. Every nucleophilic addition reaction is the same event: nucleophile → carbonyl C, electrons → O, proton workup restores O–H. Mastering this one step unlocks 80% of carbonyl chemistry.
- **Aldehyde vs. ketone reactivity ladder**: more electrophilic → more reactive; steric and electronic effects BOTH push aldehydes higher than ketones. Formaldehyde (no alkyl groups) > other aldehydes > ketones > amides (decreasing electrophilicity follows this order).
- **Tollens' vs. Fehling's selectivity**: Tollens' detects ALL aldehydes (aliphatic + aromatic); Fehling's detects ONLY aliphatic aldehydes. Remember this as: "Tollens = Total (all aldehydes); Fehling = Fatty only (not aromatic)."

## Why Students Fail
- Confusing the direction of nucleophilic attack — attacking O instead of C; students see O as δ− and conclude that's where a nucleophile attacks, forgetting that O is the product anchor, not the reaction site.
- Drawing the wrong alcohol product from aldehyde reduction: aldehyde → primary alcohol (1 carbon from the original chain, 1 H from the hydride); ketone → secondary alcohol. Students sometimes say aldehyde → secondary alcohol.
- Forgetting that Fehling's is negative for aromatic aldehydes — a common mark-losing error in UK/Indian board exams.

## Misconceptions
1. **"Nucleophiles attack the oxygen of the carbonyl because oxygen is δ−"** (Type 2 — perceptual intuition: students see the dipole correctly (C+/O−) but misidentify the electrophilic site; a nucleophile attacks the POSITIVE centre — the carbon — not the negative oxygen. The oxygen ends up as an alkoxide because the π electrons are pushed there, not because O was "attracted" to the incoming nucleophile).
   - Probe: "In the addition of NaBH₄ to ethanal, which atom does the hydride (H⁻) attack?"
   - Characteristic phrase: "H⁻ attacks the oxygen because it's δ−" / "H⁻ goes to the negative end"
   - Intervention: draw the curved arrow from H⁻ to the C(δ+); the π-bond electrons move to O → alkoxide; protonation gives the alcohol. A nucleophile (H⁻ = negative species) attacks the electrophilic site (C+), NOT another negatively polarised site. Like poles repel.

2. **"Aldehydes give secondary alcohols when reduced with NaBH₄"** (Type 1 — overgeneralization from ketone reduction: students learn "carbonyl → alcohol" and conflate aldehyde (→ 1°) with ketone (→ 2°)).
   - Probe: "What is the product of reducing propanal (CH₃CH₂CHO) with NaBH₄ followed by workup?"
   - Characteristic phrase: "the product is propan-2-ol" / "propanal reduced gives a secondary alcohol"
   - Intervention: the aldehyde carbonyl carbon gains one H from the hydride; the other substituent is the alkyl chain; the –OH is on the terminal carbon → propan-1-ol (1-propanol). Secondary alcohol requires TWO carbon groups on the carbonyl carbon → that requires a ketone.

3. **"Fehling's test is positive for all aldehydes including benzaldehyde"** (Type 5 — instruction-induced: Fehling's is introduced as a general "aldehyde test" without qualification; the distinction between aliphatic and aromatic aldehydes is often omitted in initial teaching).
   - Probe: "What would you observe when benzaldehyde is added to Fehling's solution?"
   - Characteristic phrase: "brick-red precipitate because benzaldehyde is an aldehyde"
   - Intervention: Fehling's requires an aliphatic aldehyde; benzaldehyde does NOT give a positive Fehling's test (no brick-red Cu₂O precipitate) under normal conditions. Tollens' (silver mirror) is the correct diagnostic for aromatic aldehydes. The distinction: Cu²⁺ in alkaline tartrate cannot oxidise benzaldehyde under typical conditions.

## Analogies
- **Good**: the aldehyde carbonyl carbon is like an open parking space (electron-poor, positive) with only one car already parked (one alkyl group); ketone has two cars parked → more crowded → harder for a new car (nucleophile) to enter. Formaldehyde has no cars → easiest to park.
- **Anti-analogy**: Do NOT say "aldehydes are just ketones with one H on the carbonyl" without immediately adding that this structural difference dramatically changes reactivity, oxidizability (aldehydes are easily oxidised to acids, ketones are not under mild conditions), and the alcohol product class (1° vs. 2°). The H on the carbonyl makes aldehydes uniquely different — it is an oxidisable C–H bond.

## Demonstrations
- **Tollens' silver mirror**: mix Tollens' reagent; add glucose or acetaldehyde; warm gently → a bright silver mirror deposits on the inside of the test tube. Dramatic visual proof of aldehyde oxidation.
- **Fehling's test**: prepare Fehling's A (CuSO₄) + B (NaOH + sodium potassium tartrate) → mix; add glucose → heat → brick-red Cu₂O precipitate; add benzaldehyde → no change. Confirms: aliphatic vs. aromatic aldehyde selectivity.

## Discovery Questions
1. Predict the products when propanal reacts with: (a) NaBH₄/H₂O workup; (b) CH₃MgBr then H₃O⁺; (c) HCN; (d) excess ethanol + HCl catalyst.
2. HCHO has no α-H. What happens when it is treated with NaOH? What are the two products?
3. Write the mechanism for hemiacetal formation from ethanal and methanol under acid catalysis. At what step does the acetal protecting group become stable?
4. You have three unlabelled bottles: acetone, acetaldehyde, and cyclohexanone. Describe a simple chemical test sequence that distinguishes all three.

## Teaching Sequence
1. **Structure and nomenclature**: draw the –CHO group; sp² geometry; IUPAC naming; common names.
2. **Polarisation and reactivity**: draw C(δ+)=O(δ−); explain why C is the nucleophilic attack site; compare aldehyde vs. ketone electrophilicity (steric + inductive).
3. **Nucleophilic addition mechanism**: general curved-arrow sequence; protonation on workup; product is alcohol.
4. **Reduction (NaBH₄/LiAlH₄)**: 1° alcohol product; distinguish from ketone reduction (2° alcohol).
5. **Grignard addition**: primary or secondary alcohol depending on carbonyl; chain extension.
6. **Acetal formation**: mechanism; hemiacetal; acetal; protecting group use.
7. **HCN addition**: cyanohydrin; chain extension application.
8. **Tollens' and Fehling's tests**: conditions; positive/negative results; limitations (Fehling's = not aromatic).
9. **Aldol reaction**: enolate formation; nucleophilic attack on second aldehyde; β-hydroxy aldehyde; dehydration to α,β-unsaturated aldehyde.
10. **Cannizzaro reaction**: only without α-H; disproportionation to acid + alcohol.

## Tutor Actions
- **If student attacks oxygen in mechanism**: point to C(δ+); ask "nucleophiles attack positive centres — is C or O positive in the carbonyl?"
- **If 1°/2° alcohol product is wrong**: ask "how many alkyl groups does the carbonyl C have?" — count them; 1 → 1° alcohol from aldehyde; 2 → 2° alcohol from ketone.
- **If Fehling's error**: ask "what class is benzaldehyde?" — aromatic; "does Fehling's test work for aromatic aldehydes?" — no; Tollens' does.

## Voice Teaching Notes
- "All nucleophilic addition: Nu attacks C(δ+), electrons go to O, proton on workup. Say this for EVERY reaction."
- "Aldehyde → primary alcohol. Ketone → secondary alcohol. State the rule before every reduction question."
- "Tollens' = silver mirror (ALL aldehydes). Fehling's = brick red (ALIPHATIC aldehydes only). Ketones = negative for both."

## Assessment Signals
- **Green**: draws nucleophilic addition mechanism correctly with attack at C; correctly identifies 1° alcohol from aldehyde reduction; states Tollens' is positive and Fehling's is negative for benzaldehyde; draws acetal product and states its use as a protecting group.
- **Amber**: correct product identification but mechanism arrows wrong; knows Tollens'/Fehling's results but cannot explain why Fehling's is negative for aromatic aldehydes.
- **Red**: attacks oxygen in the mechanism; predicts secondary alcohol from aldehyde reduction; says Fehling's positive for benzaldehyde.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
- Oxygen-attack error: teach the general rule "nucleophiles attack positive centres" before any carbonyl question; apply it first to a simple nucleophile + electrophile pair (Br⁻ + CH₃Br = SN2) to confirm they know this rule in a simpler context, then apply to the carbonyl.
- 1°/2° confusion: create a two-column table: "number of C groups on carbonyl" vs. "product alcohol type"; fill in for HCHO (0 C groups → 1° alcohol, methanol), RCHO (1 C group → 1° alcohol), R₂CO (2 C groups → 2° alcohol).
- Fehling's/Tollens' errors: give a decision flowchart: Is the compound an aldehyde? → Tollens' positive. Is it aliphatic? → Fehling's also positive. Is it aromatic or a ketone? → Fehling's negative.

## Memory Hooks
- **Aldehyde = –CHO; terminal = always C-1; IUPAC = "-al"**
- **Nu attacks C(δ+), not O(δ−)** — the central rule of nucleophilic addition
- **Aldehyde → 1° alcohol (one R group); ketone → 2° alcohol (two R groups)**
- **Tollens' = silver mirror = ALL aldehydes; Fehling's = brick red = ALIPHATIC aldehydes only**
- **Acetal = protecting group for carbonyl (stable to base, unstable to acid)**
- **Cannizzaro = no α-H + conc. NaOH = 50% acid + 50% alcohol (disproportionation)**

## Transfer Connections
- **Grignard reagents** (chem.hal.grignard): the Grignard's carbanion attacks the aldehyde carbonyl — the canonical application of Grignard chemistry; HCHO → 1° alcohol, RCHO → 2° alcohol.
- **Ketones** (next node in chem.carb): ketones share the nucleophilic addition framework but are less reactive; comparing aldehyde and ketone reactivity and the products of parallel reactions (1° vs. 2° alcohols) is a required synthesis skill.
- **Sugars** (chem.bio.sugars, where available): glucose and other monosaccharides have aldehyde (or ketone in ketoses) functionalities; Tollens'/Fehling's are DIAGNOSTIC TESTS FOR REDUCING SUGARS, extending the aldehyde test into biochemistry.
- **Aldol condensation**: the product α,β-unsaturated aldehyde (e.g., 2-butenal from acetaldehyde) is an important conjugated carbonyl compound used in Diels-Alder and Michael addition reactions; connects to chem.org.pericyclic.

## Cross-Subject Connections
- **Biology**: retinal (derived from vitamin A) is an aldehyde; its Schiff-base formation with lysine in rhodopsin is the first step in vision; citral (lemon scent) and cinnamaldehyde (cinnamon) are aromatic/unsaturated aldehydes; glucose (an aldehyde in the open-chain form) is the primary metabolic fuel.
- **Environmental**: formaldehyde (HCHO) is a major indoor air pollutant (off-gassing from urea-formaldehyde resins in chipboard/plywood); classified as a carcinogen by IARC; its chemistry is entirely aldehyde nucleophilic addition (with NH₂ groups in protein amino acids and nucleic acid bases).
- **Industry**: acetaldehyde (ethanal) is a major industrial feedstock; produced by Wacker oxidation of ethene (PdCl₂/CuCl₂ catalyst); used to make acetic acid (oxidation) and n-butanol (aldol condensation). Benzaldehyde is used in almond flavouring and dye synthesis.
