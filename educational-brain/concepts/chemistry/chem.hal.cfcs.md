## Learning Objective
Classify polyhalogen compounds; describe the structures, properties, and uses of key examples (dichloromethane, chloroform, carbon tetrachloride, iodoform); explain the formation and uses of CFCs and their role in ozone depletion; describe HFCs and HFOs as replacements; and explain the mechanism of ozone layer destruction.

## Core Understanding
**Polyhalogen compounds — overview**:
Polyhalogen compounds contain more than one halogen atom, either of the same or different elements. They range from common laboratory solvents (CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃, CCl₄) to industrial refrigerants (CFCs, HCFCs, HFCs) and to naturally occurring compounds.

**Key dihalomethane, trihalomethane, tetrahalomethane**:

- **Dichloromethane (DCM, CH₂Cl₂, methylene chloride)**:
  - Structure: sp³ carbon, two C–Cl bonds, two C–H bonds; polar molecule (dipole moment ≈ 1.60 D).
  - Properties: bp 40°C, colourless liquid, pleasant sweet odour, denser than water (1.33 g/mL), immiscible with water.
  - Uses: most widely used organic solvent — paint stripping, pharmaceutical manufacturing, food processing (decaffeination of coffee — caffeine extracted by DCM), metal cleaning. Low flammability makes it safer than many alternatives.
  - Toxicity: absorbed through skin; anaesthetic at high concentration; probable human carcinogen (IARC Group 2A). Regulated for consumer use in many jurisdictions.

- **Chloroform (trichloromethane, CHCl₃)**:
  - Structure: sp³ C with three C–Cl bonds, one C–H bond; polar (μ ≈ 1.87 D).
  - Properties: bp 61°C, dense (1.47 g/mL), colourless, characteristic sweet odour. Immiscible with water.
  - Historical: used as an anaesthetic (1847, James Young Simpson) before safer alternatives; abandoned due to narrow therapeutic window (anaesthetic → lethal dose close), cardiac sensitisation, liver toxicity, and carcinogenicity.
  - Current uses: NMR solvent (CDCl₃ = deuterochloroform, standard NMR solvent); synthesis of HCFC-22 (CHClF₂) for air-conditioning refrigerant; extraction solvent.
  - Light sensitivity: CHCl₃ oxidises in air and light to PHOSGENE (COCl₂, toxic war gas): 2CHCl₃ + O₂ → 2COCl₂ + 2HCl. Stored in dark bottles with 1% ethanol (ethanol reacts with phosgene before it can accumulate: COCl₂ + C₂H₅OH → CO₂ + HCl + C₂H₅Cl).

- **Carbon tetrachloride (tetrachloromethane, CCl₄)**:
  - Structure: perfect tetrahedral symmetry; NONPOLAR (all C–Cl bond dipoles cancel). Despite 4 polar bonds, μ = 0.
  - Properties: bp 77°C, very dense (1.59 g/mL), immiscible with water; nonpolar solvent.
  - Historical uses: dry-cleaning solvent, fire extinguisher (halon-type — non-flammable, CCl₄ smothers fire by vaporising over flames; vapour denser than air). Both uses have been banned — fire extinguisher use released toxic CCl₄ vapour in enclosed spaces; dry-cleaning use caused solvent exposure.
  - Current: laboratory solvent; precursor to CFCs and HCFCs.
  - Toxicity: severe hepatotoxic (liver poison) via CYP450 metabolism to CCl₃• radical; causes centrolobular necrosis. Also nephrotoxic and carcinogenic.

- **Iodoform (triiodomethane, CHI₃)**:
  - Structure: CHI₃ (one C–H, three C–I bonds); yellow solid, characteristic penetrating antiseptic odour.
  - Iodoform test: a positive test for METHYL KETONES (R–CO–CH₃) and certain secondary alcohols (CH₃–CH(OH)–R) and ethanol. Conditions: I₂ + NaOH (alkaline iodine solution). The methyl group is iodinated three times → CI₃ group; CI₃⁻ then leaves as a good leaving group from the tetrahedral intermediate → CHI₃ (yellow precipitate) + RCOO⁻. Only positive for methyl ketones (and ethanol/secondary methylcarbinols oxidisable to methyl ketones).
  - Uses: historical wound antiseptic (CHI₃ slowly releases I₂ which kills bacteria); replaced by more effective antiseptics.

**Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) — structure, uses, and ozone depletion**:

CFCs (Freons): saturated hydrocarbons in which ALL H atoms are replaced by F and Cl (no H remaining). Examples: CFC-11 (CCl₃F, trichlorofluoromethane), CFC-12 (CCl₂F₂, dichlorodifluoromethane, "Freon-12").

- **Properties**: non-flammable, non-toxic to humans, chemically inert (no reactive bonds to most chemicals), low boiling points (volatile), gases or low-bp liquids at ambient conditions. These properties made them ideal refrigerants, propellants in aerosol sprays, blowing agents for foam.
- **Uses**: refrigeration (CFC-12), aerosol propellants, Styrofoam blowing agents, fire suppression (halons, e.g. CF₃Br).

**Ozone layer depletion by CFCs (Chapman-Molina mechanism)**:
The stratospheric ozone layer (O₃, 15–35 km altitude) absorbs harmful UV-B and UV-C radiation. CFCs are photolytically cleaved by UV radiation in the stratosphere:

1. **Initiation**: CF₂Cl₂ + UV (λ < 260 nm) → •CF₂Cl + Cl•
2. **Propagation cycle**:
   - Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂ (ozone destroyed)
   - ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂ (Cl• regenerated)
   - Net: O₃ + O → 2O₂ (ozone destroyed, chlorine NOT consumed — catalytic)
3. Each Cl• destroys up to ~100,000 O₃ molecules before being deactivated.
4. **Termination**: Cl• + CH₄ → HCl + CH₃• (HCl is a reservoir species, not active); ClO• + NO₂ → ClONO₂ (another reservoir). Reservoir species can be regenerated on polar stratospheric cloud particles (the Antarctic ozone hole mechanism: HCl + ClONO₂ → Cl₂ + HNO₃ on cloud ice → Cl₂ photolysis → 2Cl• at spring sunlight).

**The Montreal Protocol (1987)**: international agreement to phase out CFCs and other ozone-depleting substances (ODS). Most successful international environmental treaty — stratospheric Cl loading peaked ~2000 and is declining; ozone hole projected to close by ~2060-2070.

**Replacements — HCFCs, HFCs, HFOs**:
- **HCFCs (hydrochlorofluorocarbons, e.g., HCFC-22, CHClF₂)**: contain H atoms (C–H bonds) → are oxidised in the troposphere by OH• before reaching the stratosphere; less ozone-depleting potential (ODP) than CFCs but still non-zero. Phased out under the Kigali Amendment (2016).
- **HFCs (hydrofluorocarbons, e.g., HFC-134a, CH₂FCF₃)**: NO CHLORINE → ODP = 0; do not deplete ozone. BUT: they are potent greenhouse gases (HFC-134a GWP = 1430 × CO₂ over 100 years). Now being phased down (not out) under the Kigali Amendment.
- **HFOs (hydrofluoroolefins, e.g., HFO-1234yf, CF₃CF=CH₂)**: contain a C=C double bond → highly reactive in the troposphere (lifetime ~11 days) → low GWP (~4); ODP = 0. The next generation of refrigerants for automotive and building air-conditioning.

## Mental Models
**CFC stability as a double-edged sword**: the same chemical inertness that made CFCs safe and useful at ground level made them catastrophic in the stratosphere. Inertness at low altitude → they survive long enough (100-year atmospheric lifetime for CFC-12) to diffuse into the stratosphere, where the only thing "chemical enough" to break them down is the intense UV radiation. A substance that is perfectly safe in your refrigerator can be perfectly destructive in the ozone layer.

**Cl• as a serial killer**: each chlorine radical from a CFC does not destroy one ozone molecule and stop — it is regenerated after each catalytic cycle. Like a catalyst, it is never permanently used up (until a reservoir species captures it). One Cl• destroys ~10⁵ O₃ molecules — a single CFC molecule becoming ~100,000 units of ozone destruction once it reaches the stratosphere.

## Why Students Fail
Students confuse CFC-12 (refrigerant, Freon, all H replaced by F+Cl) with HCFC (still has H — less ozone depleting) with HFC (no Cl — zero ODP). They forget that Cl• is a CATALYST in ozone depletion — they say "Cl is used up in the first step." They confuse the iodoform test (positive for CH₃CO– and ethanol) with a general test for all ketones.

## Misconceptions
- **MC-1 (Type 1 — overgeneralization)**: "Any compound with fluorine depletes the ozone layer because fluorine is similar to chlorine." Probe: "HFCs (hydrofluorocarbons) contain only fluorine, no chlorine. Do they deplete ozone?" Characteristic phrase: "F is a halogen like Cl, so HFCs also cause ozone depletion." Intervention: OZONE DEPLETION by halogens is almost entirely caused by CHLORINE radicals (and bromine radicals from halons). Fluorine radicals do form from HFCs, but F• reacts with water vapour in the stratosphere extremely rapidly to form HF, which does NOT react catalytically with ozone. F• + H₂O → HF + OH•; HF is not regenerated (unlike Cl•) from reservoir species on cloud particles. The ozone depletion potential (ODP) of HFCs is effectively ZERO. Chlorine is the key atom, not fluorine per se, because of the specific chemistry of the Cl/ClO catalytic cycle and the reservoir-release mechanism on polar stratospheric cloud particles.
- **MC-2 (Type 5 — instruction-induced)**: "Cl• is used up in the first step of ozone destruction, so each CFC molecule only destroys one O₃." Probe: "After Cl• reacts with O₃ to give ClO• and O₂, what happens to the ClO•?" Characteristic phrase: "Cl• attacks ozone and then it's gone — consumed." Intervention: the Cl• is REGENERATED. Step 1: Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂ (Cl• consumed, ClO• formed). Step 2: ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂ (ClO• consumed, Cl• REGENERATED). Net: O₃ + O → 2O₂; Cl• unchanged. Cl• is a CATALYST — it is not consumed in the overall reaction. The net effect is pure ozone destruction; Cl• goes around the cycle repeatedly (~10⁵ times) until a termination step converts it to a reservoir species (HCl, ClONO₂). The "one CFC → one O₃" idea misses the catalytic amplification entirely.
- **MC-3 (Type 2 — perceptual intuition)**: "CCl₄ (carbon tetrachloride) is polar because it has four polar C–Cl bonds." Probe: "Draw the tetrahedral structure of CCl₄ and add the bond dipole vectors. What is their vector sum?" Characteristic phrase: "four C–Cl bonds must add up to a dipole." Intervention: CCl₄ has perfect TETRAHEDRAL symmetry (4 equivalent C–Cl bonds at 109.5°). Each C–Cl bond is polar (δ+C, δ−Cl), but the four bond dipole vectors point toward the four vertices of a tetrahedron and EXACTLY CANCEL by symmetry. The vector sum is ZERO. μ(CCl₄) = 0. The molecule is NONPOLAR despite having four polar bonds. This is the identical logic as CO₂ (two polar C=O bonds that cancel in the linear geometry). Polarity of a molecule requires both polar bonds AND an asymmetric arrangement — CCl₄ has the former but not the latter.

## Analogies
**Valid**: CFCs' atmospheric journey is like a long-burning fuse: they are lit at the ground (emitted), travel slowly up through the troposphere (years to decades), and finally reach the stratosphere where the fuse reaches the explosive (UV radiation + ozone). The longer the atmospheric lifetime, the longer the fuse — CFC-12 has a ~100-year lifetime; HFO-1234yf has an ~11-day lifetime (the fuse burns out long before reaching the stratosphere).

## Demonstrations
**Iodoform test demonstration**: add I₂/NaOH solution to (a) acetone (propanone, a methyl ketone — positive: yellow CHI₃ precipitate, characteristic odour); (b) ethanol (positive — oxidised in situ to acetaldehyde, then iodoform); (c) propan-2-ol (positive — oxidised to acetone); (d) pentan-2-one (positive — has CH₃CO–); (e) propan-1-ol (negative — oxidised to propanal, NOT a methyl ketone). Demonstrates specificity of the iodoform test.

**Relative density of halogenated solvents**: add a few drops of DCM, CHCl₃, or CCl₄ to water in a separating funnel — all sink below the water layer (denser than water), unlike most common organic solvents. This immediately shows the effect of halogenation on density.

2. "HFO-1234yf (CF₃CF=CH₂) is used as a refrigerant in modern car air-conditioning systems instead of HFC-134a. Explain why: (a) it has lower global warming potential than HFC-134a, and (b) it does not deplete ozone, even though it contains fluorine."

## Teaching Sequence
1. Overview: polyhalogen compounds = multiple halogens on one or more carbons; familiar examples in everyday life.
2. Key dihalomethanes/trihalomethanes: DCM (solvent), CHCl₃ (NMR, phosgene risk, anaesthetic history), CCl₄ (nonpolar despite polar bonds, toxicity), CHI₃ (iodoform test specificity).
3. CFCs: structure (all H replaced by F + Cl); properties (inert, non-toxic, non-flammable); uses; Freon naming conventions (CFC-12 = 2+1=3 pairs: C, F, Cl counting rule — CFC-XYZ: digits X+1 C, Y H, Z F, rest Cl — simplified: CFC-12 is CCl₂F₂).
4. Ozone depletion: stratospheric O₃ layer absorbs UV-B/C; CFCs reach stratosphere (chemically inert in troposphere, long lifetime); UV photolysis → Cl•; catalytic cycle (Cl• + O₃ → ClO• → Cl• regenerated); each Cl• destroys ~10⁵ O₃; Antarctic ozone hole (polar stratospheric clouds, Cl₂ release in spring).
5. Montreal Protocol: phaseout schedule; success story.
6. Replacements: HCFCs (reduced ODP, still GHG), HFCs (zero ODP but high GWP — Kigali Amendment phase-down), HFOs (zero ODP, very low GWP, short lifetime).

## Tutor Actions
- **If student says Cl• is consumed in ozone depletion**: write the two-step catalytic cycle side by side, draw an arrow showing Cl• at the start and at the end of the cycle. "What's the same on both sides?" (Cl• is present at the start and regenerated at the end.) "If Cl• appears before and after, is it consumed or catalytic?" (Catalytic.)
- **If student says CCl₄ is polar**: draw the tetrahedral Lewis structure with bond dipoles. "Which direction does each C–Cl bond dipole point?" (Toward Cl.) "Sum them as vectors — if I put four identical vectors from the centre toward tetrahedral corners, do they cancel?" (Yes — net zero.) "So the molecular dipole is..." (Zero — nonpolar.)
- **FRAGILE sign**: student knows ozone depletion by memory (Cl• + O₃, etc.) but cannot explain why Cl• is not consumed or why HFCs are safer than CFCs.

## Voice Teaching Notes
The catalytic nature of Cl• in ozone depletion is the most important conceptual point in this topic and must be made crystal clear in voice before the numbers (~10⁵ O₃ molecules per Cl•) are introduced. Ask: "In step 1, Cl• attacks O₃ and forms ClO•. Does the Cl• still exist after step 1?" (No, it's been converted to ClO•.) "In step 2, what happens to ClO•?" (It reacts with O to give Cl• + O₂.) "So Cl• has been... ?" (Regenerated.) "This is a catalytic cycle. How many O₃ molecules does ONE Cl• radical eventually destroy?" (Up to ~100,000 — before termination captures it.) The shock of that number is the most memorable moment in teaching this topic.

## Assessment Signals
- **Green**: correctly identifies DCM (solvent, dense, low flammability), CHCl₃ (NMR, anaesthetic history, phosgene risk), CCl₄ (nonpolar despite 4 polar bonds, toxic), CHI₃ (iodoform test); correctly explains iodoform test specificity (methyl ketones + ethanol); describes catalytic Cl• cycle for ozone depletion with two propagation steps; states each Cl• is catalytic (regenerated); distinguishes CFCs (all-H replaced, high ODP), HCFCs (H present, low ODP), HFCs (no Cl, zero ODP but high GWP), HFOs (C=C, zero ODP, low GWP, short lifetime).
- **Amber**: correct on most properties but says Cl• is consumed in ozone depletion; or confuses HFCs with CFCs on ODP.
- **Red**: says all halogenated compounds deplete ozone; says Cl• is consumed (not catalytic); says CCl₄ is polar.
- **Probe**: "Why does HFO-1234yf have a much shorter atmospheric lifetime than HFC-134a, and how does this affect its global warming potential? Why doesn't it deplete ozone despite containing fluorine?"

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If student cannot explain why CCl₄ is nonpolar: make the vector argument physical. "Hold four identical pens pointing outward from your fist in tetrahedral directions. Now imagine each pen has a force of 1N pulling away from your fist. Do the forces cancel?" (Yes — symmetry.) "Same for CCl₄ bond dipoles — four identical forces in tetrahedral directions sum to zero. Therefore zero net dipole, nonpolar molecule." Contrast with CH₂Cl₂ (two C–Cl and two C–H, NOT tetrahedral equivalent pairs → dipoles do NOT cancel → polar molecule despite the same geometry).

## Cross-Subject Connections
- **Environmental science**: the ozone hole over Antarctica was one of the first large-scale, scientifically documented human impacts on the global atmosphere. The Montreal Protocol is frequently cited as a model for international environmental cooperation — in contrast to the slower progress on climate agreements. Ironically, HFCs (the "safe" CFC replacement) are themselves potent greenhouse gases, contributing to climate change while no longer harming the ozone layer — illustrating that solving one environmental problem can inadvertently create another.
- **Organic synthesis**: CHCl₃ as a phosgene precursor is exploited deliberately in carbonyl chemistry — controlled oxidation of CHCl₃ produces phosgene (COCl₂), which is the most important industrial carbonyl chloride, used to synthesise polycarbonates, polyurethanes, and pharmaceuticals (e.g., carbamates). DCM is ubiquitous as a reaction solvent because it is polar enough to dissolve organic reagents and salts, yet inert under most reaction conditions and easy to remove (low bp).
