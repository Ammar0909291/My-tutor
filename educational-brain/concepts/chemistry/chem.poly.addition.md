# Addition Polymerization — `chem.poly.addition`

## Identity
- **KG ID**: chem.poly.addition
- **Subject**: Chemistry
- **Domain**: Polymers (chem.poly)
- **Prerequisites**: chem.hyd.alkenes, chem.org.reactive-intermediates
- **Difficulty**: proficient
- **Bloom level**: apply
- **Estimated hours**: 1.5

## Learning Objective
Describe the three stages of radical addition polymerisation (initiation, propagation, termination), predict the monomer from a polymer structure and vice versa, distinguish addition from condensation polymerisation, explain the role of the double bond in chain growth, and name and describe the properties of key addition polymers (polyethylene, PVC, polystyrene, PTFE, polypropylene).

## Core Understanding
**Definition**: addition polymerisation (chain-growth polymerisation) converts monomers containing a C=C double bond into a polymer via a chain reaction; no atoms are lost — the formula of the repeat unit equals the formula of the monomer. The key feature distinguishing this from condensation polymerisation: NO small molecule byproduct (e.g., no water). **Radical mechanism — three stages**: (1) **Initiation**: a radical initiator (e.g., benzoyl peroxide, ROOR → heat → 2 RO•) generates a primary radical; the radical adds to the C=C double bond of the monomer → forms a new radical at the terminal carbon (the chain-initiating radical); (2) **Propagation**: the radical adds to another monomer molecule, extending the chain and regenerating a radical at the new terminal carbon; this step repeats n times (n can be thousands) → long polymer chain radical; (3) **Termination**: two chain radicals combine (combination = coupling, gives one polymer molecule) or disproportionate (one chain gains an H from the other, giving a saturated and an unsaturated chain); the radical is destroyed and chain growth stops. Molecular mass is controlled by radical concentration and temperature. **Ionic mechanism**: addition polymerisation can also proceed via cationic or anionic intermediates (e.g., Ziegler-Natta catalyst, anionic living polymerisation) — these give more stereoregular polymers (isotactic, syndiotactic) and narrower molecular mass distributions; this is industrially important for tailoring physical properties. **Important addition polymers**: (1) **Polyethylene** (PE): monomer = ethene (CH₂=CH₂); LDPE (low-density, radical, branched chains, flexible, transparent); HDPE (high-density, Ziegler-Natta, linear chains, rigid, opaque); (2) **PVC** (poly(vinyl chloride)): monomer = chloroethene (vinyl chloride, CH₂=CHCl); rigid without plasticiser; flexible with added plasticiser (phthalate esters); uses: pipes, window frames, electrical insulation; (3) **Polystyrene** (PS): monomer = phenylethene (styrene, CH₂=CHPh); brittle, transparent; expanded PS (EPS) = polystyrene foam (Styrofoam); (4) **PTFE** (polytetrafluoroethylene, Teflon): monomer = tetrafluoroethene (CF₂=CF₂); extremely inert (all C–F bonds, no C–H); non-stick; very high temperature stability; (5) **Polypropylene** (PP): monomer = propene (CH₂=CHCH₃); isotactic PP (Ziegler-Natta) = rigid, high melting point, fibres and packaging; atactic PP = amorphous, lower mp, adhesives; (6) **Perspex/PMMA** (poly(methyl methacrylate)): monomer = methyl methacrylate; transparent, glass substitute. **Distinction from condensation polymerisation**: addition = opens the π-bond; no byproduct; repeat unit = monomer; examples = polyalkenes. Condensation = reaction between TWO functional groups (e.g., –OH + –COOH → ester + H₂O; –NH₂ + –COOH → amide + H₂O); small molecule (water or HCl) lost; examples = polyesters (PET, Dacron), polyamides (nylon). **Environmental context**: addition polymers are generally non-biodegradable (saturated C–C backbone, no hydrolysable linkages); disposal is by mechanical recycling (where possible), thermal depolymerisation, or landfill/incineration; LDPE bags persist for hundreds of years; this is a major environmental challenge.

## Mental Models
- **The paper-clip chain analogy**: each monomer is an open paper clip (with a free hook = the radical end); the chain grows by each clip snapping onto the next in sequence until two "live" clips collide and lock together (termination). The final chain is a long connected string — addition polymer.
- **π-bond as the fuel**: the π-bond stores ~260 kJ/mol (vs. σ-bond ~350 kJ/mol); when the radical adds to the π-bond, it forms a new σ-bond and releases energy (polymerisation is exothermic); the new radical is lower in energy than the old one; this energy gradient drives propagation forward spontaneously until termination.
- **Repeat unit = one monomer**: the simplest diagnostic for addition polymer identification — count the carbons in the repeat unit and match to the monomer (n × monomer → [monomer unit]ₙ). No extra atoms, no atoms lost.

## Why Students Fail
- Confusing addition and condensation polymers when writing the polymer from a monomer, forgetting that condensation polymers lose H₂O or HCl between each join.
- Writing only 2 or 3 repeat units and not showing the continuation brackets with n; or omitting n entirely.
- Believing that all chain-growth polymers use radical mechanisms — not recognising that Ziegler-Natta and ionic methods also produce addition polymers, with different stereochemistry and physical properties.

## Misconceptions
1. **"Addition polymerisation and condensation polymerisation are distinguished only by the size of the polymer chain"** (Type 1 — overgeneralization: students conflate all polymer formation; they may have heard that condensation produces "smaller" polymers or produces a byproduct, but conflate this with molecular mass rather than mechanism).
   - Probe: "Nylon is formed from hexanedioic acid and hexane-1,6-diamine. Is this addition or condensation polymerisation? What is lost in each monomer-monomer reaction?"
   - Characteristic phrase: "both types make long chains so it's the same" / "addition if the chain is long enough"
   - Intervention: nylon is condensation — each amide bond formation ejects H₂O (or HCl if an acyl chloride is used); the repeat unit has FEWER atoms than the sum of its two monomers. Addition polymers: repeat unit = monomer formula, no atom lost. Chemical test: burn the polymer — if water is released only from combustion (not from the polymerisation step), it's addition.

2. **"The radical in addition polymerisation is immediately destroyed after initiator decomposition"** (Type 2 — perceptual intuition: students know radicals are reactive and assume they instantly react with the nearest available bond; they don't picture the chain-growth cascade where the radical "rides" the propagating end for thousands of steps before being destroyed).
   - Probe: "In radical polymerisation, how many monomer units does each initiator radical typically add to the chain before termination?"
   - Characteristic phrase: "the radical reacts with just one monomer then it's gone"
   - Intervention: the initiator radical adds to a monomer → new radical; this new radical adds to another monomer → new radical; and so on, typically 1,000–100,000 times per initiation event. The average degree of polymerisation (DP) is 10³–10⁵. Termination is a bimolecular event that requires two chain radicals to meet — statistically rare in the dilute propagating solution.

3. **"All polyethylene is the same regardless of how it is made"** (Type 5 — instruction-induced: introductory courses teach "ethene polymerises to polyethylene" without distinguishing LDPE from HDPE, or radical from Ziegler-Natta processes; students do not connect synthesis method to physical properties).
   - Probe: "Why is HDPE suitable for pipes and water containers but LDPE is used for plastic bags?"
   - Characteristic phrase: "they're all the same since the monomer is just ethene"
   - Intervention: radical polymerisation (free radical at moderate pressure) produces LDPE — branched chains (short-chain branching from backbiting) → chains cannot pack closely → lower crystallinity → lower density → flexible, soft. Ziegler-Natta (TiCl₄/AlEt₃ catalyst) produces HDPE — linear chains, no branching → tight packing → higher crystallinity → higher density → rigid, stronger. Same monomer, different mechanism, profoundly different material.

## Analogies
- **Good**: radical addition polymerisation is like a "chain letter": the initiator starts the letter; each recipient adds their name (monomer unit) and sends it on; the letter grows until two copies collide and both chains decide to stop (termination). The letter (polymer) grows to thousands of names long from a single initiating event.
- **Anti-analogy**: Do NOT say "polymerisation is just repeated alkene addition, like HBr adding twice" — in HBr addition, the electrophile (H⁺) is consumed and cannot propagate; in radical polymerisation, the radical persists across thousands of steps without being consumed until termination. The mechanism is fundamentally different from small-molecule addition.

## Demonstrations
- **Polystyrene dissolution in acetone**: expanded polystyrene (EPS foam) dissolves/collapses in acetone in seconds (acetone is a good solvent for polystyrene). Demonstrates the amorphous, low-crystallinity structure of EPS and the identity of polystyrene (C₈H₈ repeat unit = styrene).
- **LDPE vs. HDPE comparison**: hold up a LDPE bag and a HDPE container; press them; snap the bag vs. the container; compare translucency — LDPE transparent/flexible; HDPE opaque/rigid. Same chemical formula (–CH₂CH₂–)ₙ; different structure (branched vs. linear) → different properties. This directly illustrates the structure-property relationship.

## Discovery Questions
1. Draw the structure of the repeat unit of PVC and polystyrene. Name the monomers.
2. An addition polymer has the repeat unit –CH₂–CHBr–. What is the monomer? Is this vinyl bromide or some other compound?
3. Why is PTFE (Teflon) non-stick? Use your knowledge of C–F bond strength and intermolecular forces.
4. Compare the environmental impact of addition polymers to biodegradable condensation polymers (e.g., PLA, poly(lactic acid)). What structural feature of addition polymers causes their persistence?

## Teaching Sequence
1. **What is a polymer**: repeat unit, monomer, degree of polymerisation, molecular mass distribution.
2. **Addition vs. condensation**: key diagnostic — byproduct or not; repeat unit vs. monomer formula.
3. **Radical mechanism**: initiator decomposition; addition to C=C → radical; propagation chain; termination (combination or disproportionation).
4. **Key addition polymers**: table of monomer → polymer name → uses; LDPE, HDPE, PVC, PS, PP, PTFE, PMMA.
5. **LDPE vs. HDPE**: branching from radical backbiting; linear from Ziegler-Natta; structure → property link.
6. **Ionic mechanisms** (brief): cationic and anionic addition; Ziegler-Natta and stereoregularity (isotactic/atactic); overview only.
7. **Environmental issues**: non-biodegradability of C–C backbone; recycling codes; alternatives.

## Tutor Actions
- **If addition/condensation confusion**: ask "is any atom lost between two monomers when they join?" — no = addition; yes = condensation.
- **If radical persistence is underestimated**: ask "how many monomer additions happen per initiation?" — ask student to estimate the chain length; typical Mw 50,000–500,000 / MW(ethene) 28 ≈ 1,800–18,000 additions per chain.
- **If LDPE/HDPE distinction is missed**: draw one branched and one linear polyethylene chain; ask "which one packs together more tightly?" — linear; "which is therefore denser and more rigid?" — HDPE.

## Voice Teaching Notes
- "Addition = add together = no atoms lost. Condensation = condense = lose water (or HCl) at every join. Say this before every polymer question."
- "Radical polymerisation: initiation, propagation, termination — always these three stages. Name all three before drawing the mechanism."
- "LDPE = low density = branched = flexible = bags. HDPE = high density = linear = rigid = containers, pipes."

## Assessment Signals
- **Green**: writes full mechanism with initiation/propagation/termination; derives monomer from polymer repeat unit correctly; distinguishes LDPE from HDPE; contrasts addition vs. condensation with byproduct criterion.
- **Amber**: correct mechanism but terminates chain after one or two propagations; correctly classifies polymers but cannot explain LDPE/HDPE difference; does not mention the Ziegler-Natta route.
- **Red**: confuses addition and condensation; cannot write the radical mechanism; believes all polyethylene is identical.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
- Mechanism confusion: break the mechanism into exactly three labelled sections; require students to name the stage before drawing it; only once all three are named do they begin drawing.
- Addition/condensation confusion: give three examples of each and ask which class; identify the diagnostic (byproduct H₂O or HCl) in the condensation examples.
- LDPE/HDPE: show the physical samples or images; anchor the branched vs. linear chain structure to the observable property difference; this physical demonstration is more memorable than the mechanism explanation alone.

## Memory Hooks
- **Addition = no byproduct; repeat unit = monomer**
- **Three stages: initiation → propagation (×10³–10⁵) → termination**
- **LDPE = branched (radical), HDPE = linear (Ziegler-Natta)**
- **PVC = vinyl chloride; PS = styrene; PTFE = tetrafluoroethylene; PP = propene**
- **PTFE: all C–F bonds → inert, non-stick, heat-stable**

## Transfer Connections
- **Alkene reactions** (chem.hyd.alkenes): the radical chain mechanism in addition polymerisation applies the same radical reactivity to alkenes that is discussed in alkane halogenation; the addition step (radical + alkene → new carbon radical) is the polymerisation step.
- **Reactive intermediates** (chem.org.reactive-intermediates): radicals in polymerisation are the same species (carbon radicals) studied in radical substitution; carbocations appear in cationic polymerisation; carbanions in anionic polymerisation — all three reactive intermediate types are represented in polymer chemistry.
- **Condensation polymerisation** (chem.poly.condensation, where available): the contrast between addition and condensation is the primary structural distinction in polymer chemistry; mastering both nodes gives a complete picture.
- **Stereochemistry and Ziegler-Natta**: isotactic and syndiotactic polymers (from Ziegler-Natta) have higher crystallinity and melting points than atactic polymers (from radical) — a direct application of stereochemistry (chem.stereo.stereoisomers) to material properties.

## Cross-Subject Connections
- **Environmental science**: microplastic pollution is predominantly from addition polymers (polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene fragments); their persistence follows from the inert C–C backbone; they accumulate in food chains; understanding the chemistry explains why they are not biodegradable.
- **Materials engineering**: polymer crystallinity, glass transition temperature, and Young's modulus all correlate with chain structure (branching, tacticity, intermolecular forces) — topics at the interface of chemistry and materials engineering; HDPE pipes must resist creep (slow deformation under load) at installation temperatures.
- **Biotechnology**: addition polymers are used as scaffolds in tissue engineering (PMMA bone cement, polyacrylamide gels for electrophoresis); synthetic biology uses radical-initiated hydrogel polymerisation to encapsulate cells.
