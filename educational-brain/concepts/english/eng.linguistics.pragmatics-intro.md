## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify the literal meaning and likely actual communicative intent for indirect speech acts.
2. Explain what conversational expectation (relevance, sufficient information) enables a specific implicature inference.
3. Apply the "would ten listeners agree" test to classify an exchange as genuine implicature versus genuine ambiguity.
4. Distinguish implicature from vagueness given a statement.
5. Fully analyze a new conversational exchange for indirect speech acts, enabling conversational expectations, and implicature reliability.

## Core Understanding

This concept develops `eng.linguistics.semantics-intro`'s literal/contextual meaning preview into the full study of context-dependent meaning. Two corrections define it. First, **what a speaker literally says is not always exactly what they mean** — indirect speech acts use a sentence with one literal meaning (a question about ability) to accomplish a different communicative purpose (a request); "Can you pass the salt?" is literally a yes/no question, but pragmatically a request, and a literal "Yes, I can" with no action would not satisfy what the speaker actually wanted. Second, **implicature is not the same as vagueness or unclear communication** — implicature is meaning that is reliably CONVEYED but not literally stated, a precise, predictable inference a competent listener confidently draws based on shared conversational expectations (like relevance), fundamentally different from genuine ambiguity where different listeners would draw incompatible conclusions; "I have to work that night" reliably implicates "no" to a party invitation, the opposite of vague or unclear.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "what a speaker literally says is always exactly what they mean; implicature just means being vague or unclear."**
The simulation: interpret "Can you pass the salt?" as a literal ability question, and treat any indirect response as simply fuzzy or ambiguous communication.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — "Can you pass the salt?" at a dinner table, asked whether a literal "Yes, I can" with no action would satisfy the speaker; and "Are you coming to the party?" / "I have to work that night," asked whether this reliably implicates "no" and whether that's the same as being unclear.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Literal meaning and speaker intent can systematically differ. Implicature is a reliable inference, not vagueness."

**Intermediate — "I recognize indirect speech acts now, but I still sometimes treat a reliable implicature as just an ambiguous, unclear statement."**
The simulation: correctly identifies indirect requests and similar speech acts but hasn't yet reliably distinguished genuine implicature from actual ambiguity.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct application of the "would ten listeners agree" test to classify exchanges.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Test it: would nearly any listener draw the same inference? If yes, that's reliable implicature, not vagueness."

**Advanced — "I identify indirect speech acts, explain the conversational expectation enabling inference, test implicature reliability, and analyze a new exchange integrating all three."**
The simulation: integrates indirect-speech-act identification, cooperative-principle explanation, and reliability-testing into one coherent contextual-meaning analysis. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: the two direct unlocks — discourse analysis (meaning across multi-turn conversations) and sociolinguistics (how social context shapes language use).
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you single-utterance pragmatic inference. Discourse analysis extends this across whole conversations; sociolinguistics examines how social context shapes it."

**Expert — "at fluency, indirect-speech-act recognition and implicature-reliability testing happen as an integrated part of ordinary conversational understanding."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit identify-explain-test routine is a scaffold for a habit that, at true fluency, is inseparable from ordinary competent communication.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **most everyday communication is processed automatically and unconsciously**, so a listener normally never notices the gap between literal form and intended meaning at all — it takes a deliberate, unusual analytical stance to separate a sentence's literal semantic content from what it's actually being used to accomplish.

The second mechanism is that **implicature and vagueness both involve meaning that isn't literally stated**, so the surface similarity (both require the listener to "fill in" something) obscures the crucial difference in reliability — implicature depends on systematic conversational expectations that produce consistent inferences across listeners, while genuine ambiguity produces disagreement.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-WHAT-A-SPEAKER-LITERALLY-SAYS-IS-ALWAYS-EXACTLY-WHAT-THEY-MEAN
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-A — includes the "Can you pass the salt?" discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from ordinary communication being processed automatically, so the literal/intended-meaning gap normally goes unnoticed and unconsciously bridged, making the naive literal-equals-intended assumption feel intuitively obvious until specifically examined.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **use a concrete, universally-recognized indirect speech act** (the salt-passing request), since the fix requires the learner to notice a gap they normally never consciously process, which an abstract explanation alone wouldn't surface.
- **Verification of death**: given three sentences with conventional indirect uses, the learner identifies the literal meaning and the likely actual communicative intent for each.

### MC-B-IMPLICATURE-MEANS-THE-SAME-AS-LITERAL-VAGUENESS-OR-JUST-BEING-UNCLEAR
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — includes the "I have to work that night" discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the genuinely accurate observation that implicature isn't literally stated, extended into assuming any non-literally-stated meaning is equally unreliable or unclear, when implicature is specifically the reliable, predictable subset.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair supplies the **concrete "would ten listeners agree" test**, since the fix requires a procedural discrimination tool, not merely an assertion that implicature is "different from" vagueness.
- **Verification of death**: given two exchanges (one genuine implicature, one genuinely ambiguous), the learner applies the test to classify each correctly.

## Analogies

**Best — the Door-Knock-and-Chess-Move Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a knock on a door, universally understood as "let me in" beyond its literal physical sound, directly seeds the literal-versus-intent fix; a chess player's subtle, deliberate, rule-governed strategic move directly seeds the reliability-not-vagueness fix.
*Breaking point*: a door knock's conventional meaning is nearly universal and culturally fixed, while indirect speech acts' specific conventional forms vary somewhat across languages and cultures — useful for the beyond-the-literal-signal intuition, less precise for implying every indirect form is as universally conventional as a knock.
*Breaking point (chess)*: a chess move's rules are formally, explicitly codified, while conversational expectations (the cooperative principle) are informal, unstated norms — useful for the precise-not-random intuition, less precise for the degree of formal codification involved.

**Alternative — a nod versus a shrug in response to a question.** A nod (a conventional, reliable signal for "yes") functions like implicature — nearly everyone reads it the same way — while an ambiguous shrug (which could mean "I don't know," "I don't care," or "maybe") functions like genuine vagueness, since different observers might interpret it differently.
*Breaking point*: gestures are a different communication channel than the specifically LINGUISTIC utterance-based implicature this concept examines, making the parallel structurally similar but not identical in mechanism.

**Visual anchor — the Door-Knock-and-Chess-Move Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"People always mean exactly what they say, word for word."** Directly installs MC-A as a stated claim.
- **"If something isn't stated directly, it's just vague and open to interpretation."** Directly installs MC-B as a stated claim.

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Door-Knock-and-Chess-Move Anchor (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Does a knock mean anything beyond its literal sound?" Then connect to indirect speech acts.
2. **The "Can you pass the salt?" collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-A. *Predict first*: "Would a literal 'Yes, I can' with no action satisfy the speaker?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both pragmatic facts are directly checkable by testing real utterances against the literal/intent gap and the ten-listeners reliability test.

1. **Need**: "If someone asks 'Can you pass the salt?' and you just say 'yes' and do nothing, have you actually answered what they wanted?"
2. **Playground**: the learner examines several indirect utterances freely, guessing their actual intent, before being told the official indirect-speech-act terminology.
3. **Invention**: "Can you find a response to a question that doesn't literally answer it, but that everyone would understand the same way?"
4. **Collision**: present "I have to work that night" as a party-invitation response; ask whether this is reliable implicature or vague.
5. **Formalization**: name the two pragmatic facts — literal meaning and intent can systematically differ; implicature is reliable, not vague — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "What's said and what's meant can differ — and a reliable inference isn't a vague guess."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Literal meaning vs. speaker intent (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting MC-A with the concrete indirect-speech-act example.
- **The cooperative principle (TA-2) comes second**, explaining the mechanism (shared conversational expectations) that makes reliable inference possible, once the literal/intent gap from TA-1 is accepted.
- **Implicature as reliable, not vague (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting MC-B once the cooperative-principle foundation from TA-2 is available to explain WHY implicature is reliable.
- **Full application (TA-4) comes last**, integrating indirect-speech-act identification, the enabling mechanism, and reliability-testing into one synthesis task — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning when identifying a speaker's actual intent, or when applying the reliability test**, since a correct-looking interpretation with no stated literal/intent distinction or ten-listeners reasoning cannot be distinguished from an intuitive guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated literal-versus-intent distinction** ("literally this is a question about ability, but they actually mean...") applied to a new utterance — the direct behavioral signature of the MC-A fix taking hold.
- **A stated reliability check** ("would basically everyone read this response the same way?") applied to a new ambiguous-looking exchange — direct evidence the MC-B fix is internalized.
- **An indirect utterance answered purely literally**, or an implicature dismissed as "just unclear" without applying the reliability test — the direct behavioral signature of either misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"What's literally said and what's actually meant can differ — and a reliable inference isn't the same as a vague guess."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"Isn't 'can you pass the salt' really just asking if I'm able to?"** — take this as a reasonable literal reading before the indirect convention is noticed; respond by testing the response: "If you just said 'yes' and didn't pass it, would that satisfy the person who asked?"
- **"That response doesn't literally say 'no,' so isn't it just unclear?"** — a reasonable-sounding literalist standard; respond with the reliability test: "Would ten different people all read that response the same way? If so, that's a reliable inference, not vagueness."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full new-exchange analysis down to **identifying the literal meaning and actual intent of a single, highly conventional indirect request, with no reliability-testing task demanded.** Isolates the most basic literal/intent distinction before returning to fuller analysis.
- **Never shrink to "just say what you think the person meant."** This licenses an unanalyzed intuitive guess without the literal/intent distinction or reliability-testing discipline this concept specifically builds.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: the two direct unlocks — discourse analysis (extending inference across multi-turn conversations) and sociolinguistics (how social context shapes language use).
- **Real-world**: correctly interpreting indirect requests, hints, and polite refusals in everyday conversation and across cultures (where indirectness conventions vary), and recognizing miscommunication that arises when one party interprets an utterance too literally or too liberally relative to the other's intended pragmatic meaning.
- **Expert transfer**: the durable skill is **distinguishing what is literally communicated from what is actually, reliably conveyed through shared context and convention, and calibrating trust in an inference by its reliability rather than its explicitness** — the same reasoning used in cross-cultural communication training, negotiation (reading between the lines reliably versus over-interpreting), and any interpretive task distinguishing genuine signal from noise.
