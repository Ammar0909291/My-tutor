## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Explain the difference between descriptive linguistics (documenting how language actually is used and varies) and prescriptive grammar (rules about how language "should" be used).
2. Identify at least three major subfields of linguistics and briefly describe what each studies.
3. Explain why linguists study language variation (dialects, registers, historical change) as legitimate data rather than as "errors" to be corrected.
4. Sort a genuinely diverse set of linguistic research questions into their correct subfields.
5. Analyze a real, observable language phenomenon using a descriptive, non-judgmental lens and correctly identify which subfield(s) it belongs to.

## Core Understanding

Linguistics is a **descriptive science**, not a set of prescriptive rules — this is the concept's first load-bearing fact, and the distinction matters precisely because the two activities can look superficially similar (both discuss "how language works") while pursuing genuinely opposite goals: prescriptive grammar states how language *should* be used according to a style convention (never split an infinitive), while linguistics documents and explains how language *actually is* used and varies, including "non-standard" dialects and forms, treating all of it as legitimate data rather than error to be corrected. A linguist studying why a regional dialect consistently uses a particular grammatical construction is not correcting that dialect's speakers — they are documenting and explaining a real, rule-governed pattern, exactly as a biologist documents animal behavior without judging it against how animals "should" behave. The concept's second, equally load-bearing fact is that linguistics is **not a single narrow topic about grammar** — it comprises multiple genuinely distinct subfields studying different aspects of language: phonetics/phonology studies sounds, morphology studies word structure, syntax studies sentence structure, semantics studies meaning, and sociolinguistics studies how social factors shape language use, each a real, separately-developed branch of the discipline, much as medicine branches into cardiology, neurology, and immunology rather than remaining one undifferentiated topic.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "linguistics is basically the study of correct grammar, and it's all one single subject."**
The runnable simulation: assume a linguist's job is to identify and correct "bad grammar," and that any linguistics content beyond grammar rules would be surprising or off-topic. This is a reasonable default, since "linguistics" sounds etymologically close to "language rules," and the learner's own prior English instruction has, until now, been substantially rule-oriented (grammar, spelling, punctuation).
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — comparing a linguist's actual research question (studying a dialect's grammatical pattern) against a style-guide rule ("never split an infinitive"), and being asked which one is trying to correct speakers versus understand them; and being shown four genuinely different linguistic research questions and asked whether they're all the same kind of question.
*Shelf-life warning at replacement*: "Linguistics describes and explains how language actually works and varies — it doesn't prescribe how language should be used. And it branches into several genuinely distinct subfields, not one narrow topic."

**Intermediate — "I understand linguistics is descriptive, not prescriptive, but I still think of it as basically one subject, just studied scientifically rather than prescriptively."**
The simulation: correctly separates description from prescription, but hasn't yet recognized that "studying language scientifically" itself branches into several genuinely distinct areas (sounds, word structure, sentence structure, meaning, social variation) rather than remaining one undifferentiated scientific approach to grammar.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — being shown four research questions ("why do babies babble certain sounds first," "why does English pluralize 'cat' and 'ox' differently," "why is 'colorless green ideas sleep furiously' grammatical but meaningless," "why do teenagers develop distinct vocabulary") and asked whether these are studying the same or genuinely different aspects of language.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Linguistics isn't one topic studied scientifically — it's several genuinely different topics (sounds, word-building, sentence structure, meaning, social variation), each its own subfield."

**Advanced — "I can explain the descriptive/prescriptive distinction, sort research questions into the correct subfield, and explain why linguists treat language variation as legitimate data rather than error."**
The simulation: applies both structural facts together — analyzing any language phenomenon descriptively (not judgmentally) and correctly identifying which subfield(s) it belongs to. This is the target model, matching TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: the two direct unlocks (`eng.linguistics.phonology-intro`, `eng.linguistics.morphology-intro`), where this concept's survey-level introduction to the sound-systems and word-structure subfields becomes the entry point into their systematic, in-depth study.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the map of the discipline and its descriptive stance. The next steps go deep into two specific territories on that map — sound systems and word structure."

**Expert — "at full disciplinary fluency, the subfields are recognized as deeply interconnected, not cleanly separable — a single phenomenon (language change, child language acquisition, bilingualism) routinely draws on phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics, and sociolinguistics simultaneously, with the five-subfield map serving as a pedagogical entry point rather than the discipline's actual internal structure."**
Named here to mark the arc; this concept's clean five-subfield sort is a deliberately simplified scaffold for a discipline that, at genuine expertise, is understood as far more interconnected — named here so the tutor understands the simplification is a teaching tool, not the field's actual self-understanding.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant failure mechanism is that **the learner's entire prior English education, up to this point, has been substantially prescriptive** — correct spelling, correct punctuation, correct grammar — giving no prior occasion to encounter language study framed any other way, so assuming linguistics continues that same prescriptive project is a completely reasonable extrapolation from everything experienced so far, not an arbitrary error.

The second mechanism is that **the everyday word "grammar" is used loosely to cover almost everything about how language works** (people say "that's bad grammar" about pronunciation, word choice, and sentence structure alike), so a learner reasonably assumes a discipline that studies "how language works" must simply be a more careful, scientific version of the same undifferentiated "grammar" concept, with no prior signal that the underlying phenomena (sounds, word-building, sentence structure, meaning, social use) are actually separable, differently-mechanismed areas of study.

The third mechanism, specific to learners with substantial prior prescriptive grammar instruction (per the Blueprint's own adaptive flag), is that **this concept requires a genuine paradigm shift — from "rules to follow" to "phenomena to study"** — not merely new factual content layered onto an existing frame, and a learner whose prior instruction has been unusually rule-heavy may reasonably need more repeated exposure to settle into the new frame than a learner with lighter prior prescriptive emphasis.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns the trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-LINGUISTICS-IS-THE-STUDY-OF-CORRECT-GRAMMAR-RULES
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-A — includes the linguist's-research-question/style-guide-rule discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the learner's entire prior prescriptive English education, extended into a domain (linguistics as a scientific discipline) that is specifically, deliberately non-prescriptive.
- **Teaching consequence of the birth type**: because the prescriptive instruction the learner has received up to now wasn't itself wrong for its own purpose (formal writing does have real conventions worth following), the repair must **distinguish two legitimate but different activities** rather than dismissing prescriptive grammar as illegitimate — the Blueprint's own bridge text does this by naming both as real activities, just fundamentally different ones.
- **Verification of death**: given a set of activities including both genuinely descriptive and genuinely prescriptive examples, the learner correctly sorts them and can explain why a linguist studying "incorrect" grammar is documenting, not correcting.

### MC-B-LINGUISTICS-IS-JUST-ONE-SINGLE-SUBJECT-ABOUT-GRAMMAR
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-B — includes the four-research-questions discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 3 (language contamination), where the everyday, undifferentiated use of "grammar" to cover essentially all of language gets carried into a technical context where the underlying phenomena are actually separable into distinct subfields.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **a concrete branching structure the learner can map new content onto** (the medical-specialties analogy), since simply being told "linguistics has subfields" is less useful than having a five-part sorting framework to actively apply.
- **Verification of death**: given a novel set of linguistic research questions, the learner correctly sorts each into its subfield rather than treating them all as instances of one undifferentiated topic.

## Analogies

**Best — the Biologist-and-Medical-Specialties Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a biologist documenting wolf behavior without judgment directly seeds the descriptive-not-prescriptive fix; a diagram of medical specialties branching from one root discipline (medicine) directly seeds the multiple-subfields fix.
*Breaking point*: medical specialties are formally, institutionally separated (distinct training, distinct certifications, distinct departments), while linguistic subfields, though genuinely distinct, interact and overlap far more fluidly in actual research practice — useful for the branching-discipline intuition, less precise for implying the subfields are as cleanly separable in practice as medical specialties are administratively.

**Alternative — an ecologist studying an invasive species' spread.** The ecologist doesn't judge the invasive species as "behaving badly" — they document how and why it spreads, exactly as a linguist documents how and why a language change spreads through a population, without treating either as a moral failing to be corrected.
*Breaking point*: an invasive species' spread is usually treated as an ecological problem to be actively managed, while a linguistic change or dialect feature is not treated by linguists as a "problem" requiring intervention at all — useful for the document-without-judgment intuition, less precise for implying full neutrality about consequences, which the ecology case complicates.

**Visual anchor — the Biologist-and-Medical-Specialties Anchor** as already described above, doubling as both the primary teaching metaphor and the actual working instrument this concept's core teaching is organized around.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"A linguist's job is basically to be a grammar expert who knows the rules better than anyone."** Directly installs MC-A as a stated role description, precisely the prescriptive-authority framing this concept's first misconception's repair must counter.
- **"Linguistics is grammar, just studied more seriously."** Directly installs MC-B by implying greater rigor within the same single topic, rather than genuine branching into distinct subfields.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Biologist-and-Medical-Specialties Anchor (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Do you think a linguist's job is to correct people's grammar?" Then present the biologist parallel and resolve.
2. **The linguist's-research-question/style-guide-rule collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-A. *Predict first*: "Is this linguist trying to fix the dialect's speakers?" Then resolve.
3. **The four-research-questions sort (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-B. *Predict first*: "Are these four questions all really the same kind of question?" Then sort into subfields and resolve.
4. **The full descriptive analysis of a novel phenomenon (learner activity, TA-4).** *Predict first*: "What subfield(s) do you think this phenomenon belongs to, and should we judge it as an error?" Then analyze and confirm together.

## Discovery Questions

This concept is well suited to **guided discovery for both structural facts**, since the evidence for each (description-versus-prescription; subfield branching) is directly checkable through the learner's own sorting of real research questions and activities.

1. **Need**: "Is a linguist's job to fix people's grammar mistakes, or something else?"
2. **Playground**: the learner sorts a mixed set of activities (writing a style guide, studying child language acquisition, correcting a friend's sentence) freely, before being told the "official" descriptive/prescriptive labels.
3. **Invention**: "For the ones you called 'linguistics,' are they all studying the same kind of thing?" The learner discovers the subfield distinctions from their own sorted set.
4. **Collision**: present a genuinely non-standard dialect feature and ask whether a linguist would treat it as an error or as data.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — linguistics describes rather than prescribes, and it branches into distinct subfields — from the learner's own sorting evidence.
6. **Compression**: "Describe, don't prescribe — and know which branch you're in."

## Teaching Sequence

The pedagogical logic behind this arc:

- **Descriptive vs. prescriptive (TA-1) is established first**, directly confronting MC-A before any subfield content is introduced, since the entire discipline's stance (documenting, not judging) must be secure before the specific subfields' content can be approached in the right spirit.
- **Language variation as legitimate data (TA-2) comes second**, extending TA-1's descriptive stance specifically to the emotionally loaded case of "non-standard" forms, dialects, and slang — building the full depth of the descriptive commitment before the subfield-branching content is introduced.
- **The major subfields (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting MC-B once the descriptive stance (TA-1/TA-2) is secure, since understanding what each subfield studies is more meaningful once the learner already appreciates that all of them study *without judgment*.
- **The full application (TA-4) comes last**, requiring the learner to apply both the descriptive lens (TA-1/TA-2) and subfield identification (TA-3) to a genuinely novel phenomenon — matching this program's established sequencing principle that a concept's most integrative task is reserved for its culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

This concept's core evidence is **spoken explanation and classification with stated reasoning** — the learner's descriptive/prescriptive judgments and subfield sorts, plus the reasoning behind each, since a correct-sounding classification with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated descriptive framing applied spontaneously to a novel, potentially judgment-inviting language phenomenon** ("a linguist would just document how that pattern works, not call it wrong") — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A's repair having been internalized.
- **A subfield judgment accompanied by a stated reason naming the actual phenomenon type** (sound, word-structure, sentence-structure, meaning, or social-variation) — the direct behavioral signature of genuine subfield understanding rather than a lucky sort.
- **Immediate, judgmental language ("that's just wrong," "that's bad grammar") applied to a genuinely non-standard but rule-governed language feature presented as data** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Describe how language actually works — don't judge it against how it 'should' work."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But isn't 'I seen it' just wrong grammar, though?"** — take this as a genuine, well-reinforced prior judgment, not a mistake to be dismissed; respond by adding rather than contradicting: "It's not the standard, most widely-used form, that's true — but from a linguistics point of view, it's a real, consistent grammatical pattern in the dialects that use it, which is exactly the kind of thing a linguist would study, not correct."
- **"I thought this whole course was about learning correct English"** — a genuine, reasonable expectation given the prior curriculum's substantially prescriptive framing; respond by naming the shift directly: "Most of what you've learned so far has focused on conventions for formal writing, which is a real and useful skill — this concept is stepping back to look at language as a whole, scientific subject, which studies everything language does, not just the formal-writing rules."
- **The smaller question to shrink to**: from a full descriptive analysis with subfield identification, down to **a single, clear-cut descriptive-versus-prescriptive sort with no subfield-identification demand at all.** This removes the subfield-branching complexity entirely, isolating the most basic stance-shift before returning to the harder, subfield-integrated task.
- **Never shrink to "just say language study is basically grammar, but more scientific."** This directly licenses MC-B's single-subject framing, undermining the subfield-branching understanding this concept specifically builds.
