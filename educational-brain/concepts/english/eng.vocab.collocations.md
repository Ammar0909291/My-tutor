# Collocations — `eng.vocab.collocations`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.collocations` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Collocations" —
  combinations of words that habitually co-occur in natural English
  ("make a decision" rather than "do a decision"), taught with explicit
  attention to why synonym substitution fails and how collocations
  differ from idioms.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.connotation-denotation` — load-bearing
  because collocational judgment is a specific application of the same
  fine-grained word-choice sensitivity that concept built (noticing
  subtle usage/connotation differences between near-synonyms), now
  applied to which words habitually CO-OCCUR rather than which carry
  which emotional shading.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.vocab.idioms`, `eng.vocab.phrasal-verbs`.
  A learner cannot understand what makes a phrase NON-collocational
  (i.e., non-transparent, an idiom) without first understanding what
  makes a phrase collocational (transparent, usage-conventional); the
  puzzle-piece, shape-not-meaning framing this concept installs
  transfers directly to phrasal verbs' own fixed, non-logically-
  derivable particle pairings.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Recognize verb+noun collocations (make a decision, do a favor, take
   a photo, have a shower) and notice that near-synonym verbs don't
   cross-substitute freely.
2. Recognize adjective+noun collocations (heavy rain, strong coffee,
   deep sleep) and identify why a near-synonym substitution sounds
   "off" even when technically understandable.
3. Apply the transparency test — can the phrase's meaning be derived
   from its individual words? — to distinguish collocations from
   idioms.
4. Detect an unconventional word pairing in a piece of writing and
   correct it to the conventional collocation, explaining why the
   substituted word doesn't fit despite a close dictionary meaning.
5. Generate correct collocational pairings for new words, building an
   original small collocation reference set.

## Core Understanding

A collocation is a specific combination of words that habitually
co-occurs in natural English usage — not because logic or synonym-level
meaning dictates the pairing, but because that is simply the pattern
millions of speakers have settled on through actual use: English
speakers say "make a decision," not "do a decision," even though "make"
and "do" both can mean roughly "to perform an action" in other contexts
(and "do" IS correct in "do a favor," showing the restriction isn't
about "do" being wrong in general, only wrong specifically with
"decision"). Genuine synonym-level meaning-matching does NOT predict
collocational correctness — collocations must be learned as fixed
pairings through exposure (reading, listening, encountering the actual
pattern), not derived logically from a synonym list. A second, equally
load-bearing fact is that collocations and idioms, while both "fixed"
word combinations, work through completely different mechanisms: a
collocation's meaning is fully TRANSPARENT — "heavy rain" means exactly
what its individual words suggest (rain that is heavy/intense), it is
simply that English happens to pair "heavy" with "rain" rather than,
say, "strong rain" — while an idiom's meaning is entirely HIDDEN and
non-literal — "it's raining cats and dogs" cannot be derived from its
individual words at all; the whole expression must simply be known.
Applying a transparency test (can I understand this phrase by
understanding each word normally?) reliably separates the two
categories, which otherwise share the surface feature of being
"memorized fixed phrases."

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "if a word is a dictionary synonym for the word
  that's 'supposed' to go in a phrase, the substitution should work
  fine"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to
  correct — a natural extension of the prerequisite concept's own
  synonym-comparison framework, which correctly established that words
  can share denotation, giving no built-in signal that shared denotation
  does NOT extend to predicting collocational fit.
- **Intermediate model — "I can recognize common verb+noun and
  adjective+noun collocations and notice that near-synonym verbs/
  adjectives don't freely substitute"**: installed by TA-1 and TA-2's
  exposure practice, using the Component 3 Puzzle-Piece Pairing Board
  anchor to make the shape-not-meaning fit concrete. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly matching a genuinely novel set of verbs to their
  conventional noun pairings without relying on synonym logic.
- **Advanced model — "collocations and idioms are distinguishable using
  a transparency test — can I derive the meaning from the individual
  words, or is it hidden?"**: installed by TA-3's transparency-test
  practice, resolving MC-COLLOCATIONS-ARE-JUST-A-FANCY-NAME-FOR-IDIOMS
  through the felt contrast between "heavy rain" (transparent) and
  "raining cats and dogs" (opaque). Upgrade trigger: correctly sorting a
  fresh mixed set of collocations and idioms using the transparency
  test, explaining the criterion rather than only producing a correct
  sort.
- **Expert model — "I can detect and correct an unconventional word
  pairing in real writing, and productively generate new collocational
  pairings for unfamiliar words"**: installed by TA-4's detect-and-
  correct task (resolving MC-IF-A-SYNONYM-FITS-THE-DEFINITION-IT-FITS-
  THE-PHRASE at its most demanding level) and TA-5's personal
  collocation-bank-building capstone. Per the Blueprint's own explicit
  mastery gate, certification requires BOTH MP-1 (synonym-substitution
  rejection) and MP-2 (collocation/idiom distinction) to pass — a
  learner who matches known collocations correctly (MP-3) but still
  substitutes synonyms freely or confuses collocations with idioms has
  not yet internalized the usage-convention principle this concept
  requires.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own explicit mastery-
  gate rule (Component 7's S9 protocol), do not certify mastery on
  familiar-pairing matching alone (MP-3) — both MP-1 and MP-2 must
  independently pass, since matching already-taught pairs is a
  fundamentally easier task than the usage-convention judgment (synonym-
  substitution rejection, collocation/idiom distinction) this concept
  is actually built to install.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of genuinely useful prior vocabulary knowledge. The synonym-
substitution failure exists because synonym-level meaning-matching is a
powerful, generally reliable strategy for most vocabulary tasks — the
prerequisite concept itself validated close attention to shared
meaning between near-synonyms — so a learner reasonably extends
"similar meaning implies similar usage" into collocational contexts,
with nothing in ordinary vocabulary practice (which usually tests
meaning, not habitual pairing) marking that collocations are governed
by usage CONVENTION rather than logical meaning-derivation. The
collocation-idiom-conflation failure exists because both categories
genuinely share the surface feature of being "fixed" combinations that
must simply be learned/memorized rather than freely constructed, so a
learner reasonably groups them together as one undifferentiated
category of "weird fixed phrases," with nothing in casual exposure to
either term marking the crucial transparency-vs-opacity distinction
until directly, explicitly contrasted.

**MC-IF-A-SYNONYM-FITS-THE-DEFINITION-IT-FITS-THE-PHRASE (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — synonym-based
  meaning-matching genuinely works reliably for the large majority of
  vocabulary tasks a learner has encountered (including the entire
  prerequisite synonym/connotation concept), so the learner extends
  this reliable strategy into collocational territory, with nothing in
  that prior success marking that collocations specifically resist
  synonym-logic substitution until a specific counterexample (do a
  decision vs. make a decision) is directly tested.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when tempted to
  substitute a near-synonym into a familiar phrase, spontaneously says
  "wait, is that actually the pairing people use, or just a word that
  means the same thing?" before committing is showing the usage-
  convention discipline has generalized beyond the specific TA-1/TA-2
  practice sets.

**MC-COLLOCATIONS-ARE-JUST-A-FANCY-NAME-FOR-IDIOMS (Blueprint Component
1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: also a Type 1 signature, distinct
  mechanism — the shared "fixed phrase, must be memorized" surface
  feature genuinely applies to BOTH categories, so the learner
  reasonably extends "these are all just weird memorized phrases" into
  treating collocations and idioms as one undifferentiated group, with
  nothing in casual exposure to either term marking the transparency
  distinction until directly, explicitly tested via the transparency
  test.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when meeting a
  fresh fixed phrase, spontaneously applies the transparency test
  ("can I understand this just from the individual words?") before
  classifying it is showing the collocation/idiom distinction has been
  genuinely internalized as a working procedure, not merely recalled
  when directly asked.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the Puzzle-Piece Pairing Board itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically
  connecting left-side word cards to their ONE correctly-fitting
  right-side outcome card, discovering that fit is determined by piece
  SHAPE (usage convention) rather than piece MEANING (synonym logic),
  directly embodies the synonym-substitution fix; the "locked box"
  (raining cats and dogs) versus "clear box" (heavy rain) contrast
  directly embodies the transparency test. Breaking point: a puzzle
  implies exactly one correct fit per piece, which can understate that
  some words genuinely do collocate with multiple different nouns (e.g.
  "make" pairs with "a decision," "a mistake," AND "an effort") — this
  entry treats the puzzle framing as the introductory frame for the
  shape-not-meaning insight specifically, not a claim that each verb has
  only one possible collocational partner.
- **Alternative — a lock and its one matching key, not any similarly-
  shaped key**: a specific lock only opens with its own specific key,
  even if several keys are roughly similar in size and shape (analogous
  to near-synonym verbs); trying a "close enough" key simply doesn't
  work, no matter how reasonable the substitution seems. Breaking
  point: useful for reinforcing that similarity doesn't guarantee fit,
  but should not be extended to suggest collocational correctness is as
  binary and mechanical as a lock-and-key mechanism — some collocational
  judgments involve degrees of naturalness rather than a strict pass/
  fail.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe collocations as "grammar rules" or
  "correctness rules" the way subject-verb agreement is a rule**:
  framing collocations as grammatical rules (which are typically
  logically systematic and rule-derivable) risks reinforcing the
  synonym-substitution misconception by implying collocations, too,
  should be logically derivable from some underlying principle;
  collocations should be framed explicitly as usage CONVENTIONS learned
  through exposure, structurally different from grammar rules in this
  key respect.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
usage-convention nature of collocations and the transparency test are
both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured connotation/denotation
sensitivity, is asked whether "do a decision" sounds right, even though
"do" and "make" both can mean "perform an action"); **playground/
invention** (TA-1's verb+noun matching across several examples lets the
learner notice the fixed-pairing pattern); **collision** (the
Blueprint's own make/do contrast breaks the synonym-substitution
assumption; the heavy-rain/raining-cats-and-dogs contrast breaks the
collocation-idiom conflation); **formalization** (MP-5's generative
collocation-bank task, requiring explicit verification of new
pairings); **compression** (TA-4's fluent off-collocation detection
without each pairing separately re-derived). There is no convention-
bound terminology here requiring separate direct instruction beyond the
labels "collocation" and "idiom" themselves, best introduced once the
learner has already felt the underlying transparency distinction
through the anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (verb+noun collocation exposure) is placed first as the concept's
foundational exposure task, using the most common and memorable
collocation type (make/do/take/have + noun) to establish the fixed-
pairing pattern before any complication is introduced. TA-2 (adjective+
noun collocations) follows, extending the same fixed-pairing insight to
a second grammatical pattern, reinforcing that the phenomenon is
general rather than specific to verb+noun combinations. TA-3
(collocation vs. idiom transparency test) is placed next, directly
targeting MC-COLLOCATIONS-ARE-JUST-A-FANCY-NAME-FOR-IDIOMS — sequenced
after the learner already has secure collocation examples (TA-1/TA-2)
to contrast against idioms. TA-4 (detecting an "off" collocation in
writing) directly targets MC-IF-A-SYNONYM-FITS-THE-DEFINITION-IT-FITS-
THE-PHRASE at its most demanding, applied level — placed after TA-1–3
because detecting a wrong pairing in continuous writing requires the
learner to already have secure exposure to correct pairings to compare
against. TA-5 (building a personal collocation bank) is placed last as
the concept's fully generative capstone, requiring the learner to
research or infer new collocational pairings for unfamiliar words,
modeling genuine, ongoing real-world vocabulary-learning practice. This
entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script
(Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously checks a
  fresh verb/noun or adjective/noun pairing against actual usage
  patterns (rather than assuming a near-synonym substitution will work)
  — this is the primary diagnostic signal for whether the usage-
  convention discipline has genuinely generalized beyond the specific
  practice sets.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before confirming a
  collocation/idiom classification for a borderline-feeling phrase,
  followed by an explicit application of the transparency test ("can I
  get the meaning just from the words?"), is a good sign — it suggests
  the learner is consciously applying the criterion rather than
  guessing. Instant, confident synonym substitution into a fixed
  collocation with no hesitation at all is the concerning pattern for
  MC-IF-A-SYNONYM-FITS-THE-DEFINITION-IT-FITS-THE-PHRASE.
- **Exposure-dependence stakes**: real and worth naming explicitly —
  since collocations are learned through exposure rather than derived
  logically, a learner correctly saying "I'm not sure, I haven't seen
  that pairing before" for a genuinely unfamiliar word combination is a
  legitimate, honest response, not a failure — this should be
  distinguished from confidently guessing an incorrect pairing.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "is that the pairing you've actually
  heard, or just a word that means the same thing?" is this concept's
  load-bearing sentence for MC-IF-A-SYNONYM-FITS-THE-DEFINITION-IT-FITS-
  THE-PHRASE, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh near-synonym substitution is considered, making the
  usage-convention check a routinely-invited response rather than an
  afterthought.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a verb+noun matching task shrinks to the
  Component 3 Puzzle-Piece Pairing Board anchor using the simplest,
  most familiar pairing set available (make/do/take/have), with the
  tutor modeling the matching before the learner attempts a fresh
  pairing independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for MC-IF-A-SYNONYM-
  FITS-THE-DEFINITION-IT-FITS-THE-PHRASE, confidently substituting a
  near-synonym into a fixed collocation (e.g., "do a decision") is more
  likely than "I don't know" — per the Blueprint's own protocol
  routing, re-run TA-1's verb+noun exposure practice with a narrower,
  more repetitive pairing set rather than correcting each substitution
  in isolation. For MC-COLLOCATIONS-ARE-JUST-A-FANCY-NAME-FOR-IDIOMS,
  treating an idiom and a collocation as the same category is more
  likely than confusion — drop back to the Concrete Anchor's
  transparent-vs-locked-box framing before returning to independent
  practice.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously says "I've definitely heard that pairing before" or "I
  don't think I've ever heard that combination" when evaluating a
  collocation, grounding the judgment in actual exposure rather than
  logical derivation, is showing the usage-convention model has been
  genuinely internalized — this should be named and praised explicitly.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedure — a usage-convention verification routine
(check actual habitual pairing, don't derive from synonym logic; apply
the transparency test to distinguish collocations from idioms) applied
to genuinely novel word combinations, combined with a generative
collocation-bank-building skill (TA-5); mastery is demonstrated by
correctly rejecting synonym substitutions (MP-1), distinguishing
collocations from idioms (MP-2), and generating new correct pairings
(MP-5) rather than by matching a fixed, previously-taught example list.
Interleaving partners: mix familiar-pairing-matching items (testing
TA-1/TA-2 alone) with synonym-substitution-rejection and collocation/
idiom-distinction items (testing TA-3/TA-4 together) in ongoing review,
so the learner continues exercising both the simpler recognition skill
and the harder usage-convention discipline as distinct, separately-
verified competencies.

- **Near**: matching fresh verb+noun/adjective+noun collocations,
  sorting collocation/idiom pairs, detecting and correcting off
  collocations, and generating new pairings for unfamiliar words.
- **Far**: this concept's usage-convention discipline and collocation/
  idiom distinction are the direct foundation for `eng.vocab.idioms`'s
  deeper treatment of non-transparent expressions, per the Blueprint's
  own explicit gating rule, and for `eng.vocab.phrasal-verbs`'s own
  fixed, non-logically-derivable particle pairings, per the Blueprint's
  own explicit Adaptive Flag naming this direct reuse.
- **Real-world**: every piece of writing or speech the learner produces
  offers immediate, ungated opportunities to apply correct collocational
  choices rather than synonym-substitution guessing, and every
  unfamiliar word encountered offers an opportunity to build a personal
  collocation bank.
- **Expert-transfer**: genuinely native-like, fluent English production
  at any advanced level depends substantially on collocational accuracy
  becoming largely automatic, since collocational errors (even with
  perfect grammar and vocabulary) are one of the most persistent markers
  distinguishing highly proficient non-native speakers from native
  speakers.
