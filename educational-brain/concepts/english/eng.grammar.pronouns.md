# Pronouns — `eng.grammar.pronouns`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.pronouns` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Pronouns" — words that
  substitute for nouns, including personal, possessive, reflexive,
  relative, and indefinite pronouns.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.nouns` — load-bearing because
  pronouns are, by definition, substitution devices for nouns; a
  learner who cannot yet reliably identify and classify nouns
  (concrete, abstract, common, proper, countable, uncountable) has no
  stable set of substitution targets to work from.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.pronoun-antecedent-agreement`.
  Correctly matching a pronoun to its antecedent's number, gender, and
  category presupposes the learner already reliably substitutes
  pronouns for the full range of noun types this concept installs.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.80 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Replace repeated person-nouns with personal pronouns (he, she, they,
   I, you, we).
2. Replace repeated non-human nouns (things, places, ideas) with "it,"
   "they," "this," "that."
3. Replace possessive nouns with possessive pronouns (her book, mine,
   yours, theirs).
4. Correctly identify and use reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself,
   himself) only when the subject and object of an action are the same
   entity, or for direct emphasis — not as a general substitute for
   object pronouns.
5. Identify relative pronouns (who, which, that) and indefinite
   pronouns (someone, anything, everybody) in sentences.

## Core Understanding

Pronouns substitute for ANY noun — people (he, she, they), things (it),
places, ideas, or groups (they, these, those) — not just people. "It"
is one of the most frequently used pronouns and almost always refers to
non-human things, places, or ideas: in "I bought a book. IT was
expensive," "it" replaces "book," not a person. Checking what noun a
pronoun is actually replacing — which could be a person, but just as
often a thing, place, or idea — is the reliable test, rather than
defaulting to a people-only expectation. A second, equally load-bearing
fact is that reflexive pronouns (myself, yourself, himself, herself,
itself, ourselves, themselves) are correct specifically when the
subject performing an action and the object receiving that action are
the SAME entity ("I hurt myself") or for emphasis directly after the
noun/pronoun they intensify ("I myself saw it") — they are NOT simply
more formal or emphatic substitutes for regular object pronouns (me,
you, him, her) in general. "Give the book to me" is correct; "Give the
book to myself" is not, unless "myself" is the one giving it to
themselves. Using a reflexive pronoun only when the subject and object
refer to the same entity, or for direct emphasis, is the reliable rule.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "pronouns replace people's names, like he, she,
  and they"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept
  exists to correct — a natural default since person-referring pronouns
  (he, she, they) are the most common early examples taught, giving no
  built-in signal that non-human pronouns like "it" are just as central
  until directly tested.
- **Intermediate model — "I can replace person-nouns and non-human
  nouns with the correct pronoun"**: installed by TA-1's personal-
  pronoun practice and TA-2's non-human-pronoun practice, using the
  Component 3 pronoun-swap-game anchor to build the substitution habit
  across categories, resolving MC-PRONOUNS-ONLY-REPLACE-PEOPLE. Upgrade
  trigger: correctly substituting "it" for a fresh non-human noun
  without hesitation.
- **Advanced model — "I can replace possessive nouns with possessive
  pronouns"**: installed by TA-3's practice, extending the substitution
  framework to possession.
- **Expert model — "I use reflexive pronouns only when the subject and
  object are the same entity or for direct emphasis, and I recognize
  relative and indefinite pronouns"**: installed by TA-4's reflexive-
  pronoun same-entity-rule practice (resolving MC-REFLEXIVE-PRONOUNS-
  ARE-JUST-EMPHASIS) and TA-5's relative/indefinite-pronoun
  identification. This is the concept's genuine mastery bar — moving
  from basic substitution to precise, rule-governed reflexive usage.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 learners whose L1 has a different pronoun system (no grammatical
  gender distinction, a different formality-based pronoun system, or no
  separate reflexive-pronoun category), specific, predictable transfer
  patterns should be anticipated and addressed explicitly and
  patiently — gender confusion between he/she for L1s without gendered
  pronouns, or reflexive-pronoun overuse for L1s using reflexives more
  broadly as a politeness marker — rather than treating all pronoun
  errors as generically difficult.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of how pronouns are typically first encountered. The
people-only failure exists because person-referring pronouns (he, she,
they) genuinely ARE the most common, most vivid early examples a
learner meets, so the learner reasonably extends "pronouns replace
people" from this consistently reinforced pattern, with nothing
marking the equally central non-human-pronoun exception until directly
tested against a sentence like "I bought a book. IT was expensive."
The reflexive-overuse failure exists because reflexive pronouns
genuinely DO sound more formal or emphatic in casual perception (they
are longer, more distinctive words than simple object pronouns), so a
learner reasonably reaches for them in contexts where extra formality
or politeness seems called for, with nothing in that surface
impression of formality marking the specific, narrow grammatical rule
(same subject/object entity) governing their actual correct use — this
is a very common, persistent error even among educated native writers
in formal registers like business emails.

## Misconceptions

**MC-PRONOUNS-ONLY-REPLACE-PEOPLE (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — person-
  referring pronouns genuinely ARE the most common and vivid early
  examples a learner meets, so the learner extends "pronouns = people"
  from this consistently reinforced pattern, with nothing in that early
  exposure marking the non-human-pronoun exception until directly
  tested.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, upon meeting a
  fresh non-human antecedent, spontaneously reaches for "it" or "they"
  without hesitation is showing the noun-category-inclusive model has
  been genuinely internalized.

**MC-REFLEXIVE-PRONOUNS-ARE-JUST-EMPHASIS (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 5, instruction-induced**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a Type 5 signature — reflexive-pronoun
  overuse is substantially reinforced by informal social/register
  conventions the learner has absorbed (hearing others use "myself" in
  emails and formal-sounding requests as if it were more proper or
  polite), so the learner has typically received this pattern as
  modeled, expected behavior from authoritative or aspirational
  register examples, rather than independently inventing the belief.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when tempted to
  write "to myself" in a formal request, spontaneously checks "am I
  both the giver and the receiver here?" before committing is showing
  the same-entity-rule discipline has generalized beyond the specific
  TA-4 practice sentences.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the pronoun swap game itself is the analogy, not a
  supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): rewriting an awkwardly
  repetitive sentence ("Maria went to Maria's car and Maria drove
  Maria's car home") using pronouns across different noun categories
  (person, thing, place, group) directly embodies pronoun substitution
  as a general, cross-category tool rather than a people-only device.
  Breaking point: a single repetitive sentence rewrite can make
  pronoun substitution feel like a stylistic nicety (avoiding
  clunkiness) rather than a grammatically necessary and constrained
  system (with rules like reflexive same-entity constraints) — this
  entry treats the swap game as the introductory frame for basic
  substitution across categories, with TA-4 explicitly extending
  beyond mere style into rule-governed correctness.
- **Alternative — a mirror reflecting back at the same person, not a
  photo of someone else**: a reflexive pronoun is like a mirror —
  it reflects the action back onto the SAME person who performed it
  ("I hurt myself"); using it to refer to someone else would be like
  expecting a mirror to show a different person's reflection. Breaking
  point: useful for the reflexive-pronoun same-entity insight
  specifically, but doesn't address the people-only misconception the
  swap game's multi-category structure covers.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe reflexive pronouns as "the more
  polite/formal version" of object pronouns**: framing reflexives as a
  formality upgrade directly reinforces MC-REFLEXIVE-PRONOUNS-ARE-
  JUST-EMPHASIS; reflexive pronouns should always be presented as
  governed by a specific structural rule (same subject/object entity,
  or direct emphasis), never as a general politeness or formality
  option available whenever a request feels like it needs upgrading.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
noun-category-inclusive principle and the reflexive same-entity rule
are both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided
discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured nouns, is asked what
"it" replaces in "I bought a book. IT was expensive"); **playground/
invention** (TA-1's personal-pronoun practice builds initial confidence
with basic substitution); **collision** (the Blueprint's own "it"
probe breaks the people-only assumption; the "Give the book to myself"
probe breaks the reflexive-as-emphasis assumption); **formalization**
(MP-5's own-words statement of both the noun-category-inclusive
principle and the reflexive same-entity rule); **compression** (TA-5's
fluent relative/indefinite-pronoun identification without each
category separately re-derived). The specific terms "personal,"
"possessive," "reflexive," "relative," and "indefinite" are
convention-bound grammatical terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying
functional distinctions through the anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (personal pronouns for people) is placed first as the concept's
most intuitive entry point, per the Blueprint's own chunked-
introduction approach. TA-2 (pronouns for things, places, and ideas)
follows immediately, directly targeting MC-PRONOUNS-ONLY-REPLACE-
PEOPLE — per the Blueprint's own session architecture, this conflict-
evidence probe is placed right after TA-2, once the learner has secure
personal-pronoun practice to contrast against. TA-3 (possessive
pronouns) extends the substitution framework to a third, distinct
grammatical function (possession). TA-4 (reflexive pronouns) is placed
next, directly targeting MC-REFLEXIVE-PRONOUNS-ARE-JUST-EMPHASIS,
addressing this fourth category's more demanding, rule-governed
correctness requirement once the learner already has secure basic
substitution across the prior three categories. TA-5 (relative and
indefinite pronouns) is placed last, completing the pronoun-category
framework with two further specialized types (introducing descriptive
clauses; referring to unspecified nouns). This entry does not restate
the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see
Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously substitutes
  "it" or "they" for a fresh non-human antecedent (rather than
  defaulting to a personal pronoun or avoiding substitution
  altogether), and spontaneously checks the same-entity rule before
  using a reflexive pronoun — these are the primary diagnostic signals
  for whether TA-2 and TA-4's disciplines have genuinely generalized
  beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before using a
  reflexive pronoun in a formal-sounding request, followed by a
  correct self-check ("wait, am I both giving and receiving here?"),
  suggests the learner is consciously applying the same-entity rule
  rather than reaching for a "more proper-sounding" form — a good sign.
  Instant, confident use of "myself" in a context where "me" is
  correct is the concerning pattern for MC-REFLEXIVE-PRONOUNS-ARE-
  JUST-EMPHASIS.
- **Register-driven-error stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags — reflexive-pronoun overuse in formal
  contexts is a very common, persistent error even among educated
  native writers, warranting dedicated, explicit TA-4 practice rather
  than treating it as a quickly-resolved issue.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "who's doing the action, and who's
  receiving it — are they the same person?" is this concept's load-
  bearing sentence for MC-REFLEXIVE-PRONOUNS-ARE-JUST-EMPHASIS, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh reflexive-pronoun candidate arises, making the
  same-entity check a routinely-invited response rather than a
  formality-driven default.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework combined with procedure — a
multi-category pronoun-substitution system (personal/non-human/
possessive/relative/indefinite) combined with a specific reflexive-
pronoun correctness rule (same-entity check), applied to genuinely
novel sentences; mastery is demonstrated by correctly substituting
across all noun categories and correctly applying the reflexive rule to
unfamiliar sentences (MP-2/MP-4) rather than by recognizing a fixed,
previously-taught example list. Interleaving partners: mix category-
substitution items (testing TA-1/TA-2/TA-3/TA-5) with reflexive-
correctness items (testing TA-4) in ongoing review, so the learner
continues exercising both the broad substitution skill and the
narrower, rule-governed reflexive discipline as distinct, separately-
verified competencies.
