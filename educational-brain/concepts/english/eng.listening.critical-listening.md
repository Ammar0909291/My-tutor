## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Evaluate a spoken argument's strength independently of the speaker's delivery confidence or fluency.
2. Rank cited evidence by source credibility.
3. Explain why credible-but-tangential evidence does not fully support a specific claim.
4. Identify what evidence is missing despite a confident, polished delivery.
5. Fully critically evaluate a novel spoken argument's overall strength, delivery independence, evidence credibility, and evidence relevance.

## Core Understanding

This concept extends `eng.listening.listening-comprehension-strategies`' accurate content-tracking into evaluative judgment about a spoken argument's quality. Two corrections define it. First, **a confident, fluent speaker is not necessarily making a stronger argument** — delivery polish and argument strength are two different things that don't always travel together; a confidently-delivered argument resting on one unsupported anecdote is weaker than a hesitant argument citing a specific, named study, even though the confident delivery FEELS more convincing. Second, **not all evidence a speaker cites proves their point equally well** — a single anecdote ("my neighbor told me...") is far weaker evidence than a documented, credible, relevant source, even though both are technically "evidence" that was mentioned; a critical listener must evaluate a cited source's credibility and its actual relevance to the specific claim, not merely register that something was cited. Both corrections apply the evaluative discipline this program has established for reading-domain concepts (`eng.reading.critical-reading`, `eng.reading.evaluating-sources`) to the additional, real-time challenge of spoken delivery.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "a confident, fluent speaker is making a stronger argument; any evidence a speaker mentions proves their point."**
The simulation: rate argument quality by how convincingly it's delivered, and accept any cited evidence as equally strong support.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a confidently-delivered anecdote-based argument versus a hesitant, study-cited one, asked which sounds more convincing versus which is actually better supported; and a neighbor's anecdote versus a peer-reviewed study, asked whether both are equally strong evidence.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Evaluate the evidence and reasoning, not the delivery. Not all cited evidence is equally strong."

**Intermediate — "I separate delivery from strength now, but I still tend to accept credible-sounding evidence without checking whether it actually addresses this specific claim."**
The simulation: correctly resists being swayed by confident delivery but hasn't yet reliably checked cited evidence's relevance to the specific point being argued.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct practice with a claim supported by credible-but-only-tangentially-relevant evidence.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Credible isn't the same as relevant. Does this evidence actually support THIS specific claim?"

**Advanced — "I evaluate argument strength independent of delivery, assess evidence credibility, and check evidence relevance together on a novel spoken argument."**
The simulation: integrates delivery-independence, credibility-ranking, and relevance-checking into one coherent real-time evaluation. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: the cross-linked `eng.speaking.debate-skills`, which requires this same evidence-evaluation discipline from the producing side of an argument, not just the listening side.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the listener's evaluative discipline. Debate skills require applying the same standard when you're the one making the argument."

**Expert — "at fluency, delivery-independent evaluation and evidence-credibility/relevance checking happen automatically while listening to any spoken argument."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit separate-and-check routine is a scaffold for a listening habit that, at true fluency, is inseparable from informed, critical listening generally.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **fluency and confidence are genuinely correlated with competence in many everyday contexts** (a confident teacher, a fluent expert), so extending that correlation to argument quality specifically is a reasonable, usually-successful heuristic that fails precisely when someone is confidently wrong or a hesitant speaker is correct but nervous.

The second mechanism is that **the mere act of citing something (a name, a source, a statistic) satisfies a listener's expectation that "evidence was provided," without the listener having the time or inclination, in real-time spoken discourse, to interrogate that evidence's actual quality** — the same mechanism identified for citation-presence trust in the reading domain, intensified here by the lack of a pause-and-reread option available in listening.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-A-CONFIDENT-FLUENT-SPEAKER-IS-MAKING-A-STRONGER-ARGUMENT
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-A — includes the confident-anecdote/hesitant-study discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from confidence and fluency functioning as a fast, generally-reliable competence signal in everyday social judgment, mistakenly extended to argument-quality evaluation specifically.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **have the learner list the actual evidence in each argument before rating convincingness**, since the fix requires a concrete procedural step (evidence-first, delivery-blind evaluation), not merely an abstract warning against being swayed by confidence.
- **Verification of death**: given a confidently-delivered weak argument and a hesitantly-delivered strong one, the learner identifies which is actually better supported, independent of delivery style.

### MC-B-ANY-EVIDENCE-A-SPEAKER-MENTIONS-PROVES-THEIR-POINT-EQUALLY-WELL
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — includes the neighbor-anecdote/peer-reviewed-study discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the reasonable practice of noting that evidence was cited at all, extended into treating that citation's mere presence as proof of adequate support, regardless of the source's actual credibility.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair supplies a **starter credibility hierarchy** (documented study > named expert testimony > single anecdote), since the fix requires a concrete evaluative scaffold, not just an abstract instruction to "check credibility."
- **Verification of death**: given three pieces of cited evidence of varying quality, the learner ranks them by credibility.

## Analogies

**Best — the Shiny-Paint-Bridge and Notarized-Document-vs-Handwritten-Note Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a bridge judged safe by its paint job rather than its structure directly seeds the delivery-independence fix; a handwritten note treated as equally valid as a notarized document directly seeds the evidence-credibility fix.
*Breaking point*: a bridge's structural integrity is an objective engineering fact independently verifiable by inspection, while an argument's actual strength requires interpretive judgment about reasoning and evidence — useful for the appearance-versus-substance intuition, less precise for implying argument strength is as objectively measurable as structural load.

**Alternative — a salesperson's pitch versus a product's actual specifications.** A slick, confident sales pitch says nothing about whether the product's actual specifications meet the buyer's needs — the buyer must check the specs (the evidence) independently of how persuasively they were presented.
*Breaking point*: product specifications are typically independently verifiable facts, while an argument's "specifications" (its reasoning and evidence) require the listener's own critical evaluation rather than simple factual lookup.

**Visual anchor — the Shiny-Paint-Bridge and Notarized-Document-vs-Handwritten-Note Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"A speaker who sounds confident and fluent usually knows what they're talking about."** Directly installs MC-A as a stated heuristic.
- **"If a speaker cites some kind of evidence, their point is proven."** Directly installs MC-B as a stated rule.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Shiny-Paint-Bridge and Notarized-Document-vs-Handwritten-Note Anchor (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Does a shiny paint job tell you the bridge is structurally sound?" Then connect to delivery-versus-argument-strength.
2. **The confident-anecdote/hesitant-study collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-A. *Predict first*: "Which speaker sounds more convincing? Which argument is actually better supported?"
3. **The neighbor-anecdote/peer-reviewed-study collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-B. *Predict first*: "Are both of these equally strong evidence for their claims?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both evaluative facts are directly checkable by comparing paired spoken arguments for delivery versus substance.

1. **Need**: "If someone sounds very confident while speaking, does that tell you their argument is actually correct?"
2. **Playground**: the learner ranks several pieces of cited evidence by credibility freely before being told the official hierarchy.
3. **Invention**: "Can you list the actual evidence in this argument, separate from how confidently it was delivered?"
4. **Collision**: present a neighbor's anecdote and a peer-reviewed study as evidence for similar claims; ask whether both deserve equal trust.
5. **Formalization**: name the two evaluative facts — delivery confidence doesn't predict argument strength; not all cited evidence is equally credible — from the learner's own comparisons.
6. **Compression**: "Evaluate the evidence, not the delivery — and check the evidence's credibility and relevance, not just its presence."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Separating delivery confidence from argument strength (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting MC-A before evidence-evaluation content is introduced, since a learner still swayed by confident delivery will misjudge even well-evaluated evidence within a persuasively-delivered package.
- **Evaluating source credibility (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting MC-B with the concrete credibility hierarchy.
- **Evaluating relevance of evidence to the specific claim (TA-3) comes third**, extending TA-2's credibility check to the additional relevance dimension.
- **Full application (TA-4) comes last**, integrating delivery-independence, credibility, and relevance into one synthesis task — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

Turn-by-turn scripting, protocol routing, and adaptive flags: Blueprint **Component 7 — Session Architecture** and **Component 8 — Adaptive Flags** (the MP-1/MP-4 residual-MC-A routing, MP-2/MP-3 residual-MC-B routing, the mandatory "list the actual evidence first" fallback, the starter credibility hierarchy scaffold, and the explicit transfer parallel to `eng.speaking.debate-skills`).

## Voice Teaching Notes

This concept is **inherently a listening-and-evaluation skill** — its core evidence is the learner's real-time evaluative reasoning about a spoken argument, not written analysis of a transcript alone, since the delivery-independence check specifically requires setting aside the AUDITORY experience of confidence.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated "let me set aside how confident that sounded" move** before evaluating an argument — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A's repair.
- **A stated credibility ranking with reasoning** ("this is a documented study, that's just one person's story") applied to newly-heard evidence — direct evidence MC-B's fix is internalized.
- **A credibility verdict swayed purely by how smoothly or confidently a claim was delivered**, with no evidence-based reasoning — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Evaluate the evidence, not the delivery — and check that the evidence actually proves this specific point."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But that speaker sounded so sure of themselves"** — take this as a genuine, reasonable social read of confidence; respond with the procedural fix: "Confidence is like paint on a bridge — let's check the structure instead. What evidence did they actually give?"
- **"They cited something, so isn't their point proven?"** — a reasonable-sounding shortcut; respond with the credibility hierarchy: "A documented study is much stronger than one person's story, even though both are technically 'evidence.' Which kind was this?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full novel-argument evaluation down to **ranking a single pair of evidence pieces (one anecdote, one documented source) with no delivery-independence or full-argument task demanded.** Isolates the most basic credibility distinction before returning to fuller evaluation.
- **Never shrink to "just say if the speaker sounded convincing."** This directly re-licenses the confidence-as-strength misconception this concept specifically works to dismantle.
