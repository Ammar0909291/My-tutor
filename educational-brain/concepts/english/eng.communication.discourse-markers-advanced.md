## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Select a discourse marker that accurately matches the actual logical relationship between ideas (contrast, cause, addition), rather than treating markers as interchangeable.
2. Use discourse markers naturally and only where they genuinely fit, rather than inserting sophisticated-sounding markers regardless of fit.
3. Identify and correct a mismatched discourse marker in a given sentence pair.
4. Apply accurate, naturally-fitting discourse markers across an extended multi-paragraph passage.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **discourse markers are not interchangeable transition words** — each marker signals a SPECIFIC logical relationship (contrast, cause, addition), and using the wrong one (e.g., "however" where the relationship is actually additive) misleads the reader about how the ideas actually connect, rather than merely varying the style. Second, **a more elaborate, academic-sounding discourse marker is not automatically better than a simpler one** — a marker's job is to make the logical connection clear, and an elaborate marker that doesn't fit the sentence's natural register and flow draws attention to itself instead of clarifying the relationship, sacrificing clarity for the mere appearance of sophistication.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "discourse markers are interchangeable transition words, and more elaborate-sounding ones are always more impressive and better."**
The simulation: insert "however" or "therefore" wherever a transition feels needed regardless of the actual relationship, and reflexively swap simple connectors for elaborate ones to sound more sophisticated.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a sentence pair where "however" falsely signals a contrast that isn't there, and a paragraph where every connector is replaced with an elaborate alternative regardless of fit.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A stop sign and a yield sign mean different things — pick the marker that matches the real relationship. And a tuxedo at a backyard barbecue looks out of place, not impressive."

**Intermediate — "I can now identify the actual logical relationship and match a marker to it, but I still over-elaborate in my own original writing."**
The simulation: correctly identifies and corrects mismatched markers in provided sentence pairs but reflexively reaches for elaborate markers when writing new content.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct revision practice, taking an over-elaborated paragraph and replacing each marker with the simplest word that still accurately signals the relationship.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Read it aloud — does this marker sound natural here, or forced? Clarity beats the appearance of sophistication."

**Advanced — "I identify each relationship and select naturally-fitting, accurately-matching discourse markers throughout an extended passage."**
The simulation: integrates relationship-identification, accurate matching, and natural-fit discipline into one coherent, extended piece of writing. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: none within the current curriculum sequence — this concept is confirmed terminal.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the full discourse-marker toolkit for extended discourse."

**Expert — "at fluency, selecting accurate, naturally-fitting discourse markers happens as an integrated part of ordinary writing, without a separate deliberate relationship-check."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit identify-match-verify routine is a scaffold for a writing habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of composition.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **discourse markers are frequently taught as a list of "transition words" grouped loosely by general function** (e.g., a single "transition words" list mixing contrast, cause, and addition together), reinforcing an interchangeable-connector model rather than a precise, relationship-specific selection discipline.

The second mechanism is that **academic writing instruction often rewards more formal, elaborate vocabulary generally**, which learners overgeneralize specifically to discourse markers, assuming "sounds more academic" straightforwardly means "better," without checking whether the elaborate marker actually fits the sentence's natural register and flow.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-DISCOURSE-MARKERS-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE-CONNECTORS-REGARDLESS-OF-THE-ACTUAL-LOGICAL-RELATIONSHIP
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of "transition words" being taught as one loosely-grouped category rather than relationship-specific tools, extended into treating any marker as interchangeable with any other.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **require an explicit relationship-identification step before marker selection**, since the interchangeability habit is sustained by never having to name the specific relationship first.
- **Verification of death**: given a sentence pair with a mismatched marker, the learner identifies and corrects the mismatch based on the actual relationship.

### MC-B-MORE-SOPHISTICATED-SOUNDING-DISCOURSE-MARKERS-ARE-ALWAYS-BETTER-REGARDLESS-OF-NATURAL-FIT
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of "more formal vocabulary is rewarded in academic writing" generally, extended specifically to discourse markers regardless of natural fit.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **a read-aloud naturalness check**, since the forced, unnatural quality of an over-elaborated passage is far more convincing demonstrated audibly than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given an over-elaborated paragraph, the learner replaces each marker with the simplest word that still accurately signals the relationship.

## Analogies

**Best — the Stop-Sign-vs-Yield-Sign and Tuxedo-at-a-Backyard-Barbecue Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a stop sign and a yield sign used interchangeably despite signaling genuinely different instructions — directly seeding the fix for MC-A. Wearing a tuxedo to a casual backyard barbecue, drawing attention to itself rather than fitting the context — directly seeding the fix for MC-B.
*Breaking point*: traffic signs have a single, universally fixed correct meaning with no register variation, while discourse markers' "correct" choice can genuinely vary by writing register (formal academic prose versus casual blog writing), a flexibility traffic signs don't have.

**Alternative — choosing a tool from a toolbox for a specific job.** A screwdriver and a hammer aren't interchangeable despite both being "tools"; picking the wrong one for the job doesn't just look odd, it actually fails to do the job — just as the wrong discourse marker fails to signal the actual relationship.
*Breaking point*: a screwdriver and hammer's correct uses are almost never ambiguous or context-dependent, while discourse marker choice sometimes involves closer, more genuinely debatable calls between two similarly-fitting options.

**Visual anchor — the Stop-Sign-vs-Yield-Sign and Tuxedo-at-a-Backyard-Barbecue Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Any transition word works as long as it connects two sentences."** Directly installs the interchangeability misconception as a stated definition.
- **"Always use the fanciest-sounding connector you know."** Directly installs the elaborate-is-better misconception as a stated definition.

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by testing candidate markers against the actual relationship and against a natural-fit read-aloud check.

1. **Need**: "If I use 'however' between two ideas that actually agree with each other, does that confuse the reader?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely identifies relationships in five sentence pairs before being told the official marker-matching test.
3. **Invention**: "Take a simple connector like 'but' and try replacing it with an elaborate one — does it still sound natural here?"
4. **Collision**: present an over-elaborated paragraph and ask whether it communicates more clearly than a simpler version.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — markers must match the actual relationship, and simplicity often beats elaboration for clarity — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Match the marker to the real relationship; choose clarity over sounding sophisticated."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Identifying the actual logical relationship (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the first misconception's root cause before marker-matching itself is practiced.
- **Matching the marker to the relationship (TA-2) comes second**, extending TA-1 into active correction practice on mismatched examples.
- **Choosing natural fit over elaborate sound (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting the second misconception once accurate matching is established.
- **Full application across an extended passage (TA-4) comes last**, the generative capstone integrating both misconception fixes — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for a marker choice** — whether they can name the actual relationship and separately judge natural fit — since a correctly-chosen marker with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated relationship identification** ("this is actually a cause, not a contrast, so I need a different marker") applied to a new sentence pair — the direct behavioral signature of the accurate-matching fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary simplification of an over-elaborated marker** after a self-initiated naturalness check — direct evidence the clarity-over-elaboration fix is internalized.
- **Reflexive substitution of an elaborate marker with no relationship check** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A or MC-B still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Match the marker to the real relationship, and choose clarity over sounding sophisticated."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But 'however' is a transition word, so it should work here"** — take this as an accurate observation about its general category, not a mistake; respond by adding the stricter test: "It is a transition word, that's true — but does it signal contrast specifically? Is this relationship actually a contrast, or something else?"
- **"I used 'notwithstanding' because it sounds more advanced"** — a genuine, reasonable instinct given how formal vocabulary is often rewarded; respond by naming the additional layer: "It does sound advanced, yes — but read it aloud. Does it fit naturally here, or does it feel forced?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full extended-passage application down to **identifying the actual relationship in a single sentence pair, with marker selection and naturalness checking set aside.** Isolates the most basic relationship-identification skill before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just pick whichever transition word sounds good."** This directly licenses the interchangeability pattern, undermining the accurate-matching discipline this concept specifically builds.
