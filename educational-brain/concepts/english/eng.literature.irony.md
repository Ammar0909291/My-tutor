## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Distinguish situational irony (an outcome contrary to reasonable expectation, revealing something meaningful) from mere bad luck or coincidence.
2. Distinguish verbal irony (saying something while meaning the opposite, for a rhetorical purpose) from ordinary sarcasm's tone alone.
3. Identify dramatic irony (the audience knows something a character doesn't) in a passage.
4. Classify a new example correctly among the three irony types and explain why it fits.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **situational irony is not simply "something bad happening" or coincidence** — genuine situational irony requires an outcome that contradicts a REASONABLE expectation in a way that reveals something meaningful (a fire station burning down), not merely an unfortunate or surprising event with no such pointed contradiction. Second, **verbal irony is not identical to sarcasm** — verbal irony is the broader device of saying something while meaning its opposite for a specific rhetorical effect, while sarcasm is one particular tone or register of verbal irony (typically mocking or cutting); treating all verbal irony as sarcastic tone flattens genuinely different rhetorical effects (gentle, wistful verbal irony versus biting sarcasm) into one category.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "any bad luck is situational irony, and any verbal irony is just sarcasm."**
The simulation: label any unfortunate coincidence as "ironic" regardless of whether it contradicts a reasonable expectation meaningfully, and treat all opposite-meaning statements as sarcastic in tone.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a genuine bad-luck example with no pointed contradiction, tested against a true situational-irony example; and a gentle, non-mocking verbal-irony example tested against a genuinely sarcastic one.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Not all bad luck is ironic — and not all verbal irony is sarcastic in tone."

**Intermediate — "I can now distinguish genuine situational irony from bad luck and verbal irony from mere sarcasm, but I still confuse dramatic irony with the other two."**
The simulation: correctly applies the situational and verbal distinctions but hasn't yet isolated dramatic irony's audience-knowledge structure.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct dramatic-irony identification practice, given a passage where the audience knows something a character doesn't.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Dramatic irony isn't about outcome or tone — it's about who knows what. Check what the audience knows that the character doesn't."

**Advanced — "I classify a new example correctly among all three irony types and explain why it fits, distinguishing each from its near-miss."**
The simulation: integrates all three irony-type distinctions into one coherent classification judgment. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: none within the current curriculum sequence — this concept is confirmed terminal.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the three irony types clearly separated. Applying this classification skill to new texts is the ongoing practice ahead."

**Expert — "at fluency, irony-type classification happens as an integrated part of ordinary literary reading, without a separate deliberate three-way checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit classification routine is a scaffold for a reading habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of literary comprehension.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **"ironic" is used loosely in casual speech to mean any unfortunate or unexpected coincidence**, reinforcing an inflated, imprecise usage that the stricter literary definition (a meaningful contradiction of reasonable expectation) must actively correct.

The second mechanism is that **sarcasm is the most common, salient real-world experience of verbal irony**, so learners generalize from that one frequent instance to assume verbal irony IS sarcasm, rather than recognizing sarcasm as one tone among several the broader device can take.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-ANY-UNFORTUNATE-COINCIDENCE-OR-BAD-LUCK-COUNTS-AS-SITUATIONAL-IRONY
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 3 (language contamination), from casual everyday usage of "ironic" to describe any unfortunate coincidence, contaminating the stricter literary definition requiring a meaningful contradiction of reasonable expectation.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **add the reasonable-expectation-contradiction test as a further filter, not discard the notion of unexpectedness entirely** — genuine situational irony IS unexpected, but not everything unexpected is ironic.
- **Verification of death**: given a bad-luck example and a true situational-irony example, the learner correctly identifies which one contradicts a reasonable expectation meaningfully.

### MC-B-VERBAL-IRONY-IS-THE-SAME-THING-AS-SARCASM
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of sarcasm as the most familiar real-world instance of verbal irony, extended into treating the specific tone as the entire device.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct side-by-side comparison of a gentle, non-mocking verbal-irony example against a biting sarcastic one**, since the tone difference is far more convincing demonstrated than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given a gentle, non-sarcastic verbal-irony example, the learner correctly identifies it as verbal irony without requiring a mocking tone.

## Analogies

**Best — Thunder-Specificity and Boxing-Is-Exercise** (Blueprint Component 3): thunder is a specific, identifiable sound distinct from all loud noise generally — directly seeding the fix for MC-A (situational irony is a specific, identifiable pattern, not any unfortunate event generally). Boxing is a specific instance of the broader category "exercise," not identical to it — directly seeding the fix for MC-B (sarcasm is one specific tone of the broader verbal-irony device, not identical to it).
*Breaking point*: thunder and loud noise, or boxing and exercise, are natural-world or activity categories with fairly clean boundaries, while irony's boundary cases (a borderline coincidence, a mildly cutting remark) require more interpretive judgment than these anchors' cleaner category distinctions suggest.

**Alternative — a specific breed of dog versus "dog" in general.** A poodle is a specific instance of the broader category "dog," just as sarcasm is a specific instance of the broader category "verbal irony" — recognizing the general category doesn't require every instance to share the specific breed's traits.
*Breaking point*: dog breeds are a settled biological/taxonomic categorization, while irony's sub-types involve more genuinely contestable edge cases in literary interpretation.

**Visual anchor — Thunder-Specificity and Boxing-Is-Exercise**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Ironic just means something unlucky or unexpected happened."** Directly installs the bad-luck misconception as a stated definition.
- **"Verbal irony means being sarcastic."** Directly installs the sarcasm-equivalence misconception as a stated definition.

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since all three irony-type structural facts are directly checkable by testing candidate examples against each type's specific criteria.

1. **Need**: "If you spill coffee on yourself, is that 'ironic,' or just bad luck?"
2. **Playground**: the learner sorts several unexpected-event examples into situational-irony versus mere-bad-luck freely before being told the official test.
3. **Invention**: "Can you think of a verbal-irony example that isn't mocking or sarcastic at all?"
4. **Collision**: present a passage where the audience knows something a character doesn't, and ask whether that's the same as situational or verbal irony.
5. **Formalization**: name the three structural facts — situational irony requires meaningful contradiction of reasonable expectation, verbal irony isn't limited to sarcastic tone, dramatic irony is about audience-versus-character knowledge — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Situational: expectation contradicted meaningfully. Verbal: opposite meant, any tone. Dramatic: audience knows more than the character."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Situational irony's reasonable-expectation test (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the first misconception before the other two types are introduced, since the bad-luck confusion is the most common and needs early, dedicated correction.
- **Verbal irony beyond sarcasm (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting the second misconception once situational irony is established as a distinct type.
- **Dramatic irony's audience-knowledge structure (TA-3) comes third**, introducing the remaining, less misconception-laden type once the first two are secure.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for classifying an example among the three irony types**, since a correct-looking classification with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated reasonable-expectation-contradiction check** ("does this actually contradict what we'd expect, in a meaningful way?") applied to a new example — the direct behavioral signature of the situational-irony fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary identification of non-sarcastic verbal irony** ("this says the opposite, but it isn't mocking — it's still verbal irony") — direct evidence the sarcasm-equivalence fix is internalized.
- **Labeling any unfortunate coincidence as "ironic" with no contradiction check** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Situational irony needs a real contradiction; verbal irony isn't always sarcastic; dramatic irony is about who knows what."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But that's such bad luck, isn't that ironic?"** — take this as an accurate observation about genuine misfortune, not a mistake; respond by adding the stricter test: "It's definitely bad luck, that's true — but does it contradict what we'd reasonably expect, in a meaningful way? If not, it's just bad luck, not situational irony."
- **"I thought verbal irony meant being sarcastic"** — a genuine, reasonable expectation given how common sarcasm is as an example; respond by naming the additional layer: "Sarcasm is one way to do verbal irony, yes — but you can say the opposite of what you mean gently too. Is this mocking, or just gently opposite?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full three-way classification down to **sorting a single list of 4 examples into situational-irony versus bad-luck only, with verbal and dramatic irony set aside.** Isolates the most commonly-confused distinction before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just call anything unexpected ironic."** This directly licenses the bad-luck pattern, undermining the reasonable-expectation discipline this concept specifically builds.
