# Consonant and Vowel Digraphs — `eng.phonics.digraphs`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.phonics.digraphs` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / phonics / "Consonant and Vowel
  Digraphs" — the direct counterpart to consonant blends, teaching the
  opposite classification (two letters, one new sound) using the
  identical slow-sounding-out test.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.phonics.consonant-blends` — specifically the
  slow-sounding-out discrimination test that concept installs;
  load-bearing because this concept's core content IS that same test
  applied to letter combinations that produce the opposite result, and
  the test only functions as a genuine discrimination tool once a
  learner has a confident, well-rehearsed sense of what a TWO-sound
  answer sounds like to compare against. `eng.phonics.short-vowels` —
  specifically the short/long vowel distinction and the closed-syllable
  structural check; load-bearing because this concept's vowel-digraph
  half (TA-4/TA-5) extends that same "check the spelling before
  assuming the sound" discipline to a new spelling pattern (vowel
  teams), building directly on the habit short-vowels first installed.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.phonics.long-vowels-silent-e`. This
  concept's vowel-digraph work ("two vowels go walking, the first one
  does the talking") is one of two spelling patterns that produce long
  vowel sounds — silent-e is the other, structurally parallel pattern
  taught immediately downstream, and this concept's exception-handling
  discipline (a strong pattern with known exceptions, not a rigid rule)
  transfers directly.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.80 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Produce the single, correct sound for each common consonant digraph
   (sh, ch, th voiced/voiceless, wh, ph, ck) without attempting to
   pronounce the two letters separately.
2. Read words containing consonant digraphs correctly.
3. Distinguish a digraph (one new sound) from a blend (multiple
   retained sounds) using the slow-sounding-out test, on both familiar
   and novel two-letter combinations.
4. Read regular vowel-digraph words by applying the long-first-vowel
   pattern ("two vowels go walking, the first one does the talking").
5. Recognize a vowel-digraph exception word and adjust the expected
   sound accordingly, without treating the exception as evidence the
   pattern itself is unreliable.

## Core Understanding

A digraph is two letters that together represent one new sound not
straightforwardly derivable from either letter said alone — this is the
structural OPPOSITE of a blend, where every letter's sound is retained.
Consonant digraphs in English are highly reliable: "sh," "ch," "th,"
"wh," "ph," and "ck" each map to one consistent sound with very few true
exceptions, and this reliability is what makes the slow-sounding-out
test decisive for them — say it slowly, hear one sound, it is a
digraph. Vowel digraphs (vowel "teams" like "ai," "ea," "oa") follow a
common and useful pattern — the first vowel typically supplies its own
long sound while the second is silent — but this pattern is
meaningfully LESS reliable than consonant digraphs; a real, listable set
of common words (said, bread, head) genuinely violate it. The correct
model for vowel digraphs is therefore asymmetric to consonant digraphs:
a strong first guess to be applied confidently, held with enough
flexibility to accommodate a known exception, rather than an absolute
rule.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "each consonant digraph makes one special sound,
  different from its two letters"**: installed directly by the
  Component 3 merge-into-one card-slide anchor, which makes the
  distinction from a blend physically visible. Sufficient for TA-1's
  isolated production but not yet connected to the blend/digraph
  discrimination test.
- **Intermediate model — "I can tell a blend from a digraph by saying it
  slowly and counting the sounds I hear"**: the correct, generalizable
  discrimination model, installed and rehearsed by TA-3. This is not a
  new skill from scratch — it is the SAME test the prerequisite concept
  taught, now applied in the direction that produces one sound instead
  of two. Upgrade trigger: correct classification of a genuinely novel
  two-letter combination as either blend or digraph, using the test
  rather than a memorized list.
- **Advanced model — "vowel digraphs follow a common pattern (long
  first vowel) but are less reliable than consonant digraphs, so I stay
  flexible"**: installed by TA-4 (the regular pattern) immediately
  followed by TA-5 (explicit exceptions). This is the model that
  resolves MC-VOWEL-DIGRAPHS-FOLLOW-SHORT-VOWEL-RULES's underlying
  rigidity — the learner holds a strong default expectation while
  remaining ready to revise it for a specific known word. Upgrade
  trigger: correctly reading an exception word (e.g., "bread") without
  forcing the expected long-vowel sound onto it.
- **Expert model — "consonant digraphs and vowel digraphs have
  different reliability profiles, and I calibrate my confidence
  accordingly"**: the fully integrated model, distinguishing the near-
  absolute reliability of consonant digraphs from the strong-but-
  imperfect reliability of vowel digraphs — this asymmetry itself is
  content the learner must hold, not merely the two sub-skills in
  isolation.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner not yet secure on TA-1/TA-2's
  consonant digraphs should not be given TA-4's vowel digraphs — the two
  halves of this concept are related by the shared digraph CONCEPT but
  are otherwise independent skill sets (one highly reliable, one
  pattern-with-exceptions), and conflating them before the first is
  secure risks the learner treating vowel-digraph unreliability as
  typical of "all digraphs."

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, corresponding to this
concept's two halves. The consonant-digraph failure exists because the
immediately prior concept (consonant blends) correctly taught that two
adjacent consonant letters retain both their sounds — a learner who has
just secured that expectation reasonably, if incorrectly, extends it to
the next two-consonant-letter combination met, which happens to be a
digraph behaving oppositely; this is the same overgeneralization
mechanism as the blends concept's own sibling misconception, now
appearing one concept later in its mirror-image form. The vowel-digraph
failure exists for a different reason: a learner has, by this point,
already internalized from `eng.phonics.short-vowels` that spelling
determines vowel sound in a checkable, rule-governed way — applying
that same expectation of full reliability to vowel digraphs is a
reasonable extension of a previously CORRECT discipline (check the
spelling, expect a rule) into a domain (vowel teams) where the rule is
real but imperfect, producing genuine surprise and resistance when an
exception word is met.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.phonics.digraphs.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions
this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-DIGRAPH-IS-A-BLEND (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: this is the direct mirror image of the
  prerequisite concept's own MC-BLENDS-AND-DIGRAPHS-ARE-THE-SAME-THING —
  both are Type 1 overgeneralizations of a rule ("two adjacent
  consonant letters = two sounds") that is entirely correct within its
  only-known domain so far (blends) at the moment the misconception
  fires, extended onto the next case (digraphs) where it breaks. The
  two concepts' misconceptions are the same error observed from
  opposite temporal positions in the curriculum.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, unprompted, says
  "wait, that's not what I did with 'st'" when first corrected on a
  digraph is showing productive awareness that the rule just applied
  worked before and needs revision — this is a MORE advanced state than
  a learner who applies the digraph-as-blend error silently and shows
  no surprise at the correction, and warrants less repair effort.

**MC-VOWEL-DIGRAPHS-FOLLOW-SHORT-VOWEL-RULES (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: despite its name echoing "short vowel
  rules," the mechanism here is closer to over-applying the RELIABILITY
  of the just-learned closed-syllable/short-vowel check than literally
  expecting a short vowel sound — the learner has correctly learned
  that vowel sound is spelling-determined and checkable, and
  over-extends the EXPECTATION OF FULL RELIABILITY from that correct
  prior domain into vowel digraphs, where the pattern is common but not
  absolute.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who reads an exception
  word like "bread" and then insists "but that's wrong, it should be
  /briːd/" (rather than accepting the correction and moving on) is
  showing a more entrenched form of this misconception — an expectation
  of TOTAL reliability — than a learner who simply mispronounces it
  once and accepts the correction; the former needs the explicit
  reliability-asymmetry framing (Adaptive Flags) stated directly, not
  merely another exception example.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — consonant digraphs are a locked combination; vowel
  digraphs are a strong weather forecast**: consonant digraphs behave
  the same way essentially every time (a locked, reliable combination);
  vowel digraphs give you a very good, usually-correct prediction that
  is nonetheless sometimes wrong, the way a forecast can be. Breaking
  point: "forecast" may suggest vowel-digraph sound is genuinely
  unpredictable in the moment, when in fact it is fixed and knowable
  per word — the analogy targets the LEARNER'S prior expectation
  (a prediction made in advance), not the actual phenomenon's
  randomness.
- **Alternative — "two vowels go walking, the first one does the
  talking"** (Blueprint's own mnemonic, Component 8): the canonical,
  memorable framing for the regular vowel-digraph pattern — reused here
  as this concept's primary teaching rhyme, not restated as new content.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT extend the merge-into-one card-slide anchor
  metaphor to vowel digraphs without qualification**: the card-slide
  demonstration (Component 3) correctly shows consonant digraphs
  merging into one new, DIFFERENT sound (neither original letter's
  sound survives). Applying this exact framing to vowel digraphs
  without noting that one of the original vowel sounds usually DOES
  survive (the first vowel's long sound) would blur the genuinely
  different mechanism at work in each half of this concept.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/auditory (merge-into-one card-slide)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical for the consonant-digraph half,
  referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any two written letters (on paper, in the
  air, or on a whiteboard) substitute for cards; the slow-sounding-out
  test itself requires no equipment.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-3's blend-vs-digraph
  re-discrimination sort and TA-5's regular-vs-exception vowel-word
  sort are both hands-on categorization activities.
- **Teacher-demo (reliability contrast)**: the tutor models reading a
  consonant-digraph word and a vowel-digraph EXCEPTION word back to
  back, explicitly naming that one behaved exactly as expected and the
  other required an adjustment — making the reliability asymmetry
  audible and concrete, not merely stated as an abstract rule.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  vowel-digraph word's sound (TA-4/TA-5), ask the learner to predict
  using the "two vowels go walking" rhyme before reading — Universal
  Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**Direct instruction wins for consonant digraphs; a brief, argued
discovery step suits the vowel-digraph pattern.** Consonant digraphs
are pure convention — there is no regularity to discover in why "sh"
makes /ʃ/, only a pairing to be demonstrated and rehearsed, matching
this concept's prerequisite's own reasoning for base consonant sounds.
The vowel-digraph long-first-vowel pattern, however, is a genuine,
statable regularity worth a short guided discovery: **need** (the
learner, having just secured short-vowel spelling checks, meets "boat"
and must determine its vowel sound); **playground** (TA-4 presents
several regular vowel-team words to examine); **invention** (the
learner is guided to notice the first vowel's LONG sound is what's
heard, and the second vowel is silent); **collision** (TA-5's exception
words — "bread," "said" — directly break a rule adopted too rigidly,
forcing the "strong pattern, known exceptions" revision); **formaliz-
ation** (MP-5's own-words statement of the blend/digraph AND the
pattern/exception distinctions); **compression** (fluent reading of
novel vowel-digraph words, defaulting to the pattern while remaining
alert to exceptions).

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (consonant digraph sound production) must precede TA-2 (digraphs
in words) for the same easier-to-harder reason as every prior phonics
concept in this chain: producing a sound in isolation precedes applying
it within a word. TA-3 (blend-vs-digraph re-discrimination) is placed
immediately after, not folded into TA-1/TA-2, because it requires BOTH
digraph sounds (just secured) AND blend sounds (from the prerequisite)
to be simultaneously available for genuine contrast — this is the
concept's core discrimination content and deserves dedicated,
undivided attention. TA-4 (vowel digraph regular pattern) is placed
after the entire consonant-digraph arc, not interleaved with it, because
mixing two structurally unrelated skill sets (consonant merging vs.
vowel-team spelling patterns) within the same teaching block risks
diluting focus on either. TA-5 (vowel digraph exceptions) must
immediately follow TA-4 rather than being deferred, because presenting
the regular pattern without its exceptions in the same session risks
the rigid over-application (MC-VOWEL-DIGRAPHS-FOLLOW-SHORT-VOWEL-RULES)
hardening before it is ever challenged. This entry does not restate the
Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see
Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: for consonant digraphs, whether the learner
  produces one clean merged sound or an audible attempt at two separate
  letter sounds (the MC-DIGRAPH-IS-A-BLEND signature); for vowel
  digraphs, whether the learner's response to an exception word is a
  brief, accepting adjustment or a resistant insistence on the expected
  regular sound — the latter is the stronger signal of the entrenched
  misconception.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a pause before reading a vowel-team
  word the learner has not seen paired with a known "regular vs.
  exception" label is a good sign — it suggests active uncertainty
  being resolved rather than blind rule-application; a fast, confident
  WRONG reading of a genuine exception word (with no hesitation)
  suggests the rigid over-application is still fully active.
- **Pronunciation stakes**: real for consonant digraphs (a wrong
  attempt to split "sh" into two sounds is simply incorrect, not a
  dialect variant); more nuanced for vowel digraphs, where some vowel-
  team realizations genuinely vary by dialect (e.g. certain "ea" words)
  — judge the STRUCTURAL correctness (pattern vs. exception recognized
  appropriately) rather than a single reference pronunciation.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "say it slowly — one sound, or two?"
  carries over directly from the prerequisite concept as this concept's
  consonant-digraph load-bearing sentence; "two vowels go walking, the
  first one does the talking — but check, is this one of the
  exceptions?" is its vowel-digraph counterpart, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3`.

## Assessment Signals

- **Diagnostic (golden probe)**: the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence
  probe (Component 1) — attempting "sh" as two separate sounds, then
  comparing to how the word is actually said — cleanly separates a
  learner still carrying over the blend strategy from one who has
  already made the digraph distinction.
- **Guided practice → independent practice**: the Blueprint's WE-1 → WE-2
  → WE-3 → MP-1 through MP-5 fading ladder is reused directly
  (Components 5–6) — not re-authored here.
- **Mastery gate set** (`../../assessment/05-mastery-verification.md
  §3`): production (fresh consonant digraph sound, MP-1); rule-
  discrimination (novel blend-vs-digraph pair, MP-2 — the strongest
  evidence the TEST, not memorized examples, generalizes); pattern-
  application (a novel regular vowel-digraph word, MP-3); exception-
  handling (a novel or dialect-sensitive exception word, MP-4 — this is
  the concept's single most diagnostic item, since correctly flagging
  dialect variation or genuine exceptions demonstrates the flexible,
  non-rigid advanced model); explanation (MP-5).
- **Interpretation of response pattern**: fast-and-correct on a novel
  regular vowel-digraph word confirms the pattern is internalized;
  fast-and-WRONG on a genuine exception word (forcing the regular sound
  without hesitation) is the dangerous quadrant for the vowel-digraph
  half — it signals the pattern has been adopted as an absolute rule
  rather than a strong default, and warrants re-running TA-5's exception
  work rather than more regular-pattern practice. For the consonant-
  digraph half, fast-and-WRONG (attempting to split the digraph into two
  sounds at speed) signals the blend strategy is still the dominant
  habit and should route to TA-3's re-discrimination.
- **Transfer items**: near (a fresh consonant-digraph word; a fresh
  regular vowel-digraph word); far (a vowel digraph not heavily
  rehearsed during teaching, e.g. "ow" as in "grow," testing whether the
  pattern generalizes beyond the most-practiced digraphs); real-world
  (spotting a digraph — consonant or vowel — in text the learner is
  already reading).

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a consonant-digraph item shrinks to the
  Component 3 card-slide anchor with the most familiar digraph (typically
  "sh," the first taught), modeled by the tutor first. On a vowel-
  digraph item, it shrinks to a single, heavily-rehearsed regular-
  pattern word (e.g., "boat") before returning to whatever prompted the
  failure.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: an attempted two-sound
  split on a consonant digraph is more likely than "I don't know," per
  MC-DIGRAPH-IS-A-BLEND's mechanism — route directly to that
  misconception's recovery (the merge-into-one card-slide, re-run
  slowly) rather than treating it as a generic reading error. On the
  vowel-digraph half, a confident forced-regular reading of an exception
  word is more likely than hesitation — when this appears, name the
  specific word as one of the small set of known exceptions rather than
  re-explaining the general pattern.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who pauses
  visibly on a vowel-digraph word, seeming to check it against a mental
  list before answering, is showing the correct advanced-model behavior
  (flexible pattern application) even on items where the final answer
  is uncertain — this checking behavior should be praised explicitly.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: mixed — consonant digraphs are facts (fixed sound
pairings, reviewed via standard spaced fact-review, similar to the base
consonant set); the blend-vs-digraph discrimination test and the vowel-
digraph pattern-with-exceptions model are both procedures (checking
habits, reviewed through continued application to novel words rather
than simple recall). Interleaving partners: mix consonant-digraph items
with blend items on an ongoing basis (the discrimination test must stay
exercised, not just initially learned); mix regular and exception
vowel-digraph words within the same practice set so the learner cannot
predict "regular" or "exception" from position alone.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: reading fresh, unpracticed consonant-digraph words and
  regular-pattern vowel-digraph words.
- **Far**: the "strong pattern, known exceptions" discipline installed
  for vowel digraphs transfers directly to
  `eng.phonics.long-vowels-silent-e`, the other major long-vowel
  spelling pattern, which shares the same reliability profile (common,
  useful, imperfect).
- **Real-world**: any text the learner is already reading contains both
  consonant and vowel digraphs in high frequency — immediate,
  ungated practice.
- **Expert-transfer**: fluent, automatic digraph recognition during
  silent reading, and eventual awareness of English spelling's broader
  pattern-with-exceptions character (a theme recurring throughout later
  spelling and vocabulary work), well past this node.
