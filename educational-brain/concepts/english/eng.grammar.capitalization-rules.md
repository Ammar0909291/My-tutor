# Capitalization Rules — `eng.grammar.capitalization-rules`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.capitalization-rules` (canonical English
  KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Capitalization Rules" —
  conventions governing when letters must be capitalized: sentence
  beginnings, proper nouns, titles, and the pronoun "I."
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.sentence-types-by-function` — load-
  bearing because the first-word capitalization rule fires at every
  SENTENCE boundary; a learner cannot reliably apply this rule across
  extended text without already recognizing where one sentence ends and
  the next begins, a recognition skill that concept's own functional
  classification and punctuation awareness directly established.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.end-punctuation`. Sentence-ending
  punctuation and sentence-starting capitalization are two halves of the
  same sentence-boundary-marking system — this concept's per-sentence
  capitalization rule and the downstream concept's period/question-
  mark/exclamation-point rules are learned as a paired, mutually
  reinforcing pair of conventions.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: remember · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.80 · **Est. hours**: 1

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Capitalize the first word of EVERY sentence in a multi-sentence
   passage, not just the first sentence of the whole text.
2. Capitalize proper nouns (names, cities, countries, days of the
   week, months).
3. Distinguish proper nouns from common nouns for similar concepts
   (mom/Mom, city/Paris, dog/Rex), capitalizing only the proper-noun
   member.
4. Capitalize the pronoun "I" (always, regardless of position) and
   titles used as part of a name (Dr., President, Aunt when directly
   preceding or substituting for a specific name).
5. Explain, in their own words, that capitalization depends on the
   proper/common noun distinction rather than emotional significance,
   and that the first-word rule repeats at every sentence boundary.

## Core Understanding

Capitalization follows two independent, learnable rules, not one
holistic "important words get capitals" intuition. The first rule is
about POSITION: the first word of EVERY sentence must be capitalized —
critically, this rule REPEATS at every single sentence boundary
throughout a passage, not just once at the very start of the whole
piece of writing. The second rule is about WORD TYPE: capitalize PROPER
nouns (specific, named individuals or places: Sarah, Paris, Monday, the
White House) but not COMMON nouns (general categories: mom, beach,
teacher, city) — even when the common noun refers to something the
writer cares about deeply. Emotional significance is never the test;
specificity of naming is. A genuine bridge exception validates the
underlying "important things deserve capitals" instinct without
contradicting the rule: a common noun used in DIRECT ADDRESS, functioning
AS a name-substitute, IS capitalized ("I love my mom" — common noun,
lowercase; "I love you, Mom" — direct address, functioning like a
proper name, capitalized). Finally, the pronoun "I" is always
capitalized regardless of position in the sentence, and titles are
capitalized specifically when used as part of a name (Dr. Smith,
President Lincoln) rather than as a general common-noun role
(the doctor, a president).

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "capitalize whatever word feels important or
  significant, and capitalize only the very first word of the whole
  piece of writing"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this
  concept exists to correct — a natural default because emotionally
  significant common nouns (mom, dog, home) genuinely FEEL deserving of
  special marking, and because the very first word of a text is the
  single most visually prominent capitalization point a beginning
  writer notices, drawing attention away from the repeating per-
  sentence rule.
- **Intermediate model — "I capitalize the first word of every
  sentence, not just the first sentence"**: installed by TA-1, using
  the Component 3 capitalization-checklist-walk anchor, resolving
  MC-CAPITALIZE-ONLY-THE-VERY-FIRST-WORD-OF-A-TEXT. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly capitalizing the first word of a fresh sentence in the
  middle of a passage, not just the passage's opening.
- **Advanced model — "I capitalize proper nouns (names, places, days,
  months) as a specific, named-entity category"**: installed by TA-2,
  extending capitalization beyond sentence-position rules to word-type
  rules.
- **Expert model — "I distinguish proper from common nouns by
  specificity of naming, not emotional significance, and I correctly
  apply the 'I' and title-specific conventions"**: installed by TA-3
  and TA-4, the concept's genuine mastery bar, resolving
  MC-CAPITALIZE-ANY-IMPORTANT-SOUNDING-WORD by directly demonstrating
  that a beloved common noun (mom, dog) stays lowercase while its
  proper-noun counterpart (a specific name) is capitalized.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 has no capitalization system at all (many non-
  Latin-script languages) or a different convention entirely (German
  capitalizes all nouns), expect this concept to require genuinely
  foundational instruction — or active unlearning of an all-nouns-
  capitalized habit — not a quick review of an assumed-familiar system.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here. The important-word
failure exists because emotionally significant common nouns genuinely
DO feel special, and capitalization is a visually salient marking that
intuitively seems to signal "this word matters" — nothing marks the
specific proper-vs-common distinction (rather than emotional weight)
as the actual rule until a learner is directly asked whether "beach" in
"we went to the beach" names one specific, particular place with its
own official name. The first-word-once failure exists because the very
first word of an entire text IS the single most visually prominent
capitalization point a beginning writer encounters — nothing marks the
rule as REPEATING at every sentence boundary until a learner is
directly shown a multi-sentence passage where only the opening sentence
is capitalized and asked whether the rule applies just once or every
time a new sentence starts.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.capitalization-rules.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-CAPITALIZE-ANY-IMPORTANT-SOUNDING-WORD (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 2, perceptual intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: Type 2, not Type 1 — this is not an
  overextension of a partially-correct English rule but a genuine
  perceptual/emotional default: capitalization is visually salient, and
  marking emotionally significant words with that salience is an
  intuitively appealing correspondence a learner reaches for absent any
  rule at all, the same category of default as adjective-order
  intuitions being genuinely felt rather than taught; the misconception
  survives because the underlying instinct (important things deserve
  special marking) is not itself wrong, only misapplied to the wrong
  test (feeling vs. specificity-of-naming).
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown "we
  went to the beach," spontaneously asks "is this ONE specific,
  particular beach with its own name?" before deciding on
  capitalization is showing the proper-vs-common test has been
  genuinely internalized, replacing the feeling-based default.

**MC-CAPITALIZE-ONLY-THE-VERY-FIRST-WORD-OF-A-TEXT (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — the very first
  word of an entire text genuinely IS the single most visually
  prominent, first-noticed capitalization point, so treating it as the
  whole rule (rather than one instance of a repeating rule) is a
  reasonable generalization until a learner is directly shown a multi-
  sentence passage with only the opening sentence capitalized.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when writing a
  fresh multi-sentence passage, spontaneously capitalizes the first
  word of the SECOND and THIRD sentences without being separately
  reminded each time is showing the per-sentence-boundary rule has
  generalized beyond the specific TA-1 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the capitalization checklist walk itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically
  circling the first letter of EACH new sentence in a multi-sentence
  passage (confirming the rule repeats) while separately underlining
  proper nouns regardless of position (confirming the word-type rule is
  independent of sentence position), directly embodies both rules as a
  concrete, checkable walk-through rather than an abstract description.
  Breaking point: the checklist walk demonstrates both rules well but
  doesn't on its own address the direct-address bridge exception
  (Mom/mom), which needs its own separate, explicit demonstration to
  avoid feeling like a contradiction of the just-taught proper/common
  test.
- **Alternative — a traffic light that resets at every intersection,
  not just the first one**: the "stop and check" moment (capitalize)
  happens at every new intersection (sentence start) along a route, not
  just the very first one when leaving the driveway. Breaking point:
  useful for the repeating-rule insight, but doesn't address the
  proper-vs-common word-type distinction, which the checklist walk's
  own underlining practice handles directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe the direct-address exception
  (Mom/mom) as "capitalize it if you really love them"**: this framing
  directly reinforces MC-CAPITALIZE-ANY-IMPORTANT-SOUNDING-WORD by
  reintroducing emotional significance as the test; the actual rule is
  structural — direct address functioning AS a name-substitute — not a
  measure of affection, and both "I love my mom" (lowercase) and "I
  love you, Mom" (capitalized) can express identical depth of feeling.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the capitalization checklist walk)**: the
  Blueprint's Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any multi-sentence passage the learner has
  written or can dictate supplies real TA-1 material for the per-
  sentence rule; any specific named person, place, or pet in the
  learner's own life supplies real TA-2/TA-3 proper-noun material,
  paired directly against its common-noun category.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-3's proper-vs-common
  discrimination task (mom/Mom, city/Paris, dog/Rex) is inherently
  hands-on, requiring the learner to actively apply the specificity
  test to paired examples, not merely recognize an isolated stated
  fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the one-specific-place test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether
  "beach" in "we went to the beach" names one specific, particular
  place with its own official name, making the proper-vs-common test
  directly audible and testable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  capitalization attempt (TA-1 through TA-4), ask the learner to state
  their own attempt before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions, though this
is a genuinely concise, one-hour concept per the KG's own estimate.**
The per-sentence-boundary rule and the proper-vs-common specificity
test are both genuine, directly testable phenomena well-suited to
guided discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured TA-1's opening-
sentence capitalization, is shown their own multi-sentence attempt with
only the first sentence capitalized, discovering the second sentence
"looks wrong" once directly compared); **playground/invention** (TA-1's
first-sentence practice and TA-2's proper-noun practice build initial
confidence with both rules independently before their harder
applications are introduced); **collision** (the Blueprint's own two-
sentence contrast breaks the first-word-once assumption; the "beach"
vs. "Waikiki Beach" probe breaks the important-feeling-word assumption);
**formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of both the proper/common
specificity test and the per-sentence-boundary rule); **compression**
(TA-4's fluent application of the "I" and title conventions, integrating
both prior rules without each instance separately re-derived). The
specific terms "proper noun," "common noun," and "direct address" are
convention-bound grammatical terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying
distinctions through the checklist-walk anchor and TA-1–2.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (capitalizing the first word of every sentence) is placed first,
directly targeting MC-CAPITALIZE-ONLY-THE-VERY-FIRST-WORD-OF-A-TEXT, per
the Blueprint's own session architecture — establishing the repeating
per-sentence rule immediately, since this is a common, early-writer gap
worth correcting before word-type rules are introduced. TA-2
(capitalizing proper nouns) extends capitalization to the independent
word-type dimension, using the most unambiguous proper-noun examples
(names, cities, days, months) first. TA-3 (distinguishing proper from
common nouns) directly targets
MC-CAPITALIZE-ANY-IMPORTANT-SOUNDING-WORD by contrasting paired
common/proper examples for similar concepts, once the base proper-noun
category from TA-2 is secure. TA-4 (capitalizing "I" and titles) is
placed last as the concept's integration capstone, applying two
additional specific conventions ("I" always; titles when part of a
name) that build on the now-secure proper/common distinction. This
entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script
(Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously capitalizes
  the first word of every new sentence in a fresh multi-sentence
  passage (not just the opening one), and whether the learner
  spontaneously checks a fresh common/proper noun pair for specificity
  of naming rather than emotional weight — these are the primary
  diagnostic signals for whether TA-1 and TA-3's disciplines have
  genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before capitalizing a
  fresh emotionally-loaded common noun, followed by a correct lowercase
  decision (or correct capitalization only in direct address),
  suggests the learner is consciously applying the specificity test
  rather than the feeling-based default — a good sign. Instant,
  confident capitalization of a beloved common noun outside direct
  address (without checking specificity) is the concerning pattern for
  MC-CAPITALIZE-ANY-IMPORTANT-SOUNDING-WORD.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Protocol Routing — for S9 learners whose L1 has no
  capitalization system at all, or a different convention like German's
  all-nouns-capitalized pattern, treat this as needing genuinely
  foundational instruction or active unlearning, not an assumed-quick
  review.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does that word name one specific,
  particular thing, or a general category?" is this concept's load-
  bearing sentence for MC-CAPITALIZE-ANY-IMPORTANT-SOUNDING-WORD, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh potentially emotionally-loaded common noun is
  introduced, making the specificity check a routinely-invited response
  rather than an afterthought.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework — two independent, `remember`-level
conventions (a repeating positional rule tied to sentence boundaries,
and a word-type rule tied to the proper/common noun distinction),
applied to genuinely novel passages and word pairs; mastery is
demonstrated by correctly capitalizing fresh multi-sentence passages and
correctly discriminating fresh common/proper noun pairs (MP-1 through
MP-4) rather than by recognizing a fixed, previously-taught example
list. Interleaving partners: mix per-sentence-boundary items (testing
TA-1) with proper-noun items (testing TA-2/TA-3) and "I"/title items
(testing TA-4) in ongoing review, so the learner continues exercising
both the positional and the word-type dimensions of capitalization as
separately-verified competencies.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: capitalizing every sentence's first word in fresh multi-
  sentence passages; correctly discriminating fresh common/proper noun
  pairs; applying the "I" and title conventions.
- **Far**: this concept's per-sentence-boundary awareness is the direct
  paired counterpart to `eng.grammar.end-punctuation`'s rules for
  marking the OTHER end of the same sentence boundary (periods,
  question marks, exclamation points) — the two concepts together form
  the complete sentence-boundary-marking system.
- **Real-world**: every piece of writing the learner produces — from a
  short note to an extended multi-sentence passage — depends on
  correct, consistent capitalization for basic readability and
  correctness, and correct proper-noun recognition affects how names
  and places are represented respectfully and accurately.
- **Expert-transfer**: polished, professional-quality writing at any
  advanced level depends on effortless, automatic capitalization across
  extended text, since inconsistent per-sentence capitalization or
  incorrect proper/common noun marking remains a highly visible,
  immediately noticeable marker of unpolished writing even at otherwise
  advanced proficiency.
