## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify opinion-laden sentences in a mixed passage and revise them into objective explanatory statements.
2. Elaborate a bare technical definition with a concrete example or analogy that would clarify it for an unfamiliar reader.
3. Select and justify an appropriate expository organizational pattern for a given topic.
4. Support an explanatory claim with a specific fact or example while maintaining objective framing.
5. Compose a complete original expository piece using appropriate organization, objective framing, and elaborated definitions.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **expository writing does not mean stating your opinion about the topic** — its job is to explain, inform, or clarify using facts, definitions, and examples, not to argue a position or share a personal viewpoint; "I think photosynthesis is amazing" is opinion/persuasive framing, while "Photosynthesis is the process by which plants convert sunlight..." is objective explanation, and expository writing must stick to the latter. Second, **a definition or fact alone is not enough to explain a complex topic** — a bare, technically-correct definition ("Osmosis is the movement of water across a semi-permeable membrane") often doesn't actually help an unfamiliar reader understand HOW or WHY something works; good expository writing follows a definition with a concrete example, analogy, or elaboration that makes the abstract idea tangible.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "expository writing means sharing my opinion or view about the topic; a correct definition is enough to explain something."**
The simulation: insert personal opinions and value judgments when writing about a topic, and state bare technical definitions without further elaboration.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — "I think photosynthesis is amazing" versus an objective process explanation, asked which explains versus expresses a viewpoint; and a bare osmosis definition versus the same definition with a celery-in-water example, asked which actually helps an unfamiliar reader understand.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Explain, don't argue or opine. A definition needs an example to actually clarify."

**Intermediate — "I keep opinions out now, but I still tend to leave technical definitions bare without checking whether they'd actually make sense to someone new to the topic."**
The simulation: correctly avoids opinion framing but hasn't yet reliably added elaboration to bare definitions.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-2's direct practice adding a concrete example or analogy to a bare definition.
*Shelf-life warning*: "After stating a definition, ask: would someone unfamiliar with this actually understand it, or do they need an example?"

**Advanced — "I keep my writing objective, elaborate definitions with examples, choose an appropriate organizational pattern, support claims with objective evidence, and compose a full expository piece integrating all of this."**
The simulation: integrates objective framing, definition elaboration, organizational-pattern selection, and evidence support into one coherent, generative composition. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the three direct cross-linked concepts — the writing process, essay structure (sustained organized explanatory content), and technical writing (the same faithful-explanation discipline in specialized contexts).
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you objective, clear explanation. Essay structure extends this to longer pieces; technical writing applies it to specialized professional contexts."

**Expert — "at fluency, objective framing and definition elaboration happen as an integrated part of ordinary explanatory drafting."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit opinion-check and elaboration-check routine is a scaffold for a compositional judgment that, at true fluency, operates as an integrated part of drafting.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **most everyday writing instruction and casual communication blend opinion and information freely** (a student's natural voice often includes evaluative commentary), so the deliberate discipline of stripping out personal viewpoint requires an explicit, unfamiliar correction against a well-practiced habit.

The second mechanism is that **a correct, formal definition FEELS complete because it's technically accurate**, so a writer reasonably (but incorrectly) treats formal correctness as sufficient, without checking whether the definition's specific wording would actually be comprehensible to someone encountering the concept for the first time.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-EXPOSITORY-WRITING-MEANS-STATING-YOUR-OPINION-ABOUT-THE-TOPIC
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry — the opinion/objective-explanation discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from everyday writing and communication habitually blending personal viewpoint with information, without the genre-specific discipline of separating the two ever being explicitly taught until this concept.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct sorting practice distinguishing opinion from fact**, since the fix requires developing a genre-specific filtering habit, not merely being told opinions don't belong.
- **Verification of death**: given a passage mixing opinion and fact, the learner identifies the opinion-laden sentences and revises them into objective explanatory statements.

### MC-A-DEFINITION-OR-FACT-ALONE-IS-ENOUGH-TO-EXPLAIN-A-COMPLEX-TOPIC
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — the bare-osmosis-definition/celery-example discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from formal correctness feeling like sufficiency, mistaking technical accuracy for actual comprehensibility to an unfamiliar reader.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct comparison of a bare definition against the same definition with elaboration**, since the counterintuitive claim (correct isn't the same as clear) is more convincing demonstrated than asserted.
- **Verification of death**: given a bare technical definition, the learner adds a concrete example or analogy that would clarify it for an unfamiliar reader.

## Analogies

**Best — the Expert Translator** (Blueprint Component 3): a translator whose job is faithful, accessible explanation, NOT adding personal opinion about the information's importance, directly seeds the opinion-free fix; the same translator adding a concrete example or analogy to a bare technical definition card directly seeds the elaboration fix.
*Breaking point*: a human translator has an obvious, externally-set standard (fidelity to the original text) that an expository writer's "faithful explanation" standard doesn't have quite as concretely, since the writer is generating original explanatory content rather than converting existing text — useful for the objective-not-evaluative intuition, less precise for the exact nature of what's being "translated."

**Alternative — a museum docent explaining an exhibit versus a museum's promotional brochure.** A docent's job is to explain what an artifact is and how it worked, using accessible language and examples, not to persuade visitors the museum is worth visiting (the brochure's job); expository writing is the docent's mode, not the brochure's.
*Breaking point*: a docent's live explanation can respond dynamically to a visitor's specific confusion, while written expository text must anticipate confusion in advance through deliberate elaboration choices.

**Visual anchor — the Expert Translator**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Share what you think about the topic when you write about it."** Directly installs the opinion-sharing misconception as a stated instruction.
- **"A clear, correct definition is all you need to explain something."** Directly installs the definition-sufficiency misconception as a stated rule.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Expert Translator (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Should a translator add their own opinion about whether the information is good?" Then contrast an opinion-inserting translator with a purely explanatory one; separately, have the translator add a clarifying example to a bare definition.
2. **The opinion/objective-explanation collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Which one is explaining the topic versus expressing a viewpoint?"
3. **The bare-osmosis-definition/celery-example collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Which explanation actually helps someone who's never heard of osmosis understand it?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both explanatory facts are directly checkable by sorting sentences for opinion versus fact and by testing definitions against an "unfamiliar reader" standard.

1. **Need**: "If you write 'photosynthesis is amazing,' have you explained anything about how photosynthesis works?"
2. **Playground**: the learner sorts several sentences into opinion/fact freely before being told the official objective-explanation standard.
3. **Invention**: "Can you add an example to this bare definition that would help someone who's never heard the term before?"
4. **Collision**: present a bare definition and the same definition with an example; ask which actually helps an unfamiliar reader.
5. **Formalization**: name the two explanatory facts — expository writing explains, it doesn't opine; a definition needs elaboration to genuinely clarify — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Explain, don't argue — and always follow a definition with an example."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Opinion vs. fact sorting (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the opinion-sharing misconception before elaboration technique is taught.
- **Definitions that actually clarify (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting the definition-sufficiency misconception once objective framing is secure.
- **Organizational patterns for explanation (TA-3) comes third**, adding structural technique once content discipline is established.
- **Using evidence and examples objectively (TA-4) and composing a full original expository piece (TA-5) come last**, extending objective support and integrating everything into a generative capstone — matching this program's established sequencing principle.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for whether a sentence is opinion or fact, and their added elaboration to a bare definition**, since a technically-correct piece with no stated objectivity-check or elaboration-sufficiency reasoning cannot be distinguished from surface compliance.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated opinion-check** ("is this a fact, or am I sneaking in my own view here?") applied to a drafted sentence — the direct behavioral signature of the objectivity fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary addition of an example or analogy after stating a definition**, without being prompted for that specific step — direct evidence the elaboration fix is internalized.
- **A drafted piece containing value-laden language ("amazing," "should"), or a definition left bare with no example** — the direct behavioral signature of either misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Explain, don't argue — and never leave a definition without an example."*

- **Supporting a claim with objective evidence (MP-4).** Confirms evidence-support skill while maintaining objective framing.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"I said what I think about the topic, isn't that part of writing about it?"** — take this as a reasonable extension of everyday communication habits; respond by naming the genre boundary: "That's your view — save it for persuasive writing. Here, stick to what's factually true about the topic."
- **"I gave the correct definition, isn't that enough?"** — a reasonable-sounding standard given the definition's technical accuracy; respond by testing comprehension: "It's correct, yes — but would someone who's never heard this term actually understand it from just that? Let's add an example."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full original expository piece down to **elaborating a single bare definition with one example, with no opinion-sorting or organizational-pattern task demanded.** Isolates the most basic clarification skill before returning to fuller composition.
- **Never shrink to "just write down the facts you know."** This licenses bare, under-elaborated content without the clarification discipline this concept specifically builds.
