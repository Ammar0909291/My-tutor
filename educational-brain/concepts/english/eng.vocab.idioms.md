# Idioms — `eng.vocab.idioms`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.idioms` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Idioms" — fixed
  expressions whose overall meaning cannot be derived from the literal
  meaning of their individual words, extending the prerequisite's
  transparency test to its most extreme case: complete non-literalness.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.collocations` — load-bearing because
  that concept already installed the transparency test (can the
  meaning be derived from the individual words?) that idioms fail
  completely, and it already established the general principle that
  some fixed expressions must be learned rather than logically derived
  — idioms are the more extreme case of that same principle, where the
  meaning is COMPLETELY non-literal rather than merely conventionally
  paired.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none — this concept is terminal in the current
  curriculum sequence, though cross-linked to
  `eng.linguistics.pragmatics-intro`, since correctly interpreting an
  idiom depends on recognizing its conventional, context-dependent
  meaning rather than its literal one, a genuinely pragmatic (not
  merely lexical) skill.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Recognize when an expression is being used idiomatically (its non-
   literal meaning) rather than literally, using context to distinguish
   the two.
2. Explain the overall meaning of at least five common English idioms.
3. Recognize that idioms are typically FIXED in form — most cannot be
   freely altered (changing individual words, adding modifiers) without
   losing their idiomatic meaning or sounding wrong to a fluent speaker.
4. Use a given idiom correctly, with its exact fixed wording, in an
   original sentence.
5. Fully analyze a short text containing multiple idioms — identifying
   each as idiomatic, stating its correct meaning, and confirming fixed
   wording by testing a synonym substitution.

## Core Understanding

An idiom is a fixed expression whose overall meaning cannot be derived
from the literal meaning of its individual words at all — "it's raining
cats and dogs" has no derivable connection to actual animals falling
from the sky; the phrase's real meaning ("raining heavily") is a fixed,
conventional symbol that must simply be learned as a whole unit,
exactly the way a country's flag carries meaning as a SYMBOL of that
nation with no logical connection to the literal properties of its
individual colors. This is the most extreme point on the transparency
spectrum the prerequisite concept introduced: collocations are
transparent (habitually paired, but still logically derivable from
their words), while idioms are completely opaque (the whole expression
must be known, since the individual words offer no usable clue at all).
A second, equally load-bearing fact is that idioms are FIXED
expressions — their exact wording is conventionally locked in, and
substituting even a very close synonym for one word typically breaks
the expression entirely: "kick the bucket" (meaning to die) becomes
meaningless as "kick the pail," even though "pail" is a genuine synonym
for "bucket," because idiomatic meaning attaches to the SPECIFIC,
memorized combination of words, not to any of the words' individual
meanings or their synonyms.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "I can figure out an idiom's meaning by combining
  the literal meanings of its individual words"**: the arriving,
  pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct — a natural
  extension of ordinary reading strategy, where combining individual
  word meanings normally IS how sentence meaning is constructed, giving
  no built-in signal that idioms are the specific case where this
  otherwise-reliable strategy fails completely.
- **Intermediate model — "I can recognize when a phrase's literal
  meaning doesn't fit its context, signaling it's an idiom, and I can
  learn common idioms' conventional meanings directly"**: installed by
  TA-1's context-mismatch-recognition practice and TA-2's direct
  meaning instruction, using the Component 3 national-flag anchor to
  make the fixed-symbolic-meaning insight concrete. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly recognizing a genuinely novel phrase as idiomatic from
  context mismatch alone, before knowing its specific meaning.
- **Advanced model — "idioms are fixed expressions — I cannot freely
  substitute even a close synonym without breaking the idiomatic
  meaning"**: installed by TA-3's fixed-wording verification practice,
  resolving MC-B-YOU-CAN-FREELY-CHANGE-WORDS-WITHIN-AN-IDIOM-AS-LONG-
  AS-THE-MEANING-STAYS-SIMILAR through the felt contrast between "kick
  the bucket" and the broken "kick the pail." Upgrade trigger:
  spontaneously testing a suspected idiom's flexibility by imagining a
  synonym substitution before assuming it would still work.
- **Expert model — "I can fully analyze a text with multiple idioms —
  recognizing each, stating its meaning, and confirming its fixed
  wording — and use idioms correctly in my own original writing"**:
  installed by TA-4's full-synthesis task. Per the Blueprint's own
  explicit protocol routing, MC-A generally needs resolving before
  MC-B, since recognizing an expression AS idiomatic is itself a
  prerequisite to learning that its specific wording is fixed.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, a
  learner who masters recognition and meaning (MP-1/MP-2) but struggles
  to actively USE idioms correctly in original writing (MP-4) should be
  treated as having a PRODUCTION gap specifically, not a comprehension
  gap — this warrants more guided practice generating original
  sentences with a given idiom, not a re-teach of recognition or
  meaning.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of otherwise-reliable reading strategies. The word-by-word-
translation failure exists because combining individual word meanings
IS the normal, generally reliable strategy for constructing sentence
meaning across the vast majority of ordinary English — nothing in that
overwhelmingly successful general strategy signals that a specific
category of fixed expressions (idioms) will produce a meaning
completely disconnected from the individual words, until the learner
directly encounters a context where the literal, word-by-word
interpretation clearly fails to make sense. The free-substitution
failure exists because a learner who has just correctly grasped an
idiom's OVERALL meaning (kick the bucket = to die) reasonably assumes
that meaning is carried loosely enough to survive minor rewording, the
same way most ordinary sentences tolerate synonym substitution without
losing their meaning — nothing in learning the idiom's meaning alone
signals that its exact wording, not just its general topic, is what
carries that meaning, until a specific synonym-substituted version is
tested and found to be meaningless or wrong to a fluent speaker.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/eng.vocab.idioms.md`,
Component 1) documents both misconceptions this concept produces, with
full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content — reused
by reference below, with birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-A-YOU-CAN-FIGURE-OUT-AN-IDIOMS-MEANING-BY-TRANSLATING-EACH-WORD-
LITERALLY (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — word-by-word
  meaning-combination is genuinely the correct, reliable strategy for
  constructing the meaning of the overwhelming majority of English
  sentences a learner encounters, so the learner extends this reliable
  strategy into an unconditional approach, with nothing in ordinary
  reading exposure marking that idioms are the specific, bounded
  exception category where this strategy fails completely until a
  context-mismatch is directly encountered.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, upon meeting a
  phrase whose literal meaning clearly doesn't fit its context,
  spontaneously says something like "that must be one of those idiom
  things — let me figure out what it actually means from the situation"
  before being told is showing the context-mismatch-as-idiom-signal
  model has been genuinely internalized.

**MC-B-YOU-CAN-FREELY-CHANGE-WORDS-WITHIN-AN-IDIOM-AS-LONG-AS-THE-
MEANING-STAYS-SIMILAR (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: also a Type 1 signature, distinct
  mechanism — ordinary sentences genuinely do tolerate synonym
  substitution without losing their meaning (a general and reliable
  property of compositional language), so the learner reasonably
  extends this general flexibility onto idioms as well, with nothing in
  learning an idiom's overall meaning alone marking that idiomatic
  meaning attaches specifically to the FIXED, exact wording rather than
  to the general topic or gist, until a synonym-substituted version is
  directly tested against fluent-speaker judgment.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, before assuming a
  substituted version of an idiom still works, spontaneously says
  something like "wait, would a fluent speaker actually still
  understand this the same way?" is showing the fixed-wording
  discipline has generalized beyond the specific TA-3 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the national-flag-and-chemical-formula anchor itself
  is the analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): a
  national flag's overall meaning as a symbol of a country has nothing
  to do with the literal properties of its individual colors, directly
  embodying the fixed, whole-unit-meaning insight (MC-A's fix); a
  precise chemical formula where substituting one element for a very
  similar one produces a completely different result directly embodies
  the fixed-wording insight (MC-B's fix). Breaking point: a flag's
  symbolic meaning is essentially permanent and universally fixed
  within a culture, which can understate that a few idioms genuinely do
  have minor accepted variants (e.g., regional or informal alternate
  wordings) — this entry treats the flag/formula anchor as the
  introductory frame for the DOMINANT, reliable pattern (most idioms are
  strictly fixed), not a claim that zero variation ever exists anywhere
  in idiomatic English.
- **Alternative — a secret handshake, not a casual greeting**: a
  specific secret handshake sequence must be performed exactly as
  agreed to be recognized by the group that shares it; performing a
  "similar" sequence of gestures doesn't count, the same way a synonym-
  substituted idiom doesn't carry the original idiomatic meaning to a
  fluent speaker. Breaking point: useful for reinforcing the exact-
  wording-matters insight memorably, but should not be extended to
  imply idioms are meant to be exclusive or secretive — idioms are
  widely shared, conventional, everyday expressions, not deliberately
  restricted codes.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe idioms as "broken grammar" or
  "nonsense phrases that don't really mean anything logical"**: framing
  idioms as illogical or defective risks discouraging genuine interest
  in learning them and obscures that idioms ARE meaningful, systematic,
  conventionally-understood expressions within the language — they are
  non-literal, not nonsensical, and every fluent speaker treats them as
  legitimate, meaningful units of the language.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
context-mismatch signal and the fixed-wording property are both
genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured the collocation transparency
test, is asked whether "raining cats and dogs" makes literal sense in
context); **playground/invention** (TA-1's context-mismatch recognition
across four sentences lets the learner notice the pattern of literal
failure signaling idiom); **collision** (the Blueprint's own raining-
cats-and-dogs probe breaks the word-by-word-translation assumption; the
kick-the-bucket/kick-the-pail contrast breaks the free-substitution
assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's full contextual analysis,
requiring explicit statement of both recognition and fixed-wording
confirmation); **compression** (TA-4's fluent full-synthesis application
without each step separately re-derived). The specific TERM "idiom"
itself is convention-bound terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying context-
mismatch-signals-non-literal-meaning distinction through the anchor and
TA-1.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (recognizing when an expression is idiomatic) is placed first,
directly targeting MC-A, because recognizing an expression AS idiomatic
is itself a prerequisite to everything that follows — per the
Blueprint's own explicit protocol routing, MC-A generally needs
resolving before MC-B, since a learner who cannot yet identify that a
phrase is being used idiomatically has no occasion to apply the fixed-
wording discipline to it at all. TA-2 (learning common idiom meanings)
follows, providing the direct-instruction content (specific idiom-to-
meaning mappings) that TA-1's recognition skill needs to draw on — this
is convention-bound vocabulary knowledge, appropriately taught directly
rather than discovered. TA-3 (idioms are fixed expressions) is placed
next, directly targeting MC-B, sequenced after the learner already has
secure idiom recognition and meaning knowledge (TA-1/TA-2) to test the
fixed-wording property against. TA-4 (full application) is placed last
as the concept's complete synthesis task, requiring recognition (TA-1),
meaning knowledge (TA-2), and fixed-wording verification (TA-3) all
applied together to a genuinely novel text. This entry does not restate
the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see
Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously flags a
  context mismatch ("that doesn't make literal sense here") when
  meeting a fresh idiom, rather than attempting a literal, word-by-word
  interpretation and getting stuck — this is the primary diagnostic
  signal for whether TA-1's recognition skill has genuinely generalized
  beyond the specific practice sentences.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before confirming
  whether a synonym-substituted idiom variant still works, followed by
  a correct "no, that would sound wrong to a fluent speaker" judgment,
  suggests the learner is consciously testing rather than assuming
  flexibility — a good sign. Instant, confident acceptance of a broken
  synonym-substituted variant as equivalent is the concerning pattern
  for MC-B-YOU-CAN-FREELY-CHANGE-WORDS-WITHIN-AN-IDIOM-AS-LONG-AS-THE-
  MEANING-STAYS-SIMILAR.
- **Production-vs-comprehension stakes**: real and specifically named
  in the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags — a learner who correctly
  recognizes and explains idioms (MP-1/MP-2) but hesitates or errs when
  asked to actively USE one in original writing (MP-4) has a distinct
  production gap, not a comprehension failure, and should receive
  targeted generative practice rather than more recognition drills.
- **Over-generalization caution**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive
  Flags, watch for a learner who becomes overly cautious and starts
  flagging every non-literal or figurative phrase as an "idiom" — this
  requires the tutor to clarify that idioms are specifically FIXED,
  conventional expressions, distinct from more flexible, original
  figurative language like metaphor or simile.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does that make literal sense here, or
  is something else going on?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence
  for MC-A, per `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md
  §3` — asked whenever a fresh potentially-idiomatic phrase appears,
  making the context-mismatch check a routinely-invited response
  rather than an afterthought.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on an idiom-recognition task shrinks to the
  Component 3 national-flag anchor using the simplest, most familiar
  idiom available (e.g., "hit the books," per the Blueprint's own
  WE-1), with the tutor modeling the context-mismatch recognition
  before the learner attempts a fresh idiom independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: an attempted literal,
  word-by-word interpretation (rather than "I don't know") is more
  likely for MC-A — route directly to the Blueprint's own conflict-
  evidence probe (does this make literal sense in context?) rather than
  treating it as a generic vocabulary gap. For MC-B, confidently
  accepting a synonym-substituted idiom as equivalent is more likely
  than confusion — route directly to TA-3's fluent-speaker-judgment
  test.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously compares an English idiom to an equivalent expression in
  another language they know, unprompted, is showing genuine engagement
  with the fixed-conventional-expression concept — per the Blueprint's
  own Adaptive Flags, this cross-linguistic comparison should be
  leveraged explicitly, since most languages have idioms, making the
  concept feel less arbitrary through comparison.

  sentences.
- **Far**: this concept's context-dependent, conventional-over-literal

  semantics — the cross-link the Blueprint's own frontmatter names

- **Real-world**: every idiom the learner encounters in conversation,
  media, or reading is an immediate, ungated opportunity to apply
  context-based recognition and conventional-meaning inference, and
  every opportunity to use language naturally is a chance to
  incorporate correctly-worded idioms into original speech or writing.
- **Expert-transfer**: genuinely native-like, culturally fluent English
  communication depends substantially on idiomatic competence, since
  idioms are pervasive in everyday spoken and informal written English
  and their absence or misuse is one of the clearest markers
  distinguishing non-native from native-level fluency.
