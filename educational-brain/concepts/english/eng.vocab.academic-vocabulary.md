# Academic Vocabulary — `eng.vocab.academic-vocabulary`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.academic-vocabulary` (canonical English
  KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Academic Vocabulary" —
  general, discipline-neutral vocabulary that recurs frequently across
  academic texts (the Academic Word List tradition), distinct from the
  technical, subject-specific jargon of any one field.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.register-and-formality`,
  `eng.vocab.word-formation-processes` — both load-bearing: register-
  and-formality established that formal register is situationally
  appropriate, not "try-hard," which this concept presupposes when
  treating academic vocabulary as a legitimate, learnable word set
  rather than pretentious jargon; word-formation-processes established
  how words are built from roots and affixes, the foundation for
  recognizing academic word families (analyze/analysis/analytical) and
  the morphological patterns common across the Academic Word List.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none — this concept is terminal in the current
  curriculum sequence.
- **Difficulty**: advanced · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 3

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Distinguish general academic vocabulary (words recurring across
   every academic discipline) from discipline-specific technical
   jargon (words belonging to one field only), using a cross-subject-
   recurrence test.
2. Recognize when a common word's meaning shifts between its everyday,
   casual sense and its more precise academic sense.
3. Identify all general academic vocabulary present in an unfamiliar
   academic passage, distinguishing it from any technical jargon.
4. Produce original writing deliberately using academic vocabulary
   words in their correct academic sense.
5. Fully apply the concept to a new academic passage from an unfamiliar
   subject — identifying academic vocabulary, confirming academic
   senses, and writing a short response using several identified words
   correctly.

## Core Understanding

General academic vocabulary consists of discipline-neutral words
(analyze, significant, factor, concept, method, evidence, interpret,
context) that recur constantly across academic texts in every subject
— biology, history, and literature essays all naturally use
"significant," while a word like "mitochondria" belongs only to
biology. The reliable test for genuine academic vocabulary is
cross-subject recurrence: would this word plausibly appear in an essay
about a completely different subject too? If yes, it is general
academic vocabulary (the screwdriver, useful in nearly every workshop);
if a word only makes sense within one specific field, it is technical
jargon for that field (the soldering iron, useful only in electronics),
not general academic vocabulary — conflating the two categories misses
a genuinely useful, bounded, learnable word set by burying it inside a
much larger, unbounded list of every unfamiliar term a text contains. A
second, equally load-bearing fact is that academic writing often
narrows or sharpens a common word's meaning into a more precise sense
than its everyday, casual meaning suggests: "argument" in casual speech
means a disagreement or fight, but in academic writing it means a
reasoned case supported by evidence; "significant" casually means
"important" or "big," but in a research context it implies a specific,
measured, non-random effect. Assuming an academic word means exactly
what it means casually is like assuming a "trial" in a science class
means the same thing as a courtroom "trial" — related, but not
identical, and confusing the two senses can create real imprecision in
academic writing.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "any unfamiliar or complex-sounding word in an
  academic text counts as 'academic vocabulary'"**: the arriving,
  pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct — a natural
  reaction to encountering unfamiliar terminology, since both general
  academic words and subject-specific jargon feel equally "advanced" or
  "hard" the first time they're met, with nothing marking the
  cross-subject-recurrence distinction until directly tested.
- **Intermediate model — "I can apply the cross-subject-recurrence test
  to sort general academic vocabulary from technical jargon"**:
  installed by TA-1's sorting practice, using the Component 3
  screwdriver-vs-soldering-iron anchor to make the distinction concrete.
  Upgrade trigger: correctly sorting a genuinely mixed, unfamiliar
  passage's vocabulary into the two categories without defaulting to
  "everything unfamiliar counts."
- **Advanced model — "common words can carry a sharper, more precise
  academic sense than their everyday meaning"**: installed by TA-2's
  academic-sense-vs-everyday-sense practice, resolving MC-B-ACADEMIC-
  WORDS-MEAN-THE-SAME-THING-IN-ACADEMIC-WRITING-AS-THEY-DO-IN-EVERYDAY-
  CASUAL-SPEECH through the felt contrast between "argument" as a fight
  and "argument" as a reasoned case. Upgrade trigger: correctly
  identifying which of two senses a familiar word carries in a fresh
  academic sentence, without being told the word has multiple senses.
- **Expert model — "I can recognize academic vocabulary in an
  unfamiliar passage and productively USE it correctly in my own
  academic writing"**: installed by TA-3's in-context recognition, TA-4's
  productive-use practice, and TA-5's full reading-and-writing
  synthesis. This is the concept's genuine mastery bar — moving from
  passive sorting and sense-recognition to active, correct academic
  writing.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, a
  learner whose sorting is strong on provided passages (MP-1) but who
  reverts to listing technical jargon as "academic vocabulary" in a
  genuinely new, unfamiliar subject (MP-3) needs a MANDATORY cross-
  subject-recurrence check applied to every candidate word until the
  habit becomes automatic — this pattern indicates the sorting skill
  hasn't yet generalized beyond familiar practice material.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of how unfamiliar vocabulary is typically first encountered.
The jargon-conflation failure exists because both general academic
words and subject-specific technical terms genuinely share the surface
feature of feeling unfamiliar, advanced, or "academic-sounding" on
first encounter — nothing in the initial experience of meeting either
category signals the cross-subject-recurrence distinction, since a
learner reading a single passage from a single subject has no natural
occasion to check whether a word would ALSO appear in a completely
different subject's writing, until directly prompted to compare across
subjects. The everyday-sense-only failure exists because the vast
majority of a learner's exposure to common words like "argument,"
"significant," and "evidence" comes from casual, everyday contexts,
where the everyday sense is entirely correct and sufficient — nothing
in that ordinary exposure signals that academic writing narrows or
sharpens these same words into a more precise technical sense, until
the learner directly encounters an academic context where the everyday
sense produces genuine imprecision or confusion.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the screwdriver-vs-soldering-iron workshop anchor
  itself is the analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint
  Component 3): a screwdriver used across nearly every workshop
  (electronics, carpentry, plumbing) versus a soldering iron that
  belongs only in an electronics workshop directly embodies the
  cross-subject-recurrence test (MC-A's fix); paired with the
  courtroom-vs-classroom "trial" contrast, directly embodying the
  academic-sense-sharpening insight (MC-B's fix). Breaking point: the
  workshop-tool metaphor cleanly separates two DISTINCT categories of
  tool, which can understate that the boundary between "general
  academic" and "technical jargon" occasionally has genuinely borderline
  cases (some words are common in several but not all disciplines) —
  this entry treats the anchor as the introductory frame for the
  dominant, reliable pattern, not a claim that every word sorts
  unambiguously.
- **Alternative — a universal adapter plug vs. a country-specific
  appliance**: a universal travel adapter works in outlets across many
  countries (general academic vocabulary), while a specific appliance
  designed for one country's voltage standard only works there
  (technical jargon). Breaking point: useful for reinforcing the
  cross-context-recurrence principle, but the workshop-tool anchor more
  directly and concretely captures the specific TASK-utility framing
  (a tool used FOR something) that mirrors how academic vocabulary
  functions as tools for academic reasoning across subjects.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe technical jargon as "harder" or
  "more advanced" than general academic vocabulary**: framing technical
  jargon as inherently more sophisticated risks implying general
  academic vocabulary is somehow "easier" or less valuable — in fact,
  general academic vocabulary is arguably the MORE valuable, higher-
  leverage word set precisely because of its cross-subject
  applicability; the distinction is about SCOPE of use, not difficulty
  level.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the screwdriver-vs-soldering-iron and trial
  anchor)**: the Blueprint's Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced
  not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any two textbooks or articles from genuinely
  different subjects (e.g., a science article and a history essay)
  supply real TA-1-style cross-subject vocabulary comparison; the
  learner's own recent schoolwork across different subjects offers
  authentic material.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's original-writing task and
  TA-5's full reading-and-writing synthesis are both hands-on,
  requiring the learner to actively produce correct academic-sense
  usage, not merely recognize a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (academic-vs-everyday-sense contrast)**: the tutor
  models the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration —
  contrasting "they had an argument about the results" (a fight) with
  "the essay's argument is supported by three pieces of evidence" (a
  reasoned case) — making the sense-shift directly and memorably
  visible.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  jargon-vs-academic-vocabulary sort (TA-1) or an academic-sense
  identification (TA-2), ask the learner to state their own judgment
  before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
cross-subject-recurrence test and academic-sense-sharpening are both
genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured register/formality and word-
formation-processes, is asked whether "mitochondria" would appear in a
history essay the way "significant" would); **playground/invention**
(TA-1's cross-subject sorting across passages from multiple disciplines
lets the learner notice the recurring word set); **collision** (the
Blueprint's own mitochondria/significant contrast breaks the jargon-
conflation assumption; the argument-as-fight/argument-as-reasoned-case
contrast breaks the everyday-sense-only assumption); **formalization**
(MP-5's full reading-and-writing application, requiring explicit
identification and correct productive use); **compression** (TA-5's
fluent full synthesis without each word's status and sense separately
re-derived). There is no convention-bound terminology here requiring
separate direct instruction beyond the general concept of "academic
vocabulary" itself, best introduced once the learner has already felt
the underlying cross-subject-recurrence pattern through the anchor and
TA-1.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (general academic vocabulary vs. technical jargon) is placed
first, directly targeting MC-A, establishing the cross-subject-
recurrence test as the concept's foundational sorting skill before any
sense-precision complication is introduced. TA-2 (the academic sense
vs. the everyday sense) follows, directly targeting MC-B — per the
Blueprint's own protocol routing, these two misconceptions are "largely
independent and often need separate, explicit correction," so TA-2 is
sequenced as its own distinct instructional step rather than folded
into TA-1. TA-3 (recognizing academic vocabulary in context) extends
both TA-1's sorting skill and TA-2's sense-awareness to a genuinely
unfamiliar passage, requiring both skills operating together for the
first time. TA-4 (producing academic vocabulary in original writing)
shifts from receptive recognition to productive use, requiring the
learner to actively generate correct academic-sense sentences. TA-5
(full application: reading and writing) is placed last as the
concept's complete synthesis task, requiring cross-subject sorting
(TA-1/TA-3), academic-sense confirmation (TA-2), and productive writing
(TA-4) all applied together to a genuinely novel passage from an
unfamiliar subject. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own
turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References
below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously applies the
  cross-subject-recurrence test when meeting a fresh unfamiliar word
  (rather than defaulting to "unfamiliar = academic vocabulary") and
  spontaneously checks for a sharpened academic sense when using a
  familiar word academically — these are the primary diagnostic signals
  for whether TA-1 and TA-2's disciplines have genuinely generalized
  beyond the specific practice materials.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before classifying a
  borderline-feeling word (one common in several, but not all,
  disciplines), followed by a reasoned judgment, is a good sign — it
  suggests genuine engagement with the cross-subject test rather than a
  reflexive guess. Instant, confident classification of every complex-
  sounding word as "academic vocabulary" with no hesitation at all is
  the concerning pattern for MC-A.
- **Everyday-sense-misuse stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags — a learner whose sorting is strong
  (MP-1/MP-3) but whose own original writing (MP-5) misuses academic
  words in their everyday sense needs an explicit sense-check step ("is
  this the academic sense or the everyday sense?") required before
  finalizing each sentence, rather than a broader re-teach of sorting.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "would this word show up in an essay
  about a totally different subject?" is this concept's load-bearing
  sentence for MC-A, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh unfamiliar word is encountered, making the cross-
  subject test a routinely-invited first response rather than an
  unfamiliarity-triggered default classification.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a cross-subject-sorting task shrinks to the
  Component 3 screwdriver-vs-soldering-iron anchor using the simplest,
  most clear-cut contrast available (mitochondria vs. significant),
  with the tutor modeling the sort before the learner attempts a fresh
  passage independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for MC-A, listing every
  unfamiliar word in a passage as "academic vocabulary" is more likely
  than "I don't know" — per the Blueprint's own protocol routing, give
  passages from three different subjects and have the learner sort
  empirically into "appears across subjects" and "belongs to this
  subject only" columns rather than correcting each word in isolation.
  For MC-B, confidently applying only the everyday sense of a familiar
  word in academic writing is more likely than confusion — route
  directly to TA-2's academic-sense-vs-everyday-sense contrast practice.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously requires themselves to check "is this the academic
  sense or the everyday sense?" before finalizing a sentence in their
  own writing, without being prompted, is showing the sense-checking
  discipline has generalized — this should be named and praised
  explicitly.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework — a bounded vocabulary set (general
academic words recurring cross-subject) combined with a sense-precision
discipline (academic vs. everyday meaning), applied to genuinely novel
passages and productive writing; mastery is demonstrated by correctly
sorting and using unfamiliar words in unfamiliar academic contexts

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: sorting fresh unfamiliar words by cross-subject recurrence,
  identifying academic senses of familiar words, and producing original
  academic-register writing using them correctly.
- **Far**: per the Blueprint's own explicit Adaptive Flag, this
  concept's academic-sense-vs-everyday-sense discipline transfers
  directly to and from `eng.vocab.register-and-formality`'s formal/
  informal distinction, since both require recognizing that context
  shifts a word's appropriate meaning.
- **Real-world**: every piece of academic coursework the learner
  encounters, across every subject, offers immediate, ungated
  opportunities to apply both the cross-subject-recurrence test and the
  academic-sense discipline in real reading and writing.
- **Expert-transfer**: sophisticated, precise academic and professional
  writing at any advanced level depends substantially on fluent,
  correct use of this general academic vocabulary set, since these
  words are the connective, reasoning-oriented vocabulary underlying
  argumentation and analysis across every field of study.
