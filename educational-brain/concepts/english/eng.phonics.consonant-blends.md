# Consonant Blends — `eng.phonics.consonant-blends`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.phonics.consonant-blends` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / phonics / "Consonant Blends" — the
  first concept where single-consonant fluency is applied to genuine
  multi-consonant clusters, and the direct setup for distinguishing
  blends from digraphs one concept later.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.phonics.consonants` — specifically fluent,
  reliable production of the 18 core single consonant sounds; load-
  bearing because a blend is only meaningfully harder than two isolated
  sounds if each sound is already automatic on its own — a learner still
  effortful on single consonants has no spare capacity for the added
  motor-sequencing demand a blend introduces.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.phonics.digraphs`. This concept installs
  the "say it slowly, count the sounds" test used to detect a blend;
  digraphs are taught by the identical test producing the OPPOSITE
  result (one sound, not two) — the discrimination only makes sense
  once the learner has a confident, well-practiced sense of what "two
  separate sounds" actually feels and sounds like.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.80 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Count the number of distinct consonant sounds in an initial or final
   blend by saying it slowly, without collapsing or dropping any.
2. Blend an isolated, slowly-produced consonant cluster into its fluent,
   natural-speed form, retaining every consonant sound.
3. Read words containing final (word-ending) blends without dropping
   the second consonant sound, a site where natural speech commonly
   under-articulates.
4. Distinguish a blend (multiple sounds, each retained) from a digraph
   (one new merged sound) using the slow-sounding-out test.
5. Read genuinely unseen words containing both initial and final blends.

## Core Understanding

A consonant blend is two or three consonant sounds produced in
immediate sequence, with EVERY sound in the cluster fully and
separately articulated — nothing is merged, and nothing is dropped.
This is the load-bearing fact that distinguishes a blend from a digraph
(where two letters fuse into one new sound) and from an error (where a
learner drops a member of the cluster because producing consecutive
consonants is a genuinely harder motor-sequencing task than producing
a single consonant). The correct test for "is this a blend" is entirely
auditory and mechanical: say the letter combination slowly: if
multiple distinct, separately-identifiable sounds emerge, it is a
blend; if one new sound emerges that resembles neither letter alone, it
is a digraph (the concept immediately downstream). Word-final blends
are structurally identical to word-initial ones but behaviorally
riskier, because natural fluent speech frequently under-articulates a
word's final consonant, making blend-dropping easy to acquire as a
habit there specifically.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "I can count how many separate sounds are at the
  start of a blend word"**: installed by TA-1's sound-counting task,
  building directly on the single-consonant fluency from the
  prerequisite. Purely a recognition/counting skill, not yet production.
- **Intermediate model — "I can say a blend slowly, then speed it up,
  keeping every sound"**: the correct, operational production model,
  installed by TA-2's slow-to-fast technique (the Component 3 two-beat
  clap anchor). Upgrade trigger: fluent production of an initial blend
  at natural speed with no dropped sound, verified by the tutor listening
  specifically for both consonants.
- **Advanced model — "blends occur at the END of words too, and endings
  are where dropping happens most"**: installed by TA-4's explicit
  extension to final blends. This is a genuinely separate skill from
  initial-blend fluency, not an automatic transfer — natural speech
  patterns make final-consonant under-articulation common even for
  fluent speakers, so a learner secure on initial blends cannot be
  assumed secure on final blends without direct practice.
- **Expert model — "blends and digraphs are different categories, told
  apart by the same slow-sounding-out test"**: installed by TA-3,
  positioned deliberately mid-arc (after initial-blend fluency, before
  final-blend work) because it requires the sound-counting skill to
  already be reliable enough to serve as a genuine discrimination TEST
  rather than a guess.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner not yet reliable on TA-1's initial-
  blend sound-counting should not be given TA-4's final blends — final
  blends are harder (more prone to natural under-articulation), and
  presenting them before the counting skill is secure conflates two
  independent sources of difficulty.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here. The dropped-sound
failure exists because producing two or three consonants in immediate
succession is a genuinely harder articulatory-motor task than producing
one — this is not a conceptual misunderstanding but a coordination
demand, and the easiest way to reduce that demand (consciously or not)
is to drop or merge one of the sounds, which is exactly what natural,
fast, unmonitored speech tends to do at word endings in particular. The
blend/digraph confusion exists because both categories present
identically in print — two consonant letters adjacent to each other —
with no visual cue distinguishing which behavior applies; a learner who
has just been taught that adjacent consonant letters keep both their
sounds (from this very concept) reasonably extends that expectation to
the next two-letter combination encountered, which happens to be a
digraph that behaves oppositely.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.phonics.consonant-blends.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-BLEND-IS-ONE-FUSED-SOUND (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 2,
perceptual intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: this is a genuine perceptual/motor
  difficulty rather than a conceptually mistaken rule — the learner is
  not applying a wrong belief about how blends work, they are struggling
  with the physical coordination demand of producing consecutive
  consonants, which is a real articulatory challenge independent of any
  taught rule. This distinguishes it from Type 1/Type 5 misconceptions,
  which are about wrongly-generalized RULES rather than raw motor
  difficulty.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who reads "stop" as
  "top" but, when asked to say it slowly, CAN produce both /s/ and /t/
  separately is showing a fluency/speed-pressure issue (the sounds are
  known but drop under natural pace), which needs different treatment
  (more slow-to-fast practice) than a learner who cannot produce both
  sounds even when asked to go slowly (which suggests the prerequisite,
  single-consonant fluency, is not yet fully secure).

**MC-BLENDS-AND-DIGRAPHS-ARE-THE-SAME-THING (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: at the point this misconception is
  possible, the learner has only ever met two-letter consonant
  combinations of the "blend" type (this concept is the first exposure)
  — Type 1's signature: a rule ("two consonant letters together = two
  sounds") that is entirely correct within its only-known domain so far,
  extended forward onto the next two-letter combination the learner
  meets (a digraph), which happens to violate it.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: this specific misconception cannot
  fully manifest until `eng.phonics.digraphs` is reached — TA-3's own
  role here is pre-emptive rehearsal of the discrimination TEST (slow-
  sounding-out) before the learner has yet met a genuine digraph, so
  that when digraphs are introduced downstream, the learner already has
  a working test to apply rather than meeting the distinction cold.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — a two-person relay handoff, each runner still fully
  runs their own leg**: in a blend, each consonant sound "runs its own
  leg" in full, one immediately after another, with no leg skipped.
  Breaking point: a relay has a visible baton-pass moment; a fluent
  blend has no perceptible gap between sounds at all — the analogy
  should not be extended to suggest an audible pause belongs between
  blend sounds.
- **Alternative — two drumbeats close together vs. one drumbeat with a
  different tone**: a blend is two beats, close together but still two;
  a digraph (foreshadowing the next concept) is one beat with a
  different, blended tone. Breaking point: consonant sounds are not
  uniform "beats" — some (continuants like /s/, /f/) can be stretched,
  while others (stops like /t/, /k/) cannot, so this analogy should not
  imply all blend members can be equally elongated during slow practice.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT frame a blend as "letters that team up to make
  a new combo sound"**: "team up to make a combo sound" is precisely how
  a digraph should later be described, not a blend — using this framing
  here would directly install MC-BLENDS-AND-DIGRAPHS-ARE-THE-SAME-THING
  in advance, by pre-teaching the digraph description under the blend
  label. Blends must be framed as "each sound stays itself, just said
  quickly in a row."

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/auditory (two-beat clap)**: the Blueprint's Component 3
  anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: clapping requires nothing beyond the learner's
  own hands; the slow-to-fast technique can be rehearsed anywhere.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-3's blend-vs-digraph sort is a
  hands-on categorization task using a mixed word/letter-combination
  set.
- **Teacher-demo (dropped-sound contrast)**: the tutor models "stop"
  correctly (both /s/ and /t/ audible) immediately next to a
  deliberately dropped version ("top"), making the missing-sound error
  audible and concrete rather than only described abstractly.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  sound-count (TA-1) or a blend/digraph sort (TA-3), ask the learner to
  commit to an answer first — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**Direct instruction wins here — argued, not defaulted to.** Consonant
blends are not a regularity to be induced; they are a motor-articulatory
skill (producing consecutive consonant sounds without dropping any) to
be demonstrated and rehearsed, structurally identical in this respect
to `eng.phonics.blending-segmenting`'s own argued case for direct
instruction. There is no puzzle whose solution IS "say both sounds" —
the slow-to-fast technique must be modeled by the tutor before a learner
can attempt it, since the target behavior (retaining both sounds at
speed) is not something a learner can arrive at merely by noticing a
pattern in examples. The appropriate design is demonstrate (Component 3
clap) → guided slow production → guided fast production → independent
application to novel words — exactly the Blueprint's TA-1 through TA-5
arc.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (sound-counting) must precede TA-2 (slow-to-fast blending) because
recognizing that a cluster contains multiple sounds is a strictly easier
task than producing all of them fluently — a learner cannot be expected
to practice retaining sounds they cannot yet reliably count. TA-3
(blend-vs-digraph re-discrimination) is placed AFTER initial-blend
fluency (TA-1/TA-2) but BEFORE final blends (TA-4), because the
discrimination test itself depends on the sound-counting skill being
reliable, and placing it before digraphs are formally introduced
(downstream) gives the learner a working test in hand rather than
meeting the distinction reactively. TA-4 (final blends) is placed after
the discrimination step because final-position blend-dropping is the
single highest-risk error site (per Why Students Fail above) and
deserves dedicated, explicit practice rather than an assumed transfer
from initial-blend success. TA-5 (novel word reading) is placed last as
the integration and generalization check, requiring initial-blend
fluency, final-blend fluency, and the discrimination test all operating
together. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn
session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether BOTH (or all) consonant sounds in a
  blend are audible in the learner's production, especially at natural
  speed — this is the single most diagnostic thing to listen for, since
  a dropped sound is often the ONLY symptom of MC-BLEND-IS-ONE-FUSED-
  SOUND, with no other error visible.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a deliberate, effortful slowing on a
  final blend (rather than an initial one) is a good sign — it suggests
  the learner is consciously fighting the natural under-articulation
  tendency at word endings, exactly the skill TA-4 targets.
- **Pronunciation stakes**: real and central — a dropped consonant in a
  blend changes the word's identity (stop/top are different words), so
  this is a place where correction matters, not a dialect-tolerant
  variation; however, genuinely dialect-consistent realizations of the
  individual consonant sounds themselves (not their presence/absence)
  should never be marked wrong.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "say it slowly first — do you hear
  every sound?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence and should be
  used consistently across both initial- and final-blend work, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3`.

## Assessment Signals

- **Diagnostic (golden probe)**: the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence
  probe (Component 1) — saying "stop" slowly and asking whether one or
  two sounds are heard at the start — cleanly separates a learner who
  has not yet internalized the blend concept from one who has, before
  deciding how much time TA-1/TA-2 need.
- **Guided practice → independent practice**: the Blueprint's WE-1 → WE-2
  → WE-3 → MP-1 through MP-5 fading ladder is reused directly
  (Components 5–6) — not re-authored here.
- **Mastery gate set** (`../../assessment/05-mastery-verification.md
  §3`): production (fresh initial-blend sound-count and blending,
  MP-1/implicit); fluent-reading (a novel word with BOTH an initial and
  final blend, retaining every sound, MP-2 — the strongest single test
  of this concept's core skill); discrimination (novel blend-vs-digraph
  pair, MP-3); final-position (a fresh final-blend word, MP-4);
  explanation (MP-5).
- **Interpretation of response pattern**: fast-and-correct fluent
  reading of a blend word with no dropped sounds confirms automaticity;
  slow-and-correct (deliberate, effortful sounding-out that still
  retains every sound) is an acceptable, even expected, intermediate
  state and should not be treated as a problem. Fast-and-WRONG (rapid
  reading with a dropped sound) is the dangerous quadrant here — it
  signals the dropping has become habitual and automatic, which is
  harder to unlearn than a slow, effortful but complete production, and
  warrants returning to the Component 3 clap anchor rather than simple
  re-practice at speed.
- **Transfer items**: near (a fresh initial-blend word); far (a
  three-consonant blend, e.g. "spring" or "strap," testing whether the
  sound-retention skill generalizes beyond the two-consonant clusters
  most heavily rehearsed); real-world (any blend word already present in
  text the learner is reading elsewhere).

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a blend-reading task shrinks to the Component 3
  clap anchor with the most familiar, most-rehearsed blend available
  (typically the very first one taught), performed slowly with the
  tutor modeling first, rather than asking the learner to attempt a
  fresh blend cold.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: a confident but
  incomplete reading (dropping a sound without noticing) is more likely
  than an outright "I don't know," per MC-BLEND-IS-ONE-FUSED-SOUND's
  mechanism — when this pattern appears, route directly to that
  misconception's recovery (slow the word down, exaggerate both sounds,
  per the Blueprint's own S6 protocol routing) rather than treating it
  as a generic reading error.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: an S9 learner (L1 with
  restrictive phonotactics) inserting an extra vowel sound between
  cluster members ("sitop" for "stop") is showing a predictable
  transfer effect, per the Blueprint's own Component 8 flag — this
  should be treated as a specific, expected pattern requiring targeted
  practice on that exact cluster, not as evidence of confusion about the
  blend concept itself.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedure — a production-fluency skill (retaining all
sounds in a cluster at natural speed), following a skill-fluency pattern:
correctness first (all sounds present, even slowly), then speed, with
particular attention to final-position blends as a distinct, ongoing
review target since they are the highest-risk site for regression even
after initial mastery. Interleaving partners: mix initial- and final-
blend items within the same practice set, and mix blend items with
digraph items once `eng.phonics.digraphs` is reached, specifically to
keep the discrimination test itself in active use rather than allowing
it to be forgotten once initially learned.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: reading fresh, unpracticed words containing initial or
  final consonant blends.
- **Far**: the slow-sounding-out discrimination test transfers directly
  to `eng.phonics.digraphs`, where the identical test is applied to
  produce the opposite classification — this concept's TA-3 is
  deliberate advance rehearsal for that transfer.
- **Real-world**: any blend word encountered in the learner's own
  reading, especially final-blend words (common in everyday vocabulary:
  "jump," "hand," "desk," "list").
- **Expert-transfer**: fluent, automatic multi-consonant articulation
  during ordinary reading and speech, well past this node.
