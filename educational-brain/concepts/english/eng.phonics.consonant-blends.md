## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Count and produce the correct number of distinct consonant sounds at the start or end of a blend-containing word, without dropping or merging any.
2. Produce a blend by starting slow (each sound isolated) and progressively speeding up without losing any member sound.
3. Distinguish a consonant blend (multiple sounds, each pronounced) from a digraph (two letters, one new sound), using the slow-sounding-out test.
4. Correctly produce word-final blends, which are more prone to sound-dropping in natural speech than initial blends.
5. Read a novel word containing an initial or final blend, applying sound-counting and slow-to-fast blending independently.

## Core Understanding

A **consonant blend** (or cluster) is two or three adjacent consonant letters representing two or three **distinct, separately-pronounced sounds in sequence** — "st" in "stop" is /s/ followed by /t/, both fully articulated, not merged into anything new. This is the concept's single defining fact, and it is precisely what distinguishes a blend from a **digraph** (two letters representing one new, single sound, as "sh" represents /ʃ/, not /s/ followed by /h/) — the two categories look identical on the page (both are two consonant letters written adjacently) and behave in opposite ways when spoken, which is why confusing them is the expected default rather than an unusual error. The second structural fact is that producing a genuine consonant cluster is a **harder motor task than producing a single consonant**, because it requires the articulators to move rapidly from one specific configuration to another with no vowel in between to provide a natural transition — this genuine physical difficulty is the direct cause of the most common blend error, dropping or merging one member of the cluster (reading "stop" as "top," or "black" as "bak"), and it is why this concept's core teaching technique is explicitly **slow-to-fast production**: isolating each sound at a comfortable pace first, then progressively accelerating without ever allowing a sound to disappear along the way. The third structural fact is that blends occur in **both initial and final position** (stop, black — initial; desk, jump — final), and final blends are systematically **more vulnerable to sound-dropping** than initial ones, because natural connected speech tends to under-articulate word-final consonants generally, making final-blend practice a genuinely separate and necessary focus rather than an assumption that initial-blend mastery transfers automatically.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "a blend is basically its own single sound, like a new letter-combination sound."**
The runnable simulation: encountering two consonant letters together, retrieve or approximate a single combined sound rather than two sequential ones. This model is not tutor-installed — it is a reasonable extension of the digraph pattern the learner may have already partially encountered informally (two letters, one sound), applied to the wrong category.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — saying "stop" very slowly and stretching the beginning, discovering two genuinely separate consonant sounds rather than one.
*Shelf-life warning at replacement*: "Some two-letter combinations really are one new sound — those are digraphs. Blends keep both sounds, said one right after the other."

**Intermediate — "I can hear that a blend has two sounds when I slow down, but at normal speed I sometimes drop one."**
The simulation: correctly perceive and produce both sounds of a blend when deliberately slowed down, but under normal reading or speaking speed, one member (typically the harder-to-coordinate one) disappears. This is a genuine motor-coordination gap, not a conceptual one — the learner knows both sounds belong and cannot yet reliably produce both at speed.
*Upgrade trigger*: the slow-to-fast progression itself (TA-2), explicitly bridging from the secure slow production to a fluent fast one without ever losing a sound along the way.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Slowing down and speeding back up, carefully, without losing a sound, is the actual skill — it's not cheating to go slow first."

**Advanced — "a blend is two or three sequentially-produced, individually-articulated consonant sounds, distinguishable from a digraph by the slow-sounding-out test, and equally present (and equally vulnerable to dropping) in both initial and final position."**
The simulation: given any novel word, the learner applies sound-counting and the slow-to-fast technique to any blend encountered, in either position, and correctly sorts any ambiguous two-letter combination into blend or digraph using the slow test rather than guessing from familiarity. This is the target model, matching TA-4 and TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: three-consonant blends (str, spl, scr) and blends in less familiar or longer words, where the motor-coordination demand scales up further.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Three sounds in a row is harder still than two — the same slow-to-fast method works, it just takes more patience."

**Expert — "consonant clusters are governed by a language's phonotactic constraints — which sequences are permitted at all, and in which positions — and English permits a specific, learnable, bounded set at each word edge, distinct from many other languages' cluster inventories."**
Named here to mark the arc; this is the systematic account of exactly why the Blueprint's S9 protocol routing anticipates such specific, predictable difficulty for learners whose L1 phonotactics differ, and it belongs to a more advanced study of phonology than this concept's foundational scope.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant failure mechanism is **genuine articulatory difficulty, not conceptual confusion** — producing two consonant sounds in immediate sequence, with no vowel to provide a natural transitional gesture between them, is a harder coordinated motor task than producing either sound alone, and the single most common resulting error (dropping one member of the cluster) is a direct, expected consequence of that difficulty rather than evidence the learner has not understood what a blend is. The Blueprint's own S6 protocol routing correctly validates this explicitly, and failing to do so risks the learner concluding the error reflects a comprehension gap when it in fact reflects a motor-coordination one requiring a different remedy (slowing down, not more explanation).

The second mechanism is the now-familiar risk of two visually-identical categories requiring opposite phonetic strategies — blends and digraphs are, on the page, indistinguishable (both are simply two adjacent consonant letters), and nothing about a word's spelling signals which strategy (keep both sounds separate, or merge into one new sound) applies until the word is actually sounded out. A learner who has successfully learned one category's strategy may default to applying it to the other, particularly under the pressure of an unfamiliar word where neither strategy has yet been confirmed.

The third mechanism, specific to the final-blend case, is that **natural, connected, everyday speech genuinely under-articulates word-final consonants** more than word-initial ones — even fluent adult speakers often produce a final blend with the second member weakly released or nearly inaudible in casual speech, meaning the "correct," fully-articulated target this concept teaches is, in a real sense, more careful and deliberate than a learner's ordinary ambient speech input, which can make final-blend practice feel unnatural or overly precise compared to what the learner has actually heard modelled around them.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns the trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-BLEND-IS-ONE-FUSED-SOUND
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-BLEND-IS-ONE-FUSED-SOUND — includes the stop/top and black/"sh" discrimination pairs.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the digraph pattern onto the structurally different blend category — a reasonable extension of correct prior knowledge (some two-letter consonant combinations really do merge into one sound) to a category where it does not apply.
- **Teaching consequence of the birth type**: because the source pattern (digraphs genuinely merge) is correct elsewhere, the repair must **scope the merging behaviour precisely to digraphs**, exactly as at `eng.phonics.consonants`' hard/soft c/g repair — never framed as "letters never merge," only as "these specific two-letter groups merge; these others don't, and here's the test to tell which."
- **Verification of death**: given a novel blend-containing word, the learner produces both member sounds at normal reading speed, with no dropping, and can explicitly confirm this by slowing down on request and confirming two distinct sounds remain present — the *ability to slow down and verify* is stronger evidence than a single fast, correct-sounding production alone, which could coincidentally sound acceptable even with subtle under-articulation.

### MC-BLENDS-AND-DIGRAPHS-ARE-THE-SAME-THING
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-BLENDS-AND-DIGRAPHS-ARE-THE-SAME-THING — includes the bl/black-vs-ch/chip and sk/skip-vs-th/thin discrimination pairs.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), reinforced by a genuine visual fact — the two categories really are visually indistinguishable (both are two adjacent consonant letters), so the learner's evidence (they look the same) is accurate; only the conclusion (therefore they behave the same) is wrong.
- **Teaching consequence**: because the visual evidence genuinely cannot discriminate the categories, the repair requires an **auditory, not visual, test** applied as a standing habit — the slow-sounding-out test, run on every new two-consonant-letter combination the learner meets, rather than an attempt to memorise which specific letter pairs belong to which category (a strategy that would not generalise to novel or rare combinations).
- **Verification of death**: given a genuinely novel two-consonant-letter combination not previously drilled as either a blend or a digraph, the learner correctly applies the slow-sounding-out test and classifies it without first attempting to recall whether it "looks like" a familiar blend or digraph.

## Analogies

**Best — a fast handshake versus a single high-five.** A handshake, even done quickly, is still two distinct motions (grip, then release) happening in fast sequence — that's a blend. A high-five is one single motion from start to finish — that's a digraph. Speeding up the handshake doesn't turn it into a high-five; both actions retain their essential two-part or one-part structure regardless of speed.
*Breaking point*: physical gestures don't have an equivalent to the auditory slow-sounding-out test as a *diagnostic tool* — useful for grasping the two-versus-one structural distinction, not for teaching the verification method itself.

**Alternative — two runners in a relay race versus one runner sprinting the whole distance alone.** A relay involves a genuine handoff between two distinct participants (two sounds, a clear boundary between them); a solo sprint is one continuous effort by one participant (one sound, no internal boundary). Speeding up either doesn't change how many runners are involved.
*Breaking point*: relay handoffs have a discrete, visible moment of transfer; consonant-to-consonant transitions in a blend are acoustically continuous and don't have quite that same crisp, visible boundary — useful for the concept, not for over-literal handoff-timing claims.

**Story analogy — saying two people's names back-to-back versus one compound name.** "Sam-Tom" said quickly is still recognisably two separate names in sequence (a blend); "Samtom" as a single invented compound name is one unit (a digraph). This captures both the sequential-but-distinct nature of a blend and the fact that speed alone doesn't erase the internal structure.
*Breaking point*: none serious; a reasonably close mapping to the sequential-identity-preserved property blends have and digraphs don't.

**Visual analogy — the two-beat clap** (Blueprint Component 3): one clap per consonant sound, slow at first, then sped up while the two claps (and their corresponding sounds) remain distinct and countable. Not a metaphor but the actual physical instrument this concept's core teaching technique — slow-to-fast production — is organised around.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"A blend is when two letters blend together into one sound."** The word "blend" itself, used this way, directly installs the exact misconception this concept exists to prevent — "blend" in the technical phonics sense means "sequential, both retained," not "merged into one," and using the everyday sense of the word as an explanation is self-defeating.
- **"If you can't tell whether it's a blend or a digraph, just guess based on which letters they are."** Directly licenses memorisation over the slow-sounding-out test, and will fail on any letter combination not already memorised, precisely the generalisation failure MC-BLENDS-AND-DIGRAPHS-ARE-THE-SAME-THING's repair is designed to prevent.
- **"Final blends are basically the same as initial ones, just at the end."** Understates the genuine, separate difficulty of final-blend production (under-articulation in natural connected speech) and risks skipping the dedicated TA-4 practice the Blueprint identifies as necessary specifically because initial-blend mastery does not automatically transfer to final position.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The two-beat clap (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*, before speeding up: "Do you think both claps will still be there when we go fast, or might one disappear?"
2. **The stop/top collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence. *Predict first*: "Say 'stop' very slowly — how many separate sounds do you think are at the start?" Then stretch it and count together.
3. **The blend-vs-digraph slow test (learner activity, TA-3).** *Predict first*, for each new pair before testing: "Do you think this one will turn out to be two sounds or one, once we slow it down?"
4. **The final-blend under-articulation reveal (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** *Predict first*: "Say 'desk' the way you normally would in a fast sentence — do you think both ending sounds come out clearly, or might one get swallowed?" Then compare a careful, deliberate production against a fast, casual one.

## Discovery Questions

The **blend/digraph distinction itself is well suited to guided discovery**, since the diagnostic test (slow it down, count the sounds) is directly available to the learner with no prior theory needed, and discovering the test's reliability firsthand is more durable than being told the rule.

1. **Need**: "Some two-consonant-letter combinations are really two sounds, and some are really just one new sound. How could you tell which is which, just by listening?"
2. **Playground**: the learner slowly sounds out a handful of familiar two-consonant combinations (bl, sh, st, ch) freely, without being told in advance which category each belongs to.
3. **Invention**: "Which ones had two separate sounds you could count, and which ones turned into just one sound?" The learner sorts their own examples into two piles based on their own listening.
4. **Collision**: introduce a combination the learner has not yet sorted (sp or th, whichever wasn't in the initial set) and ask them to apply their own just-invented test rather than guess from familiarity.
5. **Formalization**: name the two categories — blend (multiple sounds, all kept) and digraph (one new sound) — and name the test that distinguishes them (slow it down and count).
6. **Compression**: "Slow it down. Count the sounds. One means digraph, two or more means blend."

The specific inventory of which letter combinations are blends versus digraphs in English is a fixed, conventional fact, surveyed directly (TA-1, TA-2) rather than derived — there is no discoverable *reason* "bl" happens to be a blend and "sh" a digraph; only the *test* for telling them apart, once presented, is discoverable.

## Teaching Sequence

The pedagogical logic behind this arc:

- **Sound-counting on already-secure blend words (TA-1) comes before slow-to-fast production practice (TA-2)** — establishing that the learner can correctly *count* the sounds present, purely as a perceptual/analytical task, before asking them to reliably *produce* all of those sounds at increasing speed, separates the perceptual skill from the motor-production skill and allows either to be diagnosed independently if difficulty arises.
- **Blend/digraph sorting (TA-3) is introduced only after the learner has direct, felt experience with genuine blends (TA-1, TA-2)** — attempting the sorting task before the learner has a secure, embodied sense of what "two sounds kept separate" actually feels and sounds like would leave them with no genuine perceptual basis for the discrimination, forcing a guess rather than a test.
- **Final blends (TA-4) are deliberately sequenced as their own dedicated stage, after initial-blend fluency, not assumed to follow automatically** — this is the Blueprint's own explicit design choice (Component 8's "final blends are a common regression site" flag) and directly reflects the genuine, separate difficulty established under Why Students Fail; skipping this stage on the assumption that initial-position skill transfers would leave a specific, predictable, and separately-addressable gap unaddressed.
- **Novel-word reading (TA-5) comes last and explicitly includes both initial and final blend positions**, testing generalisation of both the sound-counting and slow-to-fast techniques independent of any specific drilled word list, matching the generalisation-testing principle already established throughout this batch.

Turn-by-turn scripting, protocol routing, and adaptive flags: Blueprint **Component 7 — Session Architecture** and **Component 8 — Adaptive Flags** (in particular the slow-to-fast-as-core-technique framing, the final-blend-regression-risk flag, the default-assumption-of-blend/digraph-confusion flag, and L1 phonotactic-cluster transfer difficulty for S9 learners).

## Tutor Actions

In recommended order, from `../teaching-actions/`:

1. **Demonstration** (SHOW) — the two-beat clap and the stop/top and desk collisions, all benefiting from being heard aloud, with the clap technique ideally learner-echoed and learner-repeated at progressively increasing speed rather than only tutor-modelled once.
2. **Prediction** (TEST-THINKING) — predicting sound count before slowing a word down, and predicting blend-versus-digraph status before applying the slow test, both directly building the target discrimination skills.
3. **Matching** (DO) — sound-count to word, and blend/digraph classification to letter combination, with the bidirectional check (does the learner's classification hold up when re-tested on the same item later, at speed, without the deliberate slow-down step) carrying real diagnostic value for whether the skill has generalised beyond the analytical task.
4. **Error Analysis** (TEST-THINKING) — presenting a word read with one blend member dropped (a puppet's or peer's hypothetical error, not the learner's own live one) and asking the learner to identify what's missing, directly practising the discrimination MC-BLEND-IS-ONE-FUSED-SOUND's repair targets.
5. **Concept Map** (ORGANIZE) — a simple two-column sort (blend / digraph) built from a mixed set the learner classifies using the slow test, giving a compact artefact distinguishing the two categories.

**Does not fit**: **Worked Example** in the derivation sense — there is no multi-step calculation, only a perceptual-and-motor skill better served by Demonstration and Prediction. **Game** at full speed before the underlying skill is secure — a speed-focused blend-reading game risks rewarding exactly the sound-dropping shortcut this concept's first misconception consists of, and should be reserved for after slow-to-fast fluency is independently confirmed.

## Voice Teaching Notes

This concept's core evidence is **audio-and-timing**, closely paralleling `eng.phonics.blending-segmenting` earlier in this batch: the critical diagnostic difference between correct blend production (two distinct, sequential articulatory gestures) and the primary error (one gesture, one member dropped) is prosodic and timing-based, and is exactly the kind of signal `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7` identifies as discarded by the runtime's current plain-text STT capture — a transcription-only system that receives "top" cannot distinguish a genuine word "top" from a dropped-sound attempt at "stop" without the missing acoustic detail.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A single, clean consonant sound at the start or end of a blend-containing word, with no trace of the second member.** The direct auditory signature of MC-BLEND-IS-ONE-FUSED-SOUND in its production form (as distinct from the classification-confusion form) — most detectable specifically because the resulting sound is a genuinely different, real word ("top" for "stop"), which can mask the error if the tutor is only checking for a plausible-sounding response rather than the intended word.
- **Two audibly distinct articulatory gestures at reduced speed, collapsing to one at normal reading speed on the same word.** This is the direct signature of the motor-coordination gap (Why Students Fail, mechanism one) rather than a conceptual error, and should route to more slow-to-fast practice specifically, not to more explanation of what a blend is.
- **A weakly-released or barely-audible second member specifically in word-final position**, while the same learner reliably produces both members in initial position. This differential pattern is the direct signature of the final-blend-specific difficulty this concept's TA-4 exists to address, and should not be conflated with a general blend-production weakness if initial blends are independently secure.
- **Audible self-correction after a dropped sound** ("top — no, stop!"). Strongly positive: evidence the learner has an active self-monitoring check running, even if execution isn't yet fully automatic.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Say each sound on its own first, then speed them up — but never let one disappear."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads, which the Blueprint does not own:

- **Sound-counting (MP-1).** *Correct count, given at normal speed with no need to slow down first* → strong evidence of secure perceptual mastery. *Correct count, but only after the learner spontaneously slows the word down themselves* → still positive evidence, and the self-initiated slowing is itself a good sign of an active, appropriate strategy, not a deficiency.
- **Reading with correct articulation (MP-2).** This item explicitly requires retaining *both* the initial and final blend in the same word ("plant"), and is this concept's most demanding single item precisely because it tests both positions simultaneously — a learner who succeeds on the initial blend but drops the final one (or vice versa) has a real, specific, positionally-located gap that a single pass/fail score would obscure; always note *which* position failed, not merely that an error occurred.
- **Blend vs. digraph (MP-3).** *Correct, with the learner audibly or visibly applying the slow test before answering* → the target procedure is active. *Correct with no apparent test applied* → treat as unverified per this program's established practice; a correct answer here could reflect a lucky guess or recall of a specific drilled example rather than genuine application of the diagnostic test, and should be confirmed with a second, genuinely unfamiliar combination.
- **Final blend (MP-4).** Given the established asymmetric difficulty of final position, a correct answer here carries somewhat stronger evidentiary weight than an equivalent correct answer on an initial blend, and a learner who is fluent on initial blends but struggles specifically here should not be treated as failing the *concept* — they have a specific, well-understood, separately-addressable positional gap.
- **Explanation (MP-5).** Listen for whether the explanation names the *test* (say it slowly, count the sounds) rather than only the *definition* (a blend has multiple sounds) — a learner who can define but not test has a concept without yet having a reliable, generalisable procedure to apply it.

**Mastery certification trigger**: correct sound-counting on a novel word; correct, fully-articulated reading of a novel word containing both an initial and a final blend, with neither member dropped; correct blend-versus-digraph classification on a genuinely novel letter combination, with the slow test demonstrated or reliably inferable; and correct production specifically of a novel final blend, isolated from any initial-blend item, given the established positional asymmetry. The isolated final-blend requirement is essential — a learner who only ever demonstrates final-blend success embedded within a word that also has an easier initial blend has not had the harder position independently verified.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"I keep losing one of the sounds"** — take this as an accurate, self-aware report of the genuine motor-coordination difficulty this concept centres on, not as a sign of insufficient effort or understanding; the response should return immediately to the slow-to-fast technique on that specific word, explicitly reassuring that slowing down is the correct strategy, not a step backward: "That's the hardest part of blends for almost everyone — let's slow this one right back down and build it back up carefully."
- **"They look exactly the same, how am I supposed to know?"** — the characteristic protest when a blend/digraph classification is presented, and it should be validated directly rather than dismissed, since the visual identity really is total: "You're completely right that they look identical — that's exactly why we never use our eyes for this one, only our ears. Let's slow it down and listen."
- **The smaller question to shrink to**: from full-word reading or classification, down to **the two-beat clap on a single, maximally familiar blend, at the slowest possible speed, with no reading or classification demand at all** — pure sound-counting-and-production, isolated from any word-recognition or category-judgement task. This re-establishes that both sounds can be produced at all before returning to the harder, faster, or classification-based tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just say the first sound you're most confident about and skip the rest."** This directly licenses the sound-dropping error this concept exists to prevent, precisely at the moment the learner is most likely to adopt whatever strategy is modelled.

## Memory Hooks

- **Concept type**: procedural/motor skill (slow-to-fast blend production) plus a **discrimination skill** (blend vs. digraph, via the slow-test procedure). The two need distinct review, closely paralleling the treatment already established at `eng.phonics.blending-segmenting`.
- **Review form — blend production**: brief, frequent automaticity bursts specifically including final-position items in every review cycle, never only initial-position items, given the established positional asymmetry — a review schedule drawn only from initial blends would look like strong retention while leaving the harder, more error-prone final-blend skill unexercised and likely to regress unnoticed.
- **Review form — blend/digraph discrimination**: review using genuinely novel letter combinations on a rotating basis, never only the originally-drilled comparison set, mirroring the generalisation-testing principle established at every rule-application item in this batch.
- **Concept-specific deviation**: because this skill (like `eng.phonics.blending-segmenting`'s underlying operation) is directly and continuously re-exercised by ordinary decoding practice on any text containing blend words, dedicated stand-alone review can reasonably taper once initial fluency is established, provided the learner's actual reading material continues to include a healthy mix of initial- and final-blend words — a diet of exclusively simple CVC words post-acquisition would remove this natural reinforcement.
- **Interleaving partners**: `eng.phonics.digraphs`, once available, should be interleaved directly with blend items rather than taught and reviewed as a fully separate block, since the discrimination between the two categories (not merely fluency within either alone) is itself a standing skill this concept and its direct unlock jointly maintain.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: `eng.phonics.digraphs` — the direct unlock, and the relationship is close to a mirror image: this concept establishes when two adjacent consonant letters keep both sounds, and the unlock establishes the opposite case, with the shared slow-sounding-out test serving as the connecting procedure between them.
- **Far**: `eng.writing.spelling-strategies` — a learner who has secure sound-counting for blends can apply the same segmenting skill in reverse when spelling an unfamiliar word containing a cluster, correctly representing both consonant letters rather than dropping one, extending the blending-segmenting-to-spelling transfer already established earlier in this batch.
- **Real-world**: understanding why some learners of English as a second language, particularly from L1 backgrounds with more restrictive phonotactics, systematically insert an extra vowel into English consonant clusters (producing something like "sitop" for "stop") — a genuinely predictable, well-documented transfer pattern this concept's S9 routing directly anticipates, and naming it for a learner or their family reframes it as an expected linguistic phenomenon rather than a personal error.
- **Expert transfer**: the durable skill is **maintaining the integrity of a multi-part sequence under increasing performance speed, without allowing any part to be silently dropped** — the same skill required in fast piano fingering (not skipping notes as tempo increases), rapid multi-step mental arithmetic (not silently dropping a step), or any fluency-building task where speed and completeness are in genuine tension and must be reconciled through deliberate slow-to-fast practice rather than speed alone.
