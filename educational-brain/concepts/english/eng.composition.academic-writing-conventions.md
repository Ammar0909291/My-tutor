## Learning Objective
The learner will identify and revise overly casual, overly personal, or overconfident language toward objective academic prose, select hedging language that accurately reflects the actual strength of the evidence behind a claim, and explain why objectivity means grounding an argument in evidence transparently rather than having no argument at all.

## Core Understanding
Academic objectivity does not mean having no opinion — it means having a clear, evidence-grounded claim expressed with a confidence level that honestly matches how strong the supporting evidence actually is. This has two separate, independently-failable dimensions: (1) HAVING a specific claim rather than retreating into false balance ("there are many views on this"), and (2) CALIBRATING the language expressing that claim's certainty to the evidence's actual strength — confident language ("demonstrates," "establishes") for well-replicated findings, moderate hedging ("suggests," "indicates") for reasonably solid evidence, strong hedging ("may," "a preliminary study found") for weak or contested evidence. Hedging is a precision tool for representing evidence strength accurately to the reader, not a blanket softening habit applied uniformly (or omitted entirely) regardless of what the evidence actually supports.

## Mental Models
- **Judge's-Verdict Anchor**: a judge reaches a clear verdict — they don't refuse to decide just to seem "neutral" — but they base that verdict transparently on the evidence presented, not personal feelings. Academic writing works the same way: take a position, ground it in evidence, express it with appropriate certainty.
- **Volume-Dial Anchor**: hedging language is a volume dial, not a mute button — it should be set to match how confident the evidence actually allows the writer to be, not turned down uniformly on every statement regardless of evidence strength. A well-replicated finding deserves confident language; a preliminary study deserves more caution; these should sound different.

## Why Students Fail
Learners newly taught "be objective" often overcorrect into avoiding any clear position, mistaking neutrality-of-tone for absence-of-claim — because refusing to commit feels safer than being wrong. Separately, once taught to hedge at all, learners frequently apply hedging as a uniform stylistic habit rather than as a precision tool tied to the specific evidence behind each individual claim, because tracking evidence-strength-per-claim is a distinct, effortful step easy to skip.

## Misconceptions
- **MC-A (Type 1, overgeneralization from "objective = no bias = no position")**: "Academic objectivity means having no opinion or argument at all." Diagnostic: present a claim-avoiding passage ("There are many different views on climate policy") against an evidence-grounded, appropriately hedged claim, and ask which one is actually making an academic argument. Repair: judge's-verdict anchor — objectivity means (1) a clear claim, (2) grounded in evidence, (3) expressed with honest confidence — not the absence of a claim.
- **MC-B (Type 1, overgeneralization from "hedging = softening = safe")**: "Hedging language means being vague or weak about everything." Diagnostic: present three claims with genuinely different evidence strength, all written with identical uniform hedging, and ask whether this accurately represents their different evidence levels. Repair: volume-dial anchor — rank evidence strength FIRST, then assign hedging language matched precisely to that strength, not applied (or omitted) uniformly.

## Analogies
- Judge's-verdict anchor (above) for objectivity-as-grounded-claim.
- Volume-dial anchor (above) for calibrated, evidence-matched hedging.
- Anti-analogy: objectivity is not "sounding uncertain about everything" — a well-replicated finding should be stated confidently; over-hedging a strong finding is itself a miscalibration, not a safer default.

## Demonstrations
- Revise "I think social media is definitely bad for teenagers, everyone knows this" into a claim retaining specificity but calibrated differently for a well-supported finding (sleep disruption) versus a more contested one (broader mental health outcomes).
- Present three source claims with wildly different evidence strength (well-replicated, single preliminary study, contested theory) all written with identical uniform hedging; ask if this hides real differences from the reader.
- Identify the implicit position buried in a falsely "balanced" passage and make it explicit with appropriately calibrated hedging.

## Discovery Questions
- "Does this passage actually make a claim, or does it just describe that opinions exist?"
- "How strong is the evidence behind THIS specific statement — and does the hedging language match that strength?"
- "If I read only your hedging language, would I be able to tell which of your claims has the strongest evidence behind it?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Diagnostic: research-paper-writing synthesis check and basic register-shift check.
2. Anchor: judge's-verdict and volume-dial framing together.
3. TA-1: identify the implicit claim in "balanced" writing and make it explicit (targets MC-A).
4. TA-2: rank source claims by actual evidence strength (a distinct, prior step).
5. TA-3: assign calibrated hedging language to the ranked claims (targets MC-B).
6. TA-4: revise casual register (contractions, first-person overuse) while preserving claim and hedging.
7. TA-5: full revision — explicit claim, calibrated hedging, formal register, from a casual draft plus source materials.

## Tutor Actions
- SHOW: claim-avoiding vs. evidence-grounded-and-hedged passage pair; uniform-hedging vs. calibrated-hedging triple.
- DO: learner ranks source claims by evidence strength before assigning any hedging language.
- TEST-THINKING: predict which hedging phrase fits a given evidence-strength level.
- ORGANIZE: a claim→evidence-rank→hedge→register checklist as scaffolding for the full revision task.

## Voice Teaching Notes
MC-A often needs resolving before MC-B, since a learner with no clear claim has nothing yet to calibrate hedging around — sequence correction accordingly rather than addressing both simultaneously if both misfire at once.

## Assessment Signals
- Probe: "What is the actual claim in this 'balanced' passage?" A learner who cannot locate an implicit position (or insists there is none) signals MC-A.
- Probe: given four ranked claims, "assign hedging language to each." Identical hedging across claims of different evidence strength signals MC-B.
- Confident-wrong signature: a fluent, appropriately-hedged-SOUNDING passage where every claim uses the same hedge phrase regardless of underlying evidence — flag MC-B even when the prose reads smoothly.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If a learner overcorrects after MC-A into overstated, unhedged confidence (swinging from "no claim" to "absolute claim" with no calibration), address this as a distinct step — having a claim and calibrating its confidence are two separate skills that must both be present, not a single fix.

## Memory Hooks
"A judge decides, but cites the evidence" for objectivity-with-a-claim; "hedging is a volume dial, not a mute button" for calibrated (not uniform) hedging.

## Cross-Subject Connections
The evidence-strength-calibrated-hedging discipline mirrors how scientific writing distinguishes "demonstrates" (well-replicated) from "suggests" (preliminary) — the same precision-matching-confidence-to-evidence norm that governs how any empirical claim should be reported.
