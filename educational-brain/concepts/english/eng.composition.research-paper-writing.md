## Learning Objective
The learner will formulate an original, arguable thesis synthesizing multiple sources, organize a paper by IDEA rather than by source, weave sources together within a paragraph to support each point, and directly engage sources that complicate or disagree with their thesis rather than omitting them.

## Core Understanding
A research paper is the student's OWN synthesis of multiple sources around an original thesis — not a sequence of source summaries strung together. The defining organizational discipline is outlining by IDEA or SUB-ARGUMENT, not by source: for each section, the question is "which of my sources have something relevant to say about THIS specific point," not "what does this source say overall." The second essential discipline is engaging sources that complicate or partially disagree with the thesis, rather than citing only agreeing sources — a paper that presents a claim as if universally supported, while quietly omitting inconvenient evidence, is fragile the moment a reader finds the missing piece; addressing complications directly (by qualifying the thesis or explaining why the complication doesn't undermine the overall point) is what makes an argument credible.

## Mental Models
- **Trial-Lawyer Anchor**: a lawyer building a case doesn't call each witness up to give a complete, standalone monologue in turn — the lawyer organizes the closing argument by CLAIM, drawing on whichever witness's testimony supports each specific claim, and directly addresses any witness whose testimony complicates the case. This seeds both fixes: organize by argument, not by witness (source); and address inconvenient testimony directly rather than pretending it doesn't exist.
- **Doctor's-test-results bridge**: a doctor who only reads test results confirming their initial guess produces a less accurate diagnosis. A strong research paper engages the full, sometimes messy picture the sources present.

## Why Students Fail
Source-by-source organization is the path of least resistance because it mirrors how research is CONSUMED (one source read at a time) rather than how an argument should be BUILT (by idea, pulling from whichever sources are relevant). Omitting complicating sources feels protective — acknowledging disagreement feels like weakening the argument — when in fact the opposite is true; this is a confidence-management error, not a research-skill error.

## Misconceptions
- **MC-A (Type 5, instruction-induced from research habits carried into writing)**: "A research paper is a sequence of source summaries." Diagnostic: present a source-organized outline ("Source A," "Source B," "Source C" headings) against an idea-organized outline ("The physiological mechanism," "The behavioral factor," "The counterargument and response") and ask which paper is making its OWN argument. Repair: trial-lawyer anchor — outline by sub-argument first, then ask which sources contribute to THIS specific point.
- **MC-B (Type 6, analogy overextension from "a strong argument has no holes")**: "Synthesizing sources means only using sources that agree." Diagnostic: present two excerpts — one citing only agreeing sources, one acknowledging and addressing a complicating finding — and ask which seems more credible. Repair: doctor's-test-results bridge — deliberately seek out complicating sources and address them, either by showing why the disagreement doesn't undermine the point or by refining the thesis to account for it.

## Analogies
- Trial-lawyer anchor (above) for idea-organization and direct engagement with complicating testimony.
- Doctor's-test-results bridge (above) for the cost of selectively ignoring inconvenient evidence.
- Anti-analogy: engaging a complicating source does not mean abandoning the thesis at the first sign of disagreement — it means qualifying or narrowing its scope where warranted, while showing the core argument still holds.

## Demonstrations
- Convert a source-organized outline (screen-time/sleep research question) into an idea-organized one, using a source-by-idea table (sources as columns, sub-arguments as rows).
- Compare two paper excerpts on the same claim — one citing only agreeing sources, one engaging a complicating finding directly — and evaluate credibility.
- Write one paragraph that weaves two source excerpts together to support the same point, rather than summarizing each separately.

## Discovery Questions
- "Which of your sources have something to say about THIS specific point, not just 'what does this source say overall'?"
- "Is there a source you found that complicates your thesis — did you address it, or leave it out?"
- "Does this paragraph make one combined case using both sources, or does it just summarize each in turn?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Diagnostic: citations/referencing mechanics check and rhetorical-analysis/source-evaluation check (both prerequisites).
2. Anchor: trial-lawyer framing, extended with the doctor's-test-results bridge.
3. TA-1: formulate an original thesis from multiple source summaries.
4. TA-2: build a source-by-idea table and idea-first outline (targets MC-A).
5. TA-3: synthesize two sources within one paragraph.
6. TA-4: engage a complicating source directly (targets MC-B).
7. TA-5: full section construction — idea-organized outline, one synthesized paragraph, one complication-engaging paragraph.

## Tutor Actions
- SHOW: source-organized vs. idea-organized outline pair; agreeing-only vs. complication-engaging excerpt pair.
- ORGANIZE: the source-by-idea table (sources as columns, sub-arguments as rows) as a mandatory planning artifact.
- DO: learner writes one synthesized paragraph combining two source excerpts.
- TEST-THINKING: predict whether a given complicating source can be addressed by qualification or requires refining the thesis.

## Voice Teaching Notes
This is a 4-hour, expert-difficulty concept — actively watch for fatigue signals across the TA sequence and be willing to split the session, resuming at the next TA with a brief review rather than restarting from TA-1.

## Assessment Signals
- Probe: "Build a source-by-idea table for these four sources and three sub-arguments." An MC-A response defaults back to source-labeled headings.
- Probe: "Here's a thesis and a complicating source — write a paragraph addressing it." An MC-B response either omits the complication or abandons the thesis entirely rather than qualifying it.
- Confident-wrong signature: a fluent, well-cited paper that cites only agreeing sources and presents the topic as if uncontested — flag MC-B regardless of citation correctness.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If the learner can synthesize correctly at the paragraph level (single point) but reverts to source-by-source organization at full-section scale, treat this as a load-related regression, not a conceptual gap — provide one additional worked example at full section scale before re-attempting independent work.

## Memory Hooks
"Organize by claim, not by witness" for idea-first structure; "read the test results that don't confirm your guess too" for engaging complicating sources.
