## Learning Objective
The learner will explain why proofreading requires a genuinely different reading mode than content-focused reading, identify and correct discrepancies between a piece of writing and a target publication's formatting requirements, and explain that surface errors and formatting non-compliance are evaluated as a real, separate quality dimension independent of content strength.

## Core Understanding
A content-focused read does NOT reliably catch surface errors — the two reading goals COMPETE for the same limited attention, so focusing hard on meaning genuinely reduces the chance of noticing surface form simultaneously. Proofreading requires a DELIBERATE, separate reading pass specifically focused on spelling, punctuation, and formatting, distinct from any prior content-revision pass. Separately, professional publication contexts evaluate formatting compliance and surface polish as a SEPARATE, real quality dimension from content — a piece with excellent content and reasoning can still be rejected, delayed, or perceived less favorably if it fails a specific publication's formatting requirements or contains persistent surface errors, because these signal a lack of professional care independent of the underlying content quality.

## Mental Models
- **Windshield-Crack-While-Driving Anchor**: a driver intently focused on navigating traffic is genuinely less likely to notice a small crack in their windshield — both are valid uses of attention, but they compete for the same limited resource. Content-focused reading and surface-error-focused reading work the same way; one doesn't automatically accomplish the other.
- **Wrinkled-Clothing-Job-Interview Anchor**: a brilliant candidate's excellent qualifications don't fully compensate for showing up in wrinkled, inappropriate clothing — the qualifications and the surface presentation are genuinely SEPARATE evaluation dimensions, and failing one hurts the outcome regardless of the other's strength.

## Why Students Fail
Learners who've just carefully revised a piece for content assume that careful reading also caught surface errors, because "I read it carefully" feels like a single, undifferentiated act of attention rather than two competing, separately-required tasks. Separately, learners believe strong content is the dominant factor in how writing is received, underestimating that professional readers and editors genuinely weigh formatting compliance and polish as an independent signal of care, not merely a cosmetic afterthought.

## Misconceptions
- **MC-A (Type 1, overgeneralization from "I read it carefully, so I checked everything")**: "If you've already carefully read through a piece for content, you've also effectively proofread it." Diagnostic: have the learner read a passage carefully for content, confirm it makes sense, then ask them to immediately identify surface errors without re-reading — most will have missed several. Repair: windshield-crack anchor — content-focused reading and surface-error reading compete for the same attention; always do a separate, dedicated proofreading pass.
- **MC-B (Type 1, overgeneralization from "content is what really matters")**: "If the content is strong enough, formatting and surface errors won't really matter to a reader or editor." Diagnostic: present two submissions with comparable strong content — one meeting all formatting requirements and error-free, one failing several requirements with multiple surface errors — and ask whether the editor's evaluation depends only on content quality. Repair: wrinkled-clothing-interview anchor — treat formatting compliance and proofreading as a separate, equally necessary quality dimension, regardless of content strength.

## Analogies
- Windshield-crack-while-driving anchor (above) for the distinct-reading-mode principle.
- Wrinkled-clothing-job-interview anchor (above) for formatting-as-separate-evaluation-dimension.
- Anti-analogy: this is not "expect literal zero errors on a first attempt" — this concept teaches a systematic FINAL process (a dedicated pass to catch what earlier passes missed), not a standard of achieving instant perfection without any process at all.

## Demonstrations
- Have the learner immediately identify surface errors (without re-reading) in a passage they've just carefully read for content, confirming the content-focused read alone missed them.
- Present a strong-content submission failing a word-limit requirement against one meeting all formatting requirements, asking whether formatting compliance genuinely affects the outcome.
- Full worked example: a draft with three subtle surface errors and one formatting non-compliance (exceeding a word limit), resolved via a dedicated backward-reading proofreading pass and a separate formatting-requirements check.

## Discovery Questions
- "You just read this passage carefully. Did you notice these specific errors on your first read? Why might reading for meaning make you LESS likely to notice surface errors?"
- "Does the editor's evaluation of these two submissions depend only on content quality, or does the formatting difference genuinely affect the outcome?"
- "Have you done a separate, dedicated pass specifically for surface errors, or are you relying on your content-revision read to have caught them?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Diagnostic: research-methodology critical-evaluation check and academic-writing-advanced revision-precision check.
2. Anchor: windshield-crack and wrinkled-clothing-interview framing together.
3. TA-1: apply a dedicated proofreading pass (backward reading, reading aloud) to a content-reviewed passage, finding missed errors (targets MC-A).
4. TA-2: check a piece against a target publication's formatting requirements for specific discrepancies (targets MC-B).
5. TA-3: apply and compare at least two proofreading techniques on the same passage.
6. TA-4: full publication preparation — dedicated proofreading pass plus formatting compliance check.

## Tutor Actions
- SHOW: content-read-then-surprised-by-missed-errors demonstration; comparable-content submissions differing only in formatting compliance.
- DO: apply backward-sentence-by-sentence reading to a passage already content-reviewed.
- TEST-THINKING: predict whether a strong-content submission with formatting violations will be evaluated purely on content.
- ORGANIZE: a point-by-point formatting-requirements checklist (rather than skimming guidelines) as scaffolding.

## Voice Teaching Notes
If a learner becomes anxious that any remaining imperfection means their writing "isn't ready," clarify explicitly that this concept teaches a systematic final process, not an expectation of zero errors on a first attempt — the dedicated proofreading pass is precisely the tool for catching what earlier passes missed.

## Assessment Signals
- Probe: "Explain, in your own words, why a content-focused reading pass doesn't reliably catch surface errors." A response that can't articulate the competing-attention mechanism signals residual MC-A.
- Probe: "Explain why a piece with strong content can still be rejected due to formatting non-compliance." A response minimizing formatting's real effect signals residual MC-B.
- Confident-wrong signature: a learner who submits a "final" draft after only one content-focused read, confident it's error-free — flag MC-A regardless of how strong the content genuinely is.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If a learner masters proofreading technique on provided passages but doesn't apply it to their own writing in practice, connect this concept explicitly to their own upcoming writing submissions, making the dedicated final-pass habit a required step rather than just a demonstrated skill.
