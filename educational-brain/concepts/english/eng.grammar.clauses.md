# Independent and Dependent Clauses — `eng.grammar.clauses`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.clauses` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Independent and
  Dependent Clauses" — word groups containing a subject and a verb;
  independent clauses can stand alone while dependent clauses cannot.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.phrases` — load-bearing because
  clauses are formally defined in direct structural contrast to
  phrases (presence vs. absence of a subject-verb pair, per that
  concept's own defining test); a learner cannot reliably confirm
  "genuine clause" status without already having the subject-verb-pair
  test secure from the prerequisite concept.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.simple-sentences`,
  `eng.grammar.comma-usage`, `eng.linguistics.syntax-theory-intro`. The
  independent/dependent clause distinction this concept installs is the
  direct structural basis for classifying simple, compound, and complex
  sentences, and for the specific comma rules governing clause
  combination; the linguistics cross-link (a different domain within
  English) formally extends this concept's clause-combination patterns
  into syntactic theory.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 3

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify the subject-verb pair in any clause, confirming both
   independent and dependent clauses are genuine clauses.
2. Confirm independent clauses can stand alone as complete sentences.
3. Confirm dependent clauses contain a genuine subject-verb pair but
   cannot stand alone, due to a subordinating word.
4. Identify independent clauses functioning as structural units within
   compound sentences (two or more independent clauses) and complex
   sentences (independent + dependent clauses).
5. Combine given independent and dependent clauses into correctly
   formed and correctly punctuated compound and complex sentences.

## Core Understanding

A clause — independent or dependent — is defined by one thing and one
thing only: the presence of a genuine subject-verb pair, the same test
established in the prerequisite phrases concept. A DEPENDENT clause
("because she was tired") still genuinely contains a subject ("she")
and a verb ("was") — it is a real clause. The word "dependent" refers
specifically to its INABILITY TO STAND ALONE as a complete sentence,
caused by the subordinating word ("because," "although," "when," "if"),
not to any lack of clause structure. An INDEPENDENT clause is one that
COULD stand alone as a complete sentence. A second, equally load-bearing
fact is that "independent clause" describes a STRUCTURAL UNIT, not a
whole-sentence category — a single sentence can contain MULTIPLE
independent clauses joined together (a compound sentence: "I was tired,
so I went to bed" — two independent clauses joined by "so"), or an
independent clause combined with one or more dependent clauses (a
complex sentence: "I went to bed because I was tired"). "Simple
sentence" specifically means a sentence containing exactly ONE
independent clause and no dependent clauses — a narrower category than
"independent clause" itself.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "adding a word like 'because' removes clause
  status, and 'independent clause' just means 'a whole simple
  sentence'"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept
  exists to correct — a natural default because a dependent clause
  genuinely CANNOT function as a complete sentence on its own, making
  "not a clause" feel like a reasonable conclusion, and because a
  standalone independent clause genuinely IS a complete simple
  sentence, making the two terms feel synonymous until a sentence
  containing multiple clauses is directly examined.
- **Intermediate model — "I identify the subject-verb pair in any
  clause and confirm both independent and dependent clauses are
  genuine clauses"**: installed by TA-1, TA-2, and TA-3, using the
  Component 3 stand-alone-test anchor, resolving
  MC-A-CLAUSE-WITH-A-SUBORDINATING-WORD-ISNT-A-CLAUSE. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly identifying a fresh dependent clause's subject-verb pair
  and confirming it as a genuine clause without hesitation.
- **Advanced model — "I recognize independent clauses as structural
  units that can combine with each other or with dependent clauses
  within larger sentences"**: installed by TA-4, resolving
  MC-INDEPENDENT-CLAUSE-MEANS-SIMPLE-SENTENCE-ONLY by directly
  demonstrating multiple clauses within a single compound or complex
  sentence.
- **Expert model — "I combine given clauses into correctly formed and
  punctuated compound and complex sentences"**: installed by TA-5, the
  concept's genuine mastery bar — actively constructing, not merely
  identifying, multi-clause sentences.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 doesn't use subordinating conjunctions the same
  way, or organizes clause combination very differently, expect this
  concept — especially the compound/complex sentence structure (TA-4/
  TA-5) — to be foundational and require dedicated, explicit practice,
  since the downstream sentence-combining and comma-usage skills all
  depend directly on genuine mastery here, not surface familiarity.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and the Blueprint
itself explicitly names this concept as foundational for everything
downstream. The subordinating-word-removes-clause-status failure
exists because a dependent clause genuinely CANNOT stand alone as a
sentence — concluding it therefore "isn't really a clause" is a
reasonable, if incorrect, inference from that real functional
limitation, with nothing marking the subject-verb pair as still fully
present until a learner is directly asked to identify the subject and
verb inside "because she was tired" (they're both still there). The
independent-clause-means-simple-sentence failure exists because a
standalone independent clause genuinely IS a complete simple sentence —
the two concepts overlap perfectly in the simplest case, giving no
signal that "independent clause" is a smaller, reusable structural
UNIT rather than a whole-sentence category until a learner directly
examines a sentence containing two or more clauses joined together.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.clauses.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions
this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-A-CLAUSE-WITH-A-SUBORDINATING-WORD-ISNT-A-CLAUSE (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — a dependent
  clause's genuine inability to stand alone as a sentence is real,
  correctly-observed evidence, and "therefore it isn't a real clause"
  is a reasonable (if incorrect) inference from that observation, not a
  random guess or L1 transfer; the misconception survives because the
  functional limitation (can't stand alone) and the structural fact
  (still a genuine clause) are easy to conflate without a direct,
  explicit subject-verb-pair check.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a
  fresh dependent clause, spontaneously identifies its subject and verb
  before confirming it "can't stand alone, but it's still a clause" is
  showing the two-step check (clause status, then independence status)
  has been genuinely internalized.

**MC-INDEPENDENT-CLAUSE-MEANS-SIMPLE-SENTENCE-ONLY (Blueprint Component
1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — a standalone
  independent clause genuinely IS a complete simple sentence, so
  treating the two terms as synonymous is a reasonable generalization
  from a case where they genuinely coincide, until a multi-clause
  sentence directly shows "independent clause" functioning as a
  reusable building block rather than a whole-sentence label.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a fresh
  compound sentence, spontaneously counts and names TWO independent
  clauses (rather than describing the whole sentence as one undivided
  unit) before being told is showing the structural-unit understanding
  has generalized beyond the specific TA-4 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the stand-alone test itself is the analogy, not a
  supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically testing
  whether a subject-verb-pair card can be spoken alone as a complete
  sentence, sorting into "stands alone" and "needs something else"
  piles while explicitly confirming BOTH piles genuinely contain a
  subject-verb pair, directly embodies the two-step check (clause
  status first, then independence status) as a concrete, hands-on sort
  rather than an abstract distinction. Breaking point: the stand-alone
  test demonstrates the independent/dependent distinction well but
  doesn't on its own address the structural-unit insight (TA-4) — that
  independent clauses combine WITHIN larger sentences — which needs its
  own separate, multi-clause demonstration.
- **Alternative — a complete LEGO minifigure vs. the same minifigure
  attached to a larger scene**: a minifigure standing alone is a
  complete, functional unit (an independent clause as a simple
  sentence); the same minifigure attached within a larger built scene
  is still the same complete unit, just now part of something bigger
  (an independent clause within a compound or complex sentence) — it
  doesn't stop being a complete minifigure just because it's now
  attached to other pieces. Breaking point: useful for the structural-
  unit-within-a-larger-whole insight, but doesn't address the
  dependent-clause-is-still-a-clause insight, which the stand-alone
  test's own explicit both-piles-are-clauses confirmation handles
  directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe dependent clauses as "incomplete
  sentences" without immediately clarifying they are still complete,
  genuine CLAUSES**: the term "incomplete sentence" is accurate for what
  a dependent clause cannot do (stand alone) but, used alone without
  the clarification, directly risks reinforcing
  MC-A-CLAUSE-WITH-A-SUBORDINATING-WORD-ISNT-A-CLAUSE by implying
  something is missing from the clause's own internal structure, when
  in fact its internal structure (the subject-verb pair) is entirely
  complete.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
dependent-clause-is-still-a-clause insight and the independent-clause-
as-structural-unit insight are both genuine, directly testable
phenomena well-suited to guided discovery: **need** (the learner,
having secured TA-2's independent-clause practice, is asked whether
"because she was tired" has a subject and verb, discovering the answer
is yes even though the phrase clearly can't stand alone); **playground/
invention** (TA-1's mixed subject-verb-pair identification and TA-2's
independent-clause practice build initial confidence with the base
subject-verb-pair test before dependent clauses are formally
introduced); **collision** (the Blueprint's own "because she was
tired" probe breaks the subordinating-word-removes-clause-status
assumption; the "I was tired, so I went to bed" two-clause-counting
probe breaks the independent-clause-means-whole-sentence assumption);
**formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of both the dependent-
clause-is-still-a-clause insight and the independent-clause-as-
structural-unit insight); **compression** (TA-5's fluent clause
combination into compound and complex sentences, applying both
insights directly without each instance separately re-derived). The
specific terms "independent clause," "dependent clause," "compound
sentence," and "complex sentence" are convention-bound grammatical
terminology best introduced by direct instruction once the learner has
already felt the underlying distinctions through the stand-alone-test
anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (identifying the subject-verb pair in any clause) is placed first
as the concept's foundational, cross-cutting entry point, per the
Blueprint's own explicit connection back to the prerequisite phrases
concept's subject-verb-pair test — establishing that BOTH independent
and dependent clauses share this defining feature before any
independence-status distinction is introduced. TA-2 (independent
clauses, standing alone) and TA-3 (dependent clauses, cannot stand
alone) follow, directly targeting
MC-A-CLAUSE-WITH-A-SUBORDINATING-WORD-ISNT-A-CLAUSE — per the
Blueprint's own session architecture, this conflict-evidence probe is
placed right after TA-3, once the learner has secure practice with both
clause types to contrast. TA-4 (independent clauses within compound and
complex sentences) introduces a SECOND, independent dimension
(structural combination, not clause-type identification), directly
targeting MC-INDEPENDENT-CLAUSE-MEANS-SIMPLE-SENTENCE-ONLY. TA-5
(building sentences by combining clauses) is placed last as the
concept's integration capstone, requiring the learner to actively
CONSTRUCT, not merely identify, compound and complex sentences with
correct punctuation. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own
turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References
below.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: identifying subject-verb pairs and independence status in
  fresh clauses; counting and classifying clauses within fresh compound
  and complex sentences; actively combining given clauses into new,
  correctly-punctuated sentences.
- **Far**: this concept's independent/dependent clause distinction is
  the direct structural basis for `eng.grammar.simple-sentences`'s
  formal definition (exactly one independent clause, no dependent
  clauses), `eng.grammar.comma-usage`'s clause-combination-specific
  comma rules (comma before a coordinating conjunction joining two
  independent clauses; comma after an introductory dependent clause),
  and `eng.linguistics.syntax-theory-intro`'s formal extension of
  clause-combination patterns into syntactic theory (a different domain
  within English, per this concept's `unlocks` field).
- **Real-world**: every multi-idea sentence the learner constructs — in
  speech or writing — depends on correctly combining independent and
  dependent clauses to avoid both sentence-fragment errors (a dependent
  clause standing alone) and run-on errors (independent clauses
  incorrectly joined).
- **Expert-transfer**: sophisticated, varied sentence construction at
  any advanced level depends on fluent command of clause combination —
  the ability to deliberately vary between simple, compound, and
  complex sentence structures for rhetorical effect is a hallmark of
  mature writing, built directly on the clause-level structural
  awareness this concept establishes.
