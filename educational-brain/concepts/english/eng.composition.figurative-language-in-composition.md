## Learning Objective
The learner will deploy figurative language (metaphor, simile, and related devices) purposefully in expository/argumentative writing to clarify an abstract idea for a reader, and will recognize when a figure of speech helps versus when it clutters or misleads.

## Core Understanding
Figurative language in composition (as opposed to poetry/fiction) exists to make an abstract or unfamiliar idea concrete for the reader — it is a tool for CLARITY, not decoration. A metaphor like "compound interest is a snowball rolling downhill, picking up more snow the further it rolls" earns its place because it makes an abstract mathematical mechanism vivid and memorable; "inflation is a slow leak in a tire" works the same way. The craft skill is picking a figure whose STRUCTURE actually maps onto the mechanism being explained (the snowball's growth mirrors compounding; a slow leak mirrors gradual, continuous loss) — not just any vivid image. A mismatched or overloaded figure (mixed metaphors, or figurative language stacked for its own sake) actively confuses the reader instead of clarifying, because the reader now has to work out which parts of the image map to the idea and which don't.

## Mental Models
- **Directions-to-One-House Anchor**: giving someone directions using ten different landmarks when three would do doesn't help them find the house faster — it adds noise. A composition's figurative language should be like giving exactly the directions needed: enough concrete imagery to locate the abstract idea, no more.
- **Structural mapping test**: for any candidate metaphor, ask "does the STRUCTURE of the image (how it grows, moves, or breaks) mirror the STRUCTURE of the idea being explained?" — a snowball growing as it rolls maps onto interest compounding as it accrues; a tire leaking maps onto value eroding continuously. If the structures don't line up, the metaphor is decorative, not explanatory.

## Why Students Fail
Learners in fiction/poetry contexts learn that figurative language is inherently good writing, and transfer that belief wholesale into expository/argumentative writing, where the goal is clarity for a reader rather than aesthetic effect for its own sake. They then either avoid figurative language entirely in "serious" writing (missing a genuine clarity tool) or pile it on indiscriminately (creating mixed-metaphor confusion).

## Misconceptions
- **MC-A (Type 1, overgeneralization from "figurative language belongs to fiction/poetry")**: "Figurative language only belongs in fiction and poetry — expository/argumentative writing should be literal." Diagnostic: ask the learner whether a well-chosen metaphor could ever help explain a hard concept in a non-fiction essay; a flat "no, that's not what this kind of writing does" signals MC-A. Repair: show the snowball/compound-interest example working precisely BECAUSE the essay is expository — the metaphor earns its place by making a real mechanism vivid, not by being "literary."
- **MC-B (Type 6, analogy overextension from "figurative language = good writing")**: "Any figurative language I add automatically improves my writing." Diagnostic: present a passage with two clashing metaphors in adjacent sentences (a mixed-metaphor example) and ask the learner to evaluate it — if they rate it positively purely because it's "descriptive," MC-B is active. Repair: apply the structural mapping test explicitly — does each figure's structure actually track the idea, and do multiple figures in the same passage cohere with each other rather than compete?

## Analogies
- Directions-to-one-house anchor (above) for "enough is enough, more isn't automatically better."
- Compound-interest-as-snowball and inflation-as-leaking-tire as the two canonical worked examples of structurally-sound metaphors.
- Anti-analogy: this is not "figurative language is decoration to be added after the real writing is done" — a well-chosen figure IS part of the explanation, integrated at the point where the abstract idea needs it, not sprinkled in afterward.

## Demonstrations
- Show the snowball metaphor for compound interest and walk through exactly which features of a rolling snowball (starting size, growth rate, distance traveled) map onto exactly which features of compounding (principal, rate, time).
- Show a mixed-metaphor passage (e.g., mixing a "sailing" metaphor with a "battle" metaphor in the same paragraph for the same idea) and have the learner identify where the images clash.
- Have the learner take an abstract idea from their own current essay topic and test three candidate metaphors against the structural mapping test, keeping only the one that fits.

## Discovery Questions
- "What specific feature of your image (how it grows, moves, breaks) matches a specific feature of the idea you're explaining?"
- "If I removed this metaphor, would my explanation actually get harder to follow, or would nothing be lost?"
- "Do the images in this paragraph work together, or are they secretly two different pictures fighting each other?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Anchor: directions-to-one-house framing — enough figurative language to clarify, not decorate for its own sake.
2. Model: walk through the compound-interest/snowball structural mapping in full.
3. Guided: learner evaluates the inflation/leaking-tire metaphor using the same structural mapping test.
4. Guided: learner identifies the clash in a mixed-metaphor passage (targets MC-B directly).
5. Independent: learner selects and tests one metaphor for an abstract idea in their own writing.
6. Confirm: learner explains why a well-chosen metaphor belongs in expository writing (targets MC-A).

## Tutor Actions
- SHOW: the compound-interest/snowball and inflation/tire worked examples side by side with their structural mappings labeled.
- ERROR ANALYSIS: a mixed-metaphor passage for the learner to diagnose.
- DO: learner drafts and structurally tests a metaphor for a concept from their own subject area.
- ORGANIZE: a two-column "feature of the image / feature of the idea" mapping table as a scaffolding tool.

## Voice Teaching Notes
If the learner reaches for a vivid but structurally mismatched image, resist correcting it immediately — ask them to walk through the mapping themselves first ("what does the tire's leak match in your idea?"), since self-caught mismatches are more durable than tutor-flagged ones.

## Assessment Signals
- Probe: "Explain why the snowball metaphor works for compound interest, feature by feature." Correct answer names the structural correspondence explicitly, not just "it's a good image."
- Probe: given a mixed-metaphor passage, "what's wrong here?" — correct answer names the clash, not just "too much description."
- Confident-wrong signature: a learner who adds a vivid, well-written but structurally unmapped metaphor and defends it purely on aesthetic grounds — flag as MC-B regardless of the writing quality.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If a learner insists all figurative language is "too literary" for serious writing (MC-A) even after the snowball example, ask them to find a metaphor already IN a piece of serious nonfiction writing they respect (a textbook, a good article) — external proof it's already normal practice often lands better than another constructed example.

## Memory Hooks
"Enough directions to find the house, not ten landmarks" for restraint; the snowball's specific feature-by-feature mapping to compounding as the retrievable model of what a GOOD figurative choice looks like.

## Transfer Connections

## Cross-Subject Connections
The same structural-mapping discipline used to evaluate a metaphor's fitness is the same discipline used to evaluate a scientific analogy's fitness (e.g., the "atom as solar system" analogy, taught and later retired in physics) — both ask "does the image's structure actually track the mechanism?"
