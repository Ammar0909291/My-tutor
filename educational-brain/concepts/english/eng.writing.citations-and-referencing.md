## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify which specific pieces of information in a piece of writing require a citation (common knowledge vs. specific/borrowed information).
2. Correctly format both an in-text citation and its corresponding reference-list entry for a given source.
3. Explain why omitting a citation for non-common-knowledge information constitutes misrepresenting borrowed ideas as one's own, not just a formatting oversight.
4. Produce a properly paraphrased and cited sentence, its reference-list entry, and a credibility evaluation of the source, all as distinct steps.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **citing sources is not only necessary when using a source's exact wording** — paraphrasing or summarizing a source's ideas, facts, or data in one's own words still requires attribution, since changing the wording doesn't change where the underlying information came from; only genuinely common knowledge (facts widely known and not attributable to one specific source) is exempt. Second, **citing a source correctly does not mean the cited information is automatically true or good evidence** — a citation answers "where did this come from?" not "is this reliable?"; citation format and source credibility are two entirely separate questions, and a properly formatted citation to an unreliable or biased source is still unreliable, requiring a separate credibility evaluation.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "I only need to cite when I use someone's exact words, and citing a source proves the claim is true."**
The simulation: paraphrase a source's specific fact into different wording with no citation, and treat a correctly-formatted citation to a biased blog as sufficient proof of the claim.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a paraphrased fact with no citation tested against where the underlying idea actually came from, and two equally-well-formatted citations, one to a peer-reviewed study and one to a promotional blog.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Repainting a borrowed car doesn't change whose car it originally was. And a citation is an address on an envelope — it doesn't tell you what's inside is true."

**Intermediate — "I can now cite paraphrased material and separate citation from credibility, but I sometimes still forget the citation on my own independent writing under time pressure."**
The simulation: correctly applies both corrections in guided exercises but drops citations for paraphrased material when producing original, unguided writing.
*Upgrade trigger*: the adaptive-flags "source check" mandatory-pass requirement, treating this as a habit-formation gap requiring explicit process discipline.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Trace every non-common-knowledge sentence back with 'I learned this from ___' — if that blank can't be filled with your own observation, it needs a citation."

**Advanced — "I produce a properly paraphrased and cited sentence, its reference entry, and a separate credibility evaluation, all together."**
The simulation: integrates the citation-need test, mechanical formatting, and credibility evaluation into one coherent, complete sourcing practice. Matches TA-4/WE-3.
*Upgrade trigger*: `eng.composition.research-paper-writing`'s dependence on reliable source acknowledgment, since research writing is built entirely from synthesized outside sources.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you reliable source acknowledgment. A full research paper, built from many synthesized sources, comes next."

**Expert — "at fluency, tracing information to its source and evaluating credibility separately from citing happens as an integrated part of ordinary research writing, without a separate deliberate checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit trace-cite-evaluate routine is a scaffold for a sourcing habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of academic writing.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **quotation marks are the most visually salient signal of "borrowed material,"** so the citation habit attaches to that visible marker rather than to the underlying, less visually obvious fact of whether the IDEA itself originated elsewhere — paraphrasing removes the visible marker while leaving the actual attribution obligation unchanged.

The second mechanism is that **a correctly formatted citation looks authoritative and complete**, providing a strong, satisfying sense of "I did the academic thing right" that masks the entirely separate, harder question of whether the source itself deserves trust.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-ONLY-DIRECT-QUOTES-NEED-CITATION
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 4 (notation-induced), from quotation marks being the most visually salient signal of borrowed material, with the citation habit attaching to that marker rather than to the underlying attribution obligation.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **trace paraphrased material back to its origin explicitly**, since the wording-versus-ownership distinction is far more convincing demonstrated through a direct origin-trace than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given an original source sentence and a paraphrased version with no citation, the learner identifies that the paraphrase still requires a citation.

### MC-B-CITING-A-SOURCE-MEANS-THE-INFORMATION-IS-AUTOMATICALLY-TRUE-OR-GOOD-EVIDENCE
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of correct citation format providing a genuine sense of academic completeness, extended into treating that formatting alone as proof of reliability.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **evaluating two equally well-formatted citations of differing credibility side by side**, since the format/credibility separation is far more convincing demonstrated through a direct contrast than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given two correctly-cited sources making conflicting claims, the learner evaluates each source's credibility separately using a four-question check.

## Analogies

**Best — the Borrowed-Car Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a car with a "borrowed from" tag, whether driven as-is or repainted a different color — directly seeding the fix for MC-A (repainting doesn't change whose car it originally was). The same tag extended to ask whether it tells you the car is safe to drive — directly seeding the fix for MC-B (the tag tells you where it came from, not whether it's reliable).
*Breaking point*: a car's ownership is a simple, binary, legally verifiable fact, while a piece of information's "ownership" (whether it counts as common knowledge or must be attributed) sometimes involves genuinely more judgment-dependent edge cases than a car's clear title.

**Alternative — a chain of custody for physical evidence in a criminal investigation.** Evidence must be traceable back through every hand that touched it, regardless of whether it's been repackaged or relabeled along the way — and having a documented chain of custody says nothing about whether the evidence itself proves guilt.
*Breaking point*: a chain of custody is a formal, legally-mandated procedure with strict rules, while citation conventions vary more by discipline and style guide, a less rigid analog than a legal evidentiary chain.

**Visual anchor — the Borrowed-Car Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"You only need to cite if you use the exact words in quotes."** Directly installs the quotes-only misconception as a stated definition.
- **"If it's cited, it must be true."** Directly installs the citation-equals-truth misconception as a stated definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Borrowed-Car Anchor (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Does repainting the car change whose car it originally was?"
2. **The paraphrase-still-cites collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Where did this second student learn this fact — did they discover it themselves, or did it come from the source?"
3. **The citation-versus-credibility collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Both are cited correctly — does that tell us anything about whether either source is trustworthy?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by tracing paraphrased material to its origin and by evaluating source credibility independently from citation format.

1. **Need**: "If you change every word of a fact but keep the same underlying idea, is it still 'borrowed'?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely sorts ten sentences into "needs citation" and "common knowledge" before being told the official common-knowledge test.
3. **Invention**: "Can a perfectly formatted citation point to an unreliable source?"
4. **Collision**: present two properly-cited sources of very different quality, and ask whether formatting tells you anything about reliability.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — paraphrasing still requires citation, and citation format is separate from source credibility — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Trace the idea, not just the words; cite the address, then judge what's inside."

## Teaching Sequence

- **What requires a citation (TA-1) comes first**, establishing the common-knowledge test before either misconception is directly confronted.
- **Paraphrasing still requires citation (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting the first misconception once the citation-need test is established.
- **In-text citation and reference list mechanics (TA-3) comes third**, teaching the formatting skill independent of either misconception.
- **Citation format vs. source credibility (TA-4) comes last**, directly confronting the second misconception once mechanical formatting is secure — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated origin-trace for a paraphrased fact, and their separate, explicit credibility evaluation of a source**, since a correctly-cited paper with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from lucky imitation of a template.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated origin-trace** ("I learned this from ___") applied to a new paraphrased sentence — the direct behavioral signature of the paraphrase-still-cites fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary, separate credibility evaluation of a cited source**, distinct from confirming its citation format is correct — direct evidence the format/credibility separation is internalized.
- **Omitting a citation for a paraphrased fact, or treating a citation's mere presence as proof of a claim's truth** — the direct behavioral signature of either misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Trace the idea, not just the words — and cite the address, then judge what's inside."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But I put it in my own words, so it's my idea now, right?"** — take this as an accurate observation about the wording change, not a mistake; respond by adding the origin test: "You did rewrite the words, that's true — but where did the underlying fact or idea actually come from? Did you discover it yourself, or did it come from that source?"
- **"I cited it, so it's a good piece of evidence"** — a genuine, reasonable sense of having done the correct academic step; respond by naming the additional layer: "Citing it correctly shows where it came from, yes — but have you separately checked whether that source is actually reliable?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full application down to **sorting one single list of 6 sentences into needs-citation versus common-knowledge, with mechanics and credibility set aside.** Isolates the most basic citation-need discipline before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just cite anything with a quote mark around it."** This directly licenses the quotes-only pattern, undermining the origin-tracing discipline this concept specifically builds.
