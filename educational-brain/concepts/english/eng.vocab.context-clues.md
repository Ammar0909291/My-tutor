# Context Clues — `eng.vocab.context-clues`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.context-clues` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Context Clues" — using
  surrounding words and sentences to infer the meaning of an unfamiliar
  word, teaching this as multi-source evidence-gathering rather than a
  single-clue guess.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.word-recognition` — load-bearing because
  context-clue inference is precisely the productive response to a
  genuine vocabulary gap that concept teaches the learner to honestly
  identify (TA-4/MP-4 there); a learner who has not yet learned to
  distinguish "I can decode this but don't know it" from "I know this
  word" has no reliable trigger for when context-clue inference is even
  needed.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.vocab.homonyms-homophones`,
  `eng.reading.print-to-meaning`. Distinguishing which of several
  possible meanings a homonym carries in a given sentence (homonyms/
  homophones) is itself a context-clue-integration task applied to a
  known word rather than an unknown one; broader print-to-meaning
  reading comprehension likewise depends on the same whole-context
  evidence-weighing skill this concept installs.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify and use a direct synonym/restatement clue to infer an
   unfamiliar word's meaning.
2. Identify and use a contrast/antonym signal (but, however, unlike) to
   infer meaning through opposition.
3. Identify and use an example/illustration clue (a list of items
   following the word) to infer its general category or meaning.
4. Weigh MULTIPLE clue types together across a full sentence, rather
   than relying on a single nearby word alone.
5. Form an initial context-based hypothesis about a word's meaning and
   appropriately revise it when further context provides additional or
   contradicting evidence.

## Core Understanding

Context clues are the surrounding textual evidence — restatements,
contrasts, examples, descriptions of action — that a reader integrates
to infer an unfamiliar word's meaning, and reliable inference requires
weighing the WHOLE available evidence, not fixating on the first
salient nearby word. A single adjacent word can be misleading in
isolation (a word describing energetic movement near an unfamiliar
adjective might suggest "big" or "excited," when the fuller sentence
actually supports "sociable"), so genuine context-clue skill means
scanning the entire sentence, and nearby sentences if needed, for every
relevant piece of evidence before committing to a guess. A second,
equally load-bearing fact is that a context-based inference is a
reasonable WORKING HYPOTHESIS, not a guaranteed, dictionary-precise
definition — good readers stay open to revising an initial guess as
further context appears (a word first inferred as "tired" might, once
later text reveals the state persists "even after a full night's
sleep," be better understood as something like "gloomy" or "sullen"),
and recognize that even a settled context-based guess may be
approximate, appropriately followed by a dictionary check for full
precision rather than treated as final and exact.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "I can guess a word's meaning from whichever
  nearby word first catches my attention"**: the arriving, pre-
  instructional model this concept exists to correct — a natural
  shortcut, since a single salient nearby word often DOES correlate with
  the right answer, giving no signal that a more careful, whole-sentence
  approach would sometimes be needed instead.
- **Intermediate model — "I can recognize and name specific clue types
  (restatement, contrast, example) and use each one on its own to infer
  meaning"**: installed by TA-1 through TA-3's clue-type-by-clue-type
  practice, using the Component 3 detective's clue board to make each
  evidence type concrete and separately identifiable. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly naming which TYPE of clue (restatement, contrast, or
  example) is present in a fresh sentence, not merely producing a
  correct guess.
- **Advanced model — "I weigh ALL the available clues in a sentence
  together, rather than settling for the first one I notice"**:
  installed by TA-4's whole-sentence integration task, resolving
  MC-GUESS-FROM-ONE-WORD-NEARBY through the felt contrast between a
  partial, single-clue guess and a fuller, better-supported one. Upgrade
  trigger: spontaneously citing multiple distinct pieces of evidence
  from a sentence when explaining a guess, without being asked to
  "look for more clues."
- **Expert model — "a context-based guess is a working hypothesis I
  actively test and revise as more context appears, not a locked-in
  final answer"**: installed by TA-5's hypothesis-testing-and-revision
  task, resolving MC-CONTEXT-CLUES-ALWAYS-GIVE-CERTAINTY. This is the
  concept's genuine mastery bar — treating revision in light of new
  evidence as a sign of strong, careful reading rather than
  indecisiveness or failure.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 (English-learner) students whose surrounding-sentence vocabulary
  and syntax comprehension may itself still be developing, the
  surrounding context supplied for practice should be deliberately
  scaffolded to be otherwise accessible — this concept should not be
  tested by simultaneously stacking a second comprehension difficulty
  (an unfamiliar surrounding sentence structure) on top of the target
  word's own unfamiliarity.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both extensions of
reasonable, generally-useful reading habits. The single-clue failure
exists because grabbing the first salient nearby word is a fast,
low-effort strategy that succeeds often enough in casual reading to
feel reliable — nothing in ordinary reading experience signals that
this shortcut can mislead until a sentence is specifically constructed
(as TA-4's practice items are) so that the most salient nearby word
points toward a plausible but wrong inference, requiring the FULL
sentence to correct it. The certainty-treatment failure exists because,
once a learner has done the real cognitive work of assembling and
committing to a guess, that guess understandably starts to feel settled
and final — nothing in the act of guessing itself signals that further
context might overturn or refine it, and revising a hard-won conclusion
can feel like admitting the earlier work was wasted, rather than
recognizing revision as the expected, healthy continuation of the same
process.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.vocab.context-clues.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions
this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-GUESS-FROM-ONE-WORD-NEARBY (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — using a single
  salient nearby word IS a genuinely useful, often-correct strategy in
  ordinary reading (most single-clue guesses turn out fine), so the
  learner extends "the first clue I noticed usually works" into "the
  first clue I notice is sufficient," with nothing in typical reading
  marking the boundary case where the fuller context actually
  contradicts that first impression.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when asked to
  justify a guess, spontaneously says something like "well, this part
  of the sentence also suggests..." citing a SECOND piece of evidence
  unprompted is showing the whole-context habit has generalized beyond
  the specific TA-4 practice items.

**MC-CONTEXT-CLUES-ALWAYS-GIVE-CERTAINTY (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: also a Type 1 signature, distinct from
  the first misconception's mechanism — a successful context-based
  inference genuinely does feel conclusive at the moment it is reached
  (the learner has real evidence supporting it), so the learner
  extends "this guess is well-supported" into "this guess is certain
  and final," with nothing in the initial inference process itself
  signaling that later context could still refine or overturn it.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, upon encountering
  contradicting later context, says something like "oh wait, that
  changes things" and revises without resistance is showing the
  hypothesis-testing stance has been genuinely installed — a stronger
  signal than a learner who only revises when explicitly told the first
  guess was wrong.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the detective's clue board itself is the analogy,
  not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): gathering multiple
  clue cards (a restatement, a contrast, an example) around an
  unfamiliar word before naming a final "suspect" (meaning) directly
  embodies whole-context evidence-weighing as a deliberate, multi-source
  process. Breaking point: a detective's clue board typically implies
  clues eventually converge on one definitive answer, which understates
  that a context-based vocabulary guess may remain a reasonable
  approximation even after all available clues are gathered — this
  entry treats the detective anchor as the introductory frame for
  gathering evidence (TA-1–4), paired explicitly with TA-5's separate
  emphasis on revision and appropriate uncertainty.
- **Alternative — assembling a jigsaw puzzle from a few pieces, not the
  whole box**: each context clue is one puzzle piece; a single piece
  alone can suggest several possible pictures, but several pieces
  together narrow the possibilities substantially, even without
  completing the whole puzzle. Breaking point: useful for illustrating
  that partial evidence still meaningfully narrows down (rather than
  fully determines) a guess, but should not be extended to suggest a
  guess is only valid once "enough" pieces are gathered — even a
  reasonable, incomplete-evidence guess (Component 3's working
  hypothesis) has genuine value.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe a context-based guess as "cheating"
  or "not really knowing" the word compared to looking it up in a
  dictionary**: framing inference as an inferior substitute for
  dictionary lookup undermines the concept's genuine value — context-
  clue inference is a legitimate, transferable, and often faster
  real-world reading skill in its own right, not merely a workaround for
  not having a dictionary handy.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (detective's clue board)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any book, article, or even a text message
  containing an unfamiliar word supplies real context-clue practice; no
  special materials are required.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's whole-sentence integration
  task and TA-5's hypothesis-revision task are both hands-on, requiring
  the learner to actively produce and then test a guess, not merely
  recognize a correct one.
- **Teacher-demo (single-clue vs. whole-context contrast)**: the tutor
  models the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration —
  guessing from one salient word, then re-examining the full sentence —
  making the difference in reliability directly visible.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  context-based guess (TA-1–4) or a revised hypothesis (TA-5), ask the
  learner to state their own inference before the tutor confirms —
  Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** Whole-
context integration and appropriate hypothesis revision are both
genuine, directly testable phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having correctly identified a genuine vocabulary
gap via the prerequisite concept, is asked how to figure out the word's
meaning without a dictionary); **playground/invention** (TA-1 through
TA-3's clue-type-by-clue-type practice lets the learner notice several
distinct kinds of evidence exist); **collision** (TA-4's whole-sentence
integration items break the single-clue shortcut directly; TA-5's
contradicting later-context items break the certainty assumption);
**formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of the context-clues
strategy and its appropriate limits); **compression** (fluently scanning
a full sentence for multiple clue types without needing each clue type
separately prompted). The specific NAMES of clue types (restatement,
contrast, example) are convention-bound vocabulary-instruction
terminology best introduced by direct instruction alongside each TA's
worked example, since there is no regularity to discover in the labels
themselves, only in the evidence patterns they name.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (synonym/restatement clues) is placed first because it is the most
direct and explicit clue type — the meaning is stated nearly outright in
the sentence — giving the learner an accessible entry point into the
whole strategy before subtler clue types are introduced. TA-2 (contrast/
antonym clues) follows, requiring the learner to infer meaning through
OPPOSITION rather than direct restatement — a genuinely harder
inferential step, appropriately sequenced after the more transparent
restatement case. TA-3 (example/illustration clues) follows next,
requiring inference of a general CATEGORY from specific listed
instances — a different inferential direction (specific-to-general)
than TA-1/TA-2's more direct semantic relationships. TA-4 (whole-
sentence/passage integration) is placed after all three clue types have
been practiced individually, because meaningfully "weighing multiple
clue types together" presupposes the learner can already recognize each
type separately — attempting integration before the individual clue
types are secure would give the learner nothing distinct to integrate.
TA-5 (hypothesis testing and revision across a passage) is placed last,
extending the single-sentence skill (TA-1–4) to a multi-sentence,
temporally unfolding context, which is precisely what makes revision in
light of NEW, later-arriving evidence a meaningful and demonstrable
skill. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn
session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

target skill.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner cites MULTIPLE pieces of
  evidence unprompted when justifying a guess (rather than only the
  first thing they noticed) — this is the primary diagnostic signal for
  whether whole-context integration (TA-4's target) has genuinely
  transferred beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before revising an
  initial guess in light of new context (TA-5), followed by a correctly
  adjusted inference, is a good sign — it suggests active reconsideration
  rather than either stubborn adherence to the first guess or an
  uncritical, reflexive change. Instant, confident refusal to reconsider
  a guess even when directly asked "does that still fit?" is the
  concerning pattern for MC-CONTEXT-CLUES-ALWAYS-GIVE-CERTAINTY.
- **Confidence calibration stakes**: real and central to MP-5
  specifically — the learner should express appropriate, moderate
  confidence in a context-based guess (a reasonable working hypothesis)
  rather than either false certainty (claiming an exact, dictionary-
  precise definition) or excessive hedging (refusing to commit to any
  guess at all pending a dictionary check).
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does that still fit, now that you've
  seen more of the sentence?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence,
  per `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  after presenting new context, making revision-readiness an explicit,
  routinely-invited response rather than something the learner must
  independently decide to offer.

## Assessment Signals

- **Diagnostic (golden probe)**: the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence
  probe (Component 1) — presenting a plausible single-clue guess, then
  directing attention to the whole sentence's fuller evidence — cleanly
  separates a learner still fixating on one nearby word from one who
  correctly integrates the fuller context.
- **Guided practice → independent practice**: the Blueprint's WE-1 →
  WE-2 → WE-3 → MP-1 through MP-5 fading ladder is reused directly
  (Components 5–6) — not re-authored here.
- **Mastery gate set** (`../../assessment/05-mastery-verification.md
  §3`): restatement-clue inference (MP-1); contrast-clue inference
  (MP-2); example-clue inference (MP-3); whole-sentence integration
  (MP-4, a strong diagnostic item since it explicitly requires citing
  multiple clues rather than one); strategy explanation with appropriate
  epistemic humility (MP-5, requiring the learner to articulate both the
  process and its inherent approximateness).
- **Interpretation of response pattern**: fast-and-correct inference
  using explicitly multiple cited clues on a genuinely novel sentence
  confirms whole-context integration is internalized. A correct-looking
  guess justified by only ONE piece of evidence, even when the guess
  happens to be right, is the dangerous quadrant for MC-GUESS-FROM-ONE-
  WORD-NEARBY — the correct answer can mask an unreliable process,
  warranting a return to TA-4's multi-clue-required items specifically
  designed so single-clue guessing produces a wrong answer. A learner
  who refuses to adjust a guess when directly shown contradicting later
  context is showing MC-CONTEXT-CLUES-ALWAYS-GIVE-CERTAINTY still
  active, warranting a return to TA-5.
- **Transfer items**: near (inferring a fresh word's meaning using
  multiple clue types in a single sentence); far (revising an initial
  guess across a genuinely novel multi-sentence passage, per TA-5);
  real-world (applying context-clue inference to unfamiliar words
  encountered in independent reading, without a structured clue-type
  prompt); cross-domain (this concept's whole-context-integration skill
  transfers directly to `eng.vocab.homonyms-homophones`'s task of
  selecting the correct meaning of a known word from context, and to
  `eng.reading.print-to-meaning`'s broader comprehension work).

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a context-clue inference task shrinks to the
  Component 3 detective's clue board using the most transparent clue
  type available (a direct restatement, TA-1's level), with the tutor
  modeling the clue-gathering process explicitly before the learner
  attempts independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: a confident but
  single-clue-justified guess is more likely than "I don't know" —
  route directly to MC-GUESS-FROM-ONE-WORD-NEARBY's recovery (re-
  examining the whole sentence for additional clues) rather than
  treating a plausible-sounding wrong guess as a generic vocabulary gap.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously re-reads a sentence a second time before answering
  (rather than answering immediately after a single read) is showing
  the whole-context habit generalizing — this should be named and
  praised explicitly, per the Blueprint's own regulation-tail guidance
  (praising gathering multiple clues and staying open to revision).

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedure — a multi-step inferential strategy
(scan for clue types, integrate multiple clues, hold the result as a
revisable hypothesis) applied to novel unfamiliar words, rather than a
fixed fact set, following a skill-fluency pattern: correctness first
(reliably identifying individual clue types, even slowly), then fluent,
integrated whole-context inference at reading speed, with the
hypothesis-revision stance maintained as a standing habit rather than
something eventually abandoned once guesses feel confident.
Interleaving partners: mix single-clue-type items (testing TA-1–3's
component skills) with whole-sentence-integration and multi-sentence-
revision items (testing TA-4/TA-5's synthesis) in ongoing review, so the
learner continues exercising both the component clue-recognition skills
and their combined, revisable application.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: inferring a fresh unfamiliar word's meaning using multiple
  clue types in a genuinely novel sentence.
- **Far**: this concept's whole-context-integration skill is the direct
  foundation for `eng.vocab.homonyms-homophones` (selecting the correct
  meaning of a KNOWN word from its surrounding context, rather than
  inferring an unknown one) and `eng.reading.print-to-meaning` (broader
  passage-level comprehension depending on the same evidence-weighing
  habit).
- **Real-world**: every unfamiliar word encountered during independent
  reading — books, articles, instructions — is an immediate, ungated
  opportunity to apply context-clue inference without a dictionary at
  hand.
- **Expert-transfer**: efficient, dictionary-independent vocabulary
  growth throughout a reader's lifetime depends substantially on this
  concept's inference skill becoming fluent and automatic, since most
  vocabulary growth after formal schooling occurs through incidental
  reading rather than explicit word study.
