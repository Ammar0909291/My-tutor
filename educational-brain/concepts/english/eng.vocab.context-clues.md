## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify a restatement/synonym clue nearby an unfamiliar word and derive its meaning from it.
2. Identify a contrast/antonym clue signaled by words like "but," "however," or "unlike" and infer meaning through opposition.
3. Identify an example/illustration clue and infer the unfamiliar word's general category.
4. Integrate multiple clue types across a whole sentence, rather than guessing from a single nearby word.
5. Form an initial hypothesis about a word's meaning and revise it when further context provides additional or contradicting evidence.

## Core Understanding

Context-clue inference is a **multi-source evidence-gathering process**, not a single-clue guess — this is the concept's first load-bearing fact, directly following from `eng.vocab.word-recognition`'s own diagnostic work: once a word is correctly identified as a genuine vocabulary gap, the surrounding sentence or passage typically contains several distinct types of evidence (a direct restatement or synonym, a contrast signaled by words like "but" or "unlike," a list of examples revealing a category) that should be weighed together, not resolved by latching onto whichever single nearby word happens to be most familiar or emotionally salient. The concept's second load-bearing fact is that a context-based inference is a **reasonable working hypothesis, not a guaranteed, precise definition** — the resulting guess may be approximate, may need refinement as later context provides more evidence, and should be held with appropriate flexibility rather than locked in as a final, certain answer the moment it's formed; good readers actively revise an initial guess when later sentences supply better evidence, and recognize that a context-based inference might benefit from later confirmation (such as checking a dictionary) rather than treating the process as having failed simply because the resulting understanding is somewhat general.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "I'll grab the first nearby word that gives me a clue and build my guess from that."**
The runnable simulation: notice one salient nearby word or phrase and form a meaning guess from it alone, without checking whether the rest of the sentence supports or contradicts that guess. This is a reasonable default for a learner newly attempting inference, since attending to one clear signal is a natural first strategy before the habit of scanning comprehensively is built.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — guessing "gregarious" means "big" from "bounded" alone, then being shown the fuller clue ("bounded up to every visitor") points toward "sociable" instead.
*Shelf-life warning at replacement*: "One clue is a starting point, not the finish line — scan the whole sentence before settling on a guess."

**Intermediate — "I scan the whole sentence for clues now, but once I land on a guess, I treat it as the definite, final answer."**
The simulation: correctly integrates multiple clue types across a sentence, but stops checking once an initial guess is formed, even when later context in the same passage would refine or contradict it.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — guessing "morose" means "tired," then being shown "even after a full night's sleep, he stayed morose," which doesn't fit a sleep-related meaning.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A context-clue guess is a hypothesis, not a locked-in answer — keep checking it against whatever context comes next."

**Advanced — "I gather all available context clues across the sentence or passage, form a reasonable working hypothesis, and stay ready to revise it as further context arrives — treating a context-based guess as approximate unless confirmed."**
The simulation: integrates multiple evidence types, forms an appropriately tentative hypothesis, and actively revises it when later context provides better evidence, recognizing the result may still be approximate. This is the target model, matching TA-4/TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the two direct unlocks (`eng.vocab.homonyms-homophones`, `eng.reading.print-to-meaning`), where this concept's clue-integration-and-revision habit becomes foundational to handling words with multiple possible meanings and to the broader skill of building meaning directly from print.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the general-purpose inference tool. The next steps add specific complications — words that sound alike but mean different things, and the fuller skill of building meaning from print at large."

**Expert — "at full reading fluency, context-clue inference happens largely automatically and continuously during ordinary reading, with explicit, deliberate clue-gathering reserved for genuinely difficult or high-stakes unfamiliar words."**
Named here to mark the arc; this concept's explicit, step-by-step clue-gathering routine is a deliberate scaffold for a process that, at true reading fluency, mostly operates beneath conscious awareness — named here so the tutor understands the routine's eventual goal is automatic, continuous inference, not a permanently maintained conscious procedure.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant failure mechanism is that **grabbing the first salient clue is a genuinely efficient, often-successful shortcut**, so a learner's habit of doing so is not an arbitrary error but a reasonably reinforced strategy that works whenever the first noticed clue happens to align with the fuller sentence's evidence — the failure surfaces specifically when the first-noticed clue is partial or misleading, which is common but not universal, making the shortcut's weakness intermittent and therefore harder to notice without direct comparison.

The second mechanism is that **once real inferential effort has gone into forming a guess, that guess starts to feel earned and final**, creating a natural reluctance to revisit or revise it even when later evidence should prompt reconsideration — this is a general property of effortful inference, not specific to vocabulary, and the Blueprint's own s6_path names it directly as feeling like "indecisiveness" rather than the sign of careful reading it actually is.

The third mechanism, specific to learners whose broader vocabulary or syntax comprehension is still developing (per the Blueprint's own S9 routing), is that **the surrounding context itself may contain additional unfamiliar vocabulary or complex structures**, compounding the target word's own unfamiliarity with a second, independent comprehension difficulty — making it genuinely harder for this population to extract usable clues at all, quite apart from whether the inference strategy itself is sound.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns the trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-GUESS-FROM-ONE-WORD-NEARBY
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-GUESS-FROM-ONE-WORD-NEARBY — includes the "gregarious"/"bounded" and single-word/full-sentence discrimination pairs.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the genuinely efficient strategy of attending to the most salient available clue, extended into treating that single clue as sufficient without checking the rest of the sentence.
- **Teaching consequence of the birth type**: because attending to a salient clue isn't wrong in itself, only incomplete, the repair must **add comprehensive scanning as a supplement, not discard the initial noticing instinct** — the Blueprint's own detective's-clue-board anchor frames this correctly as "gather all the clue cards," not "ignore the first one you noticed."
- **Verification of death**: given a novel sentence where one salient clue would suggest a different meaning than the full sentence's combined evidence, the learner integrates all available clues and produces the fuller-evidence-supported meaning, rather than the single-clue-supported one.

### MC-CONTEXT-CLUES-ALWAYS-GIVE-CERTAINTY
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-CONTEXT-CLUES-ALWAYS-GIVE-CERTAINTY — includes the "morose"/sleep-contradiction and revised-guess/stubborn-guess discrimination pairs.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the reasonable expectation that a completed inference should be a final answer, extended past the genuinely approximate, revisable nature of context-based hypotheses.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct, concrete experience of contradicting later context**, since an abstract claim that "guesses might be wrong" is far less convincing than personally encountering a case where the learner's own confident guess needed revision.
- **Verification of death**: given a passage where later context contradicts an initial guess, the learner revises the guess appropriately, rather than defending the original guess or ignoring the contradicting evidence.

## Analogies

**Best — a detective gathering multiple pieces of evidence before naming a suspect, rather than accusing the first person who looks suspicious.** Jumping to a conclusion from one clue (a single nearby word) risks a wrong verdict; a careful detective assembles fingerprints, witness accounts, and alibis (restatement, contrast, example clues) together before committing to a conclusion — and stays open to revising that conclusion if new evidence emerges later in the investigation.
*Breaking point*: a detective's case eventually reaches a final, closed verdict, while many context-clue inferences remain permanently approximate unless independently confirmed (e.g., by a dictionary) — useful for the multi-evidence-gathering and revision-readiness intuitions, less precise for implying every case reaches full certainty.

**Alternative — assembling a jigsaw puzzle from a partial set of pieces.** A single piece (one clue) rarely shows the whole picture; several pieces together (multiple clue types) reveal a much clearer, more reliable image, and finding a new piece that doesn't fit your current guess (contradicting context) is a signal to reconsider the picture, not to force the piece in anyway.
*Breaking point*: jigsaw pieces have one single correct final arrangement, while a context-clue inference may remain a reasonable approximation rather than a single, precisely "correct" definition — useful for the multi-piece-assembly intuition, less precise for implying a single, fully determinate final answer always exists.

**Visual analogy — the detective's clue board** (Blueprint Component 3): not a metaphor but the actual working instrument this concept's core teaching is organized around, physically representing multiple clue types gathered before a final guess.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Trust your gut — the first clue that jumps out is usually right."** Directly installs MC-GUESS-FROM-ONE-WORD-NEARBY as endorsed strategy, precisely the single-clue shortcut this concept's first misconception consists of.
- **"Once you've figured out what a word means from context, that's the definition — no need to double-check."** Directly installs MC-CONTEXT-CLUES-ALWAYS-GIVE-CERTAINTY by treating a context-based inference as equivalent to a confirmed, precise definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The detective's clue board (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Based on just this one word nearby, what do you think the unfamiliar word means?" Then gather all clue cards and compare the fuller-evidence guess.
2. **The "gregarious"/"bounded" collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-GUESS-FROM-ONE-WORD-NEARBY. *Predict first*: "Does 'bounded' alone tell you the full meaning?" Then check the fuller clue and resolve.
3. **The "morose"/sleep-contradiction collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-CONTEXT-CLUES-ALWAYS-GIVE-CERTAINTY. *Predict first*: "Does 'tired' still fit once we read the next sentence?" Then check and revise together.
4. **The hypothesis-testing-and-revision passage (learner activity, TA-5).** *Predict first*: "What's your first guess for this word's meaning?" Then read on and check whether the guess needs adjusting.

## Discovery Questions

This concept is well suited to **guided discovery for both structural facts**, since the evidence for each (multi-clue integration beats single-clue guessing; revision beats locking in) is directly available through the learner's own clue-gathering and passage-reading attempts.

1. **Need**: "If you only look at one clue nearby an unfamiliar word, how confident can you really be in your guess?"
2. **Playground**: the learner reads several sentences containing unfamiliar words with multiple available clue types, freely forming initial guesses before being told which clues are "official."
3. **Invention**: "Which clues did you actually use, and are there others in the sentence you missed?" The learner re-scans and discovers additional supporting or complicating evidence.
4. **Collision**: present a passage where later context contradicts an earlier guess, and ask whether the guess should be kept or revised.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — gather multiple clues, and treat the result as a revisable hypothesis — from the learner's own evidence.
6. **Compression**: "Gather all the clues, guess carefully, and stay ready to adjust."

## Teaching Sequence

The pedagogical logic behind this arc:

- **The three individual clue types (restatement/synonym — TA-1, contrast/antonym — TA-2, example/illustration — TA-3) are taught separately and in this order**, from the most explicit and direct clue type (a restatement essentially states the meaning outright) to progressively more inferential types (contrast requires reasoning about opposition; example requires reasoning about category membership) — building analytical confidence before harder inferential demands.
- **Whole-sentence integration (TA-4) comes fourth**, directly confronting MC-GUESS-FROM-ONE-WORD-NEARBY only once the learner has separately practiced recognizing each individual clue type, since integration presupposes the learner can already identify each clue type on its own.
- **Hypothesis testing and revision across a passage (TA-5) comes last**, extending the single-sentence integration skill (TA-4) into a multi-sentence context where revision becomes necessary — this ordering matters because MC-CONTEXT-CLUES-ALWAYS-GIVE-CERTAINTY's repair presupposes the learner already has a working hypothesis-forming process (TA-4) to apply and then revise.

Turn-by-turn scripting, protocol routing, and adaptive flags: Blueprint **Component 7 — Session Architecture** and **Component 8 — Adaptive Flags** (in particular the whole-context-integration priority, the hypothesis-and-revise framing, the clue-type vocabulary as a transfer aid, and the surrounding-context-difficulty scaffolding for S9 learners).

## Voice Teaching Notes

This concept's core evidence is **spoken reasoning about textual evidence** — the learner's stated clue-gathering process and the specific evidence cited, not merely the final guessed meaning, since a correct final guess with no stated evidence-gathering cannot be distinguished from a lucky single-clue guess that happened to land correctly.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated scan of multiple sentence elements before committing to a guess** ("it says 'bounded' but also 'every visitor,' so maybe...") — the direct behavioral signature of comprehensive clue-gathering rather than single-clue reliance.
- **Confident, immediate meaning assertion from one salient word, offered with no reference to the rest of the sentence** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-GUESS-FROM-ONE-WORD-NEARBY still active.
- **A visible willingness to revisit and adjust a stated guess when presented with new contradicting context, without defensiveness** ("oh, that changes it — maybe it's more like...") — the target evidence for MC-CONTEXT-CLUES-ALWAYS-GIVE-CERTAINTY's repair, and per the Blueprint's own regulation-tail guidance, this should be explicitly praised as a sign of careful reading, not indecisiveness.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Gather every clue in the sentence before you guess — and stay ready to change your mind if the next sentence tells you more."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads, which the Blueprint does not own:

- **Restatement, contrast, and example clue identification (MP-1, MP-2, MP-3).** *Correct meaning with a cited clue type* → full target evidence; *correct meaning with no cited evidence* → cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess or prior vocabulary knowledge unrelated to this concept's skill.
- **Whole-sentence integration (MP-4).** This concept's most direct test of MC-GUESS-FROM-ONE-WORD-NEARBY; watch specifically for whether the stated reasoning cites more than one piece of evidence from the sentence, not merely whether the final meaning happens to be correct.
- **Explanation (MP-5).** Listen for whether the explanation names both required elements — that context clues should be gathered from the whole surrounding sentence/passage, and that the resulting guess is a working hypothesis, not a guaranteed precise definition.

**Mastery certification trigger**: correct meaning derivation with cited evidence across novel restatement, contrast, and example items; correct, multi-evidence-cited integration on a whole-sentence item; and an explanation naming both the comprehensive-scanning principle and the hypothesis-not-certainty framing. Revision behavior on a genuinely contradicting passage (per TA-5, not separately probed in the mastery set) should be observed and noted qualitatively even though it isn't a scored MP item.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But I already found a clue, isn't that enough?"** — take this as an accurate statement about having found real evidence, not a mistake to be dismissed; respond by adding rather than contradicting: "That's a real clue, and a good one to notice — let's check if the rest of the sentence agrees with it or adds something more."
- **"I don't like not being sure my guess is right"** — a genuine, reasonable discomfort with inferential uncertainty, per the Blueprint's own S6 routing; respond by reframing directly: "A careful, well-reasoned guess that you're ready to adjust is actually a sign of strong reading — even skilled readers treat context clues as a starting point, not a locked-in final answer."
- **The smaller question to shrink to**: from a full multi-clue-type integration task, down to **a single sentence with one, unmistakably clear restatement clue and no contrast or example complexity at all.** This removes the multi-clue-integration demand entirely, isolating the most basic clue-to-meaning derivation before returning to harder, multi-source cases.
- **Never shrink to "just pick whichever meaning sounds most familiar."** This bypasses the actual evidence-gathering process entirely and would not build genuine context-clue skill.

## Memory Hooks

- **Concept type**: inferential reasoning skill combining evidence-integration (comprehensive scanning) with a metacognitive component (holding conclusions as revisable hypotheses).
- **Review form**: spaced items always mixing single-clue-type identification with whole-sentence-integration and revision-required items in the same review cycle, since all three skill layers (identify, integrate, revise) must be maintained together, and reviewing only the easiest layer would understate the skill actually being assessed.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: `eng.vocab.homonyms-homophones`, `eng.reading.print-to-meaning` — both direct unlocks, the first adding the complication of words with multiple possible context-dependent meanings, the second building the broader skill of constructing meaning from print at large.
- **Real-world**: reading unfamiliar technical, academic, or foreign-influenced vocabulary in news articles, workplace documents, or advanced fiction, where looking up every unfamiliar word would be impractical — the direct, practical payoff of a reliable, self-sufficient inference strategy.
- **Expert transfer**: the durable skill is **forming a working hypothesis from partial, multi-source evidence and holding it with appropriate, revisable confidence rather than either refusing to conclude anything or treating an early conclusion as unquestionable** — the same transfer skill needed in forming a preliminary scientific hypothesis from initial observations and revising it as further data arrives, or reaching a provisional diagnosis from initial symptoms while remaining open to revision as further test results come in.
