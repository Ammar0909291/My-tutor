# Multiple-Meaning Words — `eng.vocab.multiple-meaning-words`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.multiple-meaning-words` (canonical English
  KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Multiple-Meaning Words"
  — single words with several distinct but related meanings (polysemy),
  disambiguated by context, taught with an explicit relatedness test
  distinguishing polysemy from homonymy and a context-over-dictionary-
  order discipline.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.homonyms-homophones` — load-bearing
  because this concept's core distinction (polysemy's RELATED multiple
  meanings versus homonymy's typically UNRELATED, coincidental multiple
  meanings) presupposes the learner already has a secure, contrasting
  category (homonymy) to distinguish polysemy from; the prerequisite's
  own context-disambiguation skill is also directly extended here to a
  new case (related senses, not merely different spellings/sounds).
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.vocab.thesaurus-and-dictionary-skills`.
  This concept's context-over-dictionary-listing-order discipline is a
  direct prerequisite for productive, non-naive dictionary use — a
  learner who assumes a dictionary's first-listed sense is automatically
  the intended one cannot yet use a dictionary entry correctly for an
  ambiguous word.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify polysemous (related-meaning) multiple-meaning words and map
   their several senses back to a shared core concept.
2. Distinguish polysemy (related meanings) from homonymy (typically
   unrelated, coincidental meanings) using a conceptual-relatedness
   test, while acknowledging genuinely fuzzy borderline cases.
3. Select the contextually correct meaning of a multiple-meaning word,
   explicitly not defaulting to whichever sense feels most familiar or
   "main."
4. Determine which sense of a dictionary entry's several numbered
   meanings fits a specific sentence's context, regardless of the
   sense's numerical position in the entry.
5. Correctly identify the intended sense of several multiple-meaning
   words encountered within extended, connected text.

## Core Understanding

Multiple-meaning (polysemous) words carry several distinct but
conceptually RELATED meanings, connected by an underlying shared core
concept — "bright" meaning "shining with light" and "bright" meaning
"intelligent" both connect to an underlying idea of clarity or
vividness, and "head" (body part), "head" (leader), and "head" (top of a
page) all connect to a shared "topmost/most important part" concept.
This is a genuinely distinct phenomenon from homonymy, where multiple
meanings under one spelling/sound are typically historically UNRELATED
and coincidental (a "bat" the flying animal has no meaningful connection
to a "bat" used in sports) — though the everyday line between the two
categories can be genuinely fuzzy at the edges, and dictionaries
themselves don't always agree on borderline cases. A second, equally
load-bearing fact is that determining WHICH of a multiple-meaning word's
several senses applies in a given sentence is a job for CONTEXT, not for
dictionary listing order — dictionaries vary in their ordering
convention (some list by historical/etymological order, others by
frequency), so the first-listed sense is not automatically the most
common or most relevant one for a specific context, and readers must
always check the surrounding sentence to determine the intended meaning
rather than defaulting to whichever sense a dictionary happens to list
first.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "a word with multiple meanings is basically the
  same thing as a homonym — one word-form, several unrelated
  definitions"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept
  exists to correct — a natural extension of the prerequisite concept's
  own homonymy category, since both phenomena share the surface feature
  of "one word-form, multiple meanings," giving no built-in signal that
  the meanings' internal relatedness differs between the two categories.
- **Intermediate model — "I can map a polysemous word's several senses
  back to a shared core concept"**: installed by TA-1's meaning-mapping
  practice, using the Component 3 meaning-web anchor to make the
  conceptual connection visually concrete. Upgrade trigger: correctly
  articulating the shared core concept linking two senses of a fresh
  polysemous word without being told what the connection is.
- **Advanced model — "polysemy (related meanings) and homonymy
  (typically unrelated meanings) are distinguishable using a
  conceptual-relatedness test, even though some cases are genuinely
  fuzzy"**: installed by TA-2's polysemy-vs-homonymy sorting task,
  resolving MC-MULTIPLE-MEANING-WORDS-ARE-JUST-HOMONYMS through the
  felt contrast between "bright" (clearly related senses) and "bat"
  (clearly unrelated senses). Upgrade trigger: correctly reasoning
  through a genuinely borderline case (like "bank," per MP-2) using the
  relatedness test, rather than either forcing a confident answer or
  refusing to engage with the ambiguity.
- **Expert model — "I use context, not dictionary listing position, to
  select the correct sense of a multiple-meaning word, including when
  reading a numbered dictionary entry"**: installed by TA-3's context-
  selection practice and TA-4's dictionary-entry practice, resolving
  MC-DICTIONARY-LISTS-MEANINGS-IN-IMPORTANCE-ORDER. This is the
  concept's genuine mastery bar, and TA-5's extended-text application
  demonstrates it operating fluently across multiple words in a single
  passage.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 learners who rely heavily on dictionary lookups, or whose L1
  dictionary conventions differ from English ones, MC-DICTIONARY-LISTS-
  MEANINGS-IN-IMPORTANCE-ORDER is a particularly relevant, practical
  hazard — these learners are especially prone to defaulting to the
  first-listed sense and should receive prioritized TA-3/TA-4 context-
  first practice rather than assuming the general population's typical
  exposure level to this issue.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of adjacent, already-secure concepts. The polysemy-as-
homonymy failure exists because both categories genuinely share the
surface structure of "one word-form, multiple meanings" — the
prerequisite concept's own homonymy category is the most recent and
salient prior experience of this surface pattern, so the learner
reasonably assimilates polysemy into that same category, with nothing
in ordinary exposure marking the internal-relatedness distinction until
directly contrasted (bright/bright versus bat/bat). The dictionary-
order-as-importance failure exists because many other reference
materials genuinely DO list their most important or common item first
(a "top 10" list, a most-frequently-asked-questions page), so a learner
reasonably extrapolates this common convention onto dictionaries
specifically, with nothing in ordinary dictionary use — which rarely
requires comparing entry order against actual sentence context —
marking that dictionary ordering conventions vary and don't reliably
track contextual relevance.

## Misconceptions

**MC-MULTIPLE-MEANING-WORDS-ARE-JUST-HOMONYMS (Blueprint Component 1)
— Type 3, language contamination**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a Type 3 signature — the prerequisite
  concept's own correctly-functioning homonymy category (a distinct,
  properly-learned phenomenon) intrudes into this new domain, where a
  structurally different but surface-similar phenomenon (polysemy)
  actually operates; the learner is not misapplying a wrong rule, but
  rather correctly applying a genuinely-learned category from an
  adjacent concept into a context where a different category actually
  fits better.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when given a fresh
  multiple-meaning word, spontaneously asks "are these meanings actually
  connected, or just coincidental?" before classifying it is showing the
  relatedness test has generalized beyond the specific TA-2 practice
  pairs.

**MC-DICTIONARY-LISTS-MEANINGS-IN-IMPORTANCE-ORDER (Blueprint Component
1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — many reference
  materials genuinely DO order their content by importance or frequency
  (a common, reasonable pattern the learner has encountered elsewhere),
  so the learner extends this general reference-material convention
  onto dictionaries specifically, with nothing in typical dictionary
  use — which rarely stress-tests entry order against actual sentence
  context — marking that dictionary ordering conventions vary by
  publisher and don't reliably indicate contextual relevance.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when consulting a
  dictionary entry, spontaneously scans past the first sense to check
  whether a LATER sense better fits the context, before committing to an
  answer, is showing the context-over-order discipline has generalized
  beyond the specific TA-4 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the meaning-web vs. coincidence-pair sort itself is
  the analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): drawing
  a "meaning web" connecting several senses of a polysemous word back to
  one shared core concept, contrasted with a true homonym pair for
  which no such connecting web is possible, directly embodies the
  relatedness test. Breaking point: a clean, drawable web implies every
  case sorts unambiguously into "connected" or "not connected," which
  can understate that some cases (like "bank," per MP-2) are genuinely
  debatable even among linguists and lexicographers — this entry treats
  the meaning-web anchor as the introductory frame for clear cases, with
  MP-2 explicitly testing the learner's ability to reason through a
  genuinely fuzzy borderline case rather than force a confident verdict.
- **Alternative — a family tree vs. two strangers who share a
  surname**: a polysemous word's senses are like descendants of one
  common ancestor (genuinely, traceably related, even if quite different
  from each other now); a homonym pair is like two unrelated people who
  happen to share the same surname purely by coincidence, with no actual
  family connection. Breaking point: useful for reinforcing the
  relatedness-vs-coincidence distinction memorably, but should not be
  extended to suggest polysemous senses are always as cleanly
  traceable as a documented family tree — some polysemous connections
  are more intuitively felt than historically documentable.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe dictionary entry order as
  "arbitrary" or "meaningless"**: framing dictionary ordering as
  entirely random risks overcorrecting into a different error — entry
  order does follow SOME systematic convention (historical or
  frequency-based) within a given dictionary, it simply isn't a
  reliable guide to which sense fits a SPECIFIC sentence's context; the
  correct frame is "systematic but not context-predictive," not
  "meaningless."

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the meaning-web vs. coincidence-pair sort)**: the
  Blueprint's Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: the meaning web can be drawn on any paper or
  described verbally; any physical or online dictionary supplies real
  TA-4-style entry-order-versus-context practice.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-3's context-selection task and
  TA-5's extended-text application are both hands-on, requiring the
  learner to actively determine the correct sense, not merely recognize
  a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (relatedness contrast)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether
  "shining" and "intelligent" (both senses of "bright") share any
  conceptual connection, unlike a flying mammal and sports equipment —
  making the relatedness distinction directly and memorably visible.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  polysemy/homonymy classification (TA-2) or a context-based sense
  selection (TA-3/TA-4), ask the learner to state their own judgment
  before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
relatedness test and context-over-order discipline are both genuine,
directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery: **need** (the
learner, having secured homonymy, is asked whether "bright" (shining)
and "bright" (smart) are "the same kind of thing" as "bat"/"bat");
**playground/invention** (TA-1's meaning-mapping across several
polysemous words lets the learner notice a consistent relatedness
pattern); **collision** (the Blueprint's own bright/bat comparison
breaks the just-homonyms assumption; the river-bank dictionary-order
probe breaks the importance-order assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's
own-words statement of both insights); **compression** (TA-5's fluent
sense-selection across an extended passage without each word's
relatedness or ordering separately re-derived). The specific terms
"polysemy" and "homonymy" are convention-bound linguistic terminology
best introduced by direct instruction once the learner has already felt
the underlying relatedness distinction through the anchor and TA-1–2.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (identifying related, polysemous multiple meanings) is placed
first as the concept's confirmatory baseline, giving the learner secure
practice mapping a word's senses to a shared core concept before any
contrast with homonymy is introduced. TA-2 (contrasting polysemy with
homonymy) follows immediately, directly targeting MC-MULTIPLE-MEANING-
WORDS-ARE-JUST-HOMONYMS — per the Blueprint's own session architecture,
this conflict-evidence probe is placed right after TA-2, once the
learner has both a secure polysemy example (TA-1) and the prerequisite's
own homonymy knowledge to contrast against. TA-3 (using context to
select the correct meaning) is placed next, directly targeting
MC-DICTIONARY-LISTS-MEANINGS-IN-IMPORTANCE-ORDER — sequenced after the
relatedness distinction is secure because context-based sense selection
applies regardless of whether a word's multiple meanings are polysemous
or homonymous, making it a natural next skill layer rather than
dependent on the TA-1/TA-2 distinction. TA-4 (checking dictionary
entries without assuming order = importance) extends TA-3's context-
selection skill to the specific, practically important case of reading
an actual numbered dictionary entry. TA-5 (multiple-meaning words in
extended text) is placed last as the concept's integration capstone,
requiring the learner to apply context-based sense selection fluently
across several words within a single connected passage. This entry does
not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script
(Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously checks for
  conceptual relatedness (rather than assuming homonymy by default) when
  meeting a fresh multiple-meaning word — this is the primary
  diagnostic signal for whether TA-2's relatedness test has genuinely
  generalized beyond the specific practice pairs.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief, thoughtful pause before
  classifying a genuinely borderline case (like "bank," per MP-2),
  followed by a reasoned judgment acknowledging the ambiguity, is a good
  sign — it suggests the learner is engaging with the relatedness test
  honestly rather than forcing a confident but unjustified answer.
  Instant, confident classification of every case with no
  acknowledgment that some are genuinely debatable is a subtler
  concerning pattern worth gentle correction, distinct from outright
  misclassification.
- **Dictionary-reliance stakes**: real and practically significant, per
  the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags — for dictionary-dependent
  learners especially, MC-DICTIONARY-LISTS-MEANINGS-IN-IMPORTANCE-ORDER
  is a genuinely common, practical hazard; a learner who defaults to a
  dictionary's first-listed sense without checking context should be
  redirected immediately and explicitly to the context-check discipline.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does the context actually support that
  meaning, or did you just pick whichever sense came to mind first?" is
  this concept's load-bearing sentence for MC-DICTIONARY-LISTS-
  MEANINGS-IN-IMPORTANCE-ORDER, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh multiple-meaning word appears in context, making the
  context-check a routinely-invited response rather than an
  afterthought.

- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for MC-MULTIPLE-

  should be named and praised explicitly, per the Blueprint's own
  regulation-tail guidance (praising checking context and relatedness

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework — a relatedness-classification test
(polysemy vs. homonymy) combined with a context-based sense-selection
procedure, applied to genuinely novel words and sentences; mastery is
demonstrated by correctly reasoning through unfamiliar cases, including
genuinely borderline ones (MP-2), and by selecting contextually
appropriate senses (MP-3/MP-4) rather than by recognizing a fixed,
previously-taught example list. Interleaving partners: mix relatedness-
classification-only items (testing TA-1/TA-2) with context-selection
items (testing TA-3/TA-4 together) in ongoing review, so the learner
continues exercising both the classification skill and the practical
disambiguation skill as distinct, separately-verified competencies,
with periodic exposure to genuinely fuzzy borderline cases to maintain
appropriate epistemic humility.

  such words are pervasive throughout ordinary English text.
