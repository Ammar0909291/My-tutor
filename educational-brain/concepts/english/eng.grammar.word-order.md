# Basic Word Order — `eng.grammar.word-order`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.word-order` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Basic Word Order" — the
  canonical Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) word order of English declarative
  sentences, and the conventional positions of adjectives and adverbs
  within that order.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.subject-and-predicate` — load-bearing
  because SVO order depends directly on the subject/predicate split
  already secured; a learner cannot reason about WHERE the subject and
  object belong relative to the verb without already reliably
  identifying which part of a sentence is the subject and which is the
  predicate.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.sentence-types-by-function`,
  `eng.grammar.phrases`. Recognizing sentence types by function
  (declarative, interrogative, imperative, exclamatory) depends on
  first having a secure baseline SVO order to recognize DEVIATIONS from
  (e.g., inverted order signaling a question); phrase-level analysis
  depends on understanding how words group and order themselves within
  the larger SVO frame this concept establishes.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify Subject-Verb-Object order in simple declarative sentences
   and confirm each noun's grammatical role (doer vs. receiver) based
   on its position.
2. Demonstrate that scrambling a sentence's word order changes or
   reverses its meaning, even when every individual word stays the
   same.
3. Correctly place adjectives before the noun they modify.
4. Correctly place manner/degree adverbs after the verb+object, rather
   than splitting the verb from its object.
5. Identify and correct sentences with scrambled SVO order or misplaced
   adjectives/adverbs, restoring standard English word order.

## Core Understanding

English is a WORD-ORDER-DEPENDENT language: unlike languages that use
case-marking endings on nouns to signal grammatical roles regardless of
position, English relies on POSITION itself to tell the listener who
did what to whom. In a simple declarative sentence, the noun BEFORE the
verb is understood as the subject/doer, and the noun AFTER the verb is
understood as the object/receiver — this is the Subject-Verb-Object
(SVO) pattern. Scrambling this order doesn't just sound awkward, it
genuinely changes or reverses the sentence's meaning ("the dog bit the
cat" and "the cat bit the dog" use identical words but mean opposite
things). This word-order dependency extends beyond the core SVO
skeleton to modifying words too: English has specific, conventional
positions for adjectives (typically directly BEFORE the noun they
modify: "the red house," not "the house red") and for manner/degree
adverbs (typically AFTER the verb+object, not splitting them apart: "I
like chocolate very much," not "I like very much chocolate"). Both the
core SVO skeleton and these modifier-placement conventions are genuine,
learnable, checkable rules — not stylistic preferences or arbitrary
restrictions.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "word order in English is flexible, or at least as
  flexible as I'm used to, and descriptive words can go wherever feels
  natural"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists
  to correct — a natural default for learners whose L1 uses case-
  marking morphology (word endings) rather than position to signal
  grammatical roles, or whose L1 places adjectives/adverbs in different
  conventional positions.
- **Intermediate model — "I identify SVO order and understand that
  scrambling it reverses meaning"**: installed by TA-1 and TA-2, using
  the Component 3 word-order train-cars anchor, resolving
  MC-WORD-ORDER-IS-FLEXIBLE-LIKE-OTHER-LANGUAGES. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly predicting, without prompting, that swapping a fresh
  sentence's subject and object will reverse its meaning, not merely
  sound odd.
- **Advanced model — "I place adjectives before the noun and adverbs
  after the verb+object"**: installed by TA-3 and TA-4, beginning to
  resolve MC-ADJECTIVES-AND-ADVERBS-CAN-GO-ANYWHERE by establishing both
  specific, concrete conventional positions.
- **Expert model — "I identify and correct scrambled or misordered
  sentences across all three dimensions (SVO, adjective placement,
  adverb placement)"**: installed by TA-5, the concept's genuine mastery
  bar — applying the full word-order discipline diagnostically to
  flawed sentences, not merely producing correctly-ordered sentences
  from scratch.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 has flexible word order due to case-marking
  morphology (Russian, Turkish, Japanese, Korean, Latin) or different
  default adjective/adverb positions (French/Spanish postnominal
  adjectives), expect this to be one of the most significant, well-
  documented structural transfer challenges in the whole curriculum —
  dedicate substantially more practice time than the standard session
  suggests, not a proportionally similar amount to other grammar
  concepts.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and the Blueprint
itself explicitly names this as one of the most persistent, structural
transfer challenges for a large portion of L2 learners. The word-order-
is-flexible failure exists because many world languages genuinely DO use
case-marking endings rather than strict position to signal grammatical
roles, making flexible order a completely reasonable expectation
transferred from the learner's L1 — nothing marks English's specific
position-dependency until a learner is directly shown that reversing
"the boy threw the ball" to "the ball threw the boy" doesn't just sound
odd, it grammatically claims the ball did the throwing. The adjectives-
and-adverbs-can-go-anywhere failure exists because many languages place
descriptive words in different conventional positions (adjectives after
nouns in several Romance languages, different adverb-placement
conventions elsewhere), making the learner's L1 placement instinct a
reasonable starting point — nothing marks English's specific
conventions until a learner directly produces a sentence like "I like
very much chocolate" and is shown it splits the verb from its object in
a way that sounds distinctly unnatural to a fluent English speaker.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.word-order.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions
this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-WORD-ORDER-IS-FLEXIBLE-LIKE-OTHER-LANGUAGES (Blueprint Component
1) — Type 3, language contamination**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 3 signature, not Type 1 —
  this misconception is not a reasonable extension from English's own
  patterns, but a direct transfer from the learner's L1 grammatical
  system (case-marking morphology that permits flexible word order
  regardless of position); the misconception survives because it
  reflects a genuinely different, internally consistent grammatical
  strategy the learner already has full command of in another
  language, not a guess or partial rule.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a fresh
  scrambled-order sentence, spontaneously identifies which reading a
  fluent English speaker would default to (based purely on position,
  even if it produces a nonsensical claim) before being told is showing
  the position-carries-meaning discipline has been genuinely
  internalized.

**MC-ADJECTIVES-AND-ADVERBS-CAN-GO-ANYWHERE (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 3, language contamination**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 3 signature — this reflects a
  direct transfer from the learner's L1 conventional word-placement
  patterns (postnominal adjectives, different adverb-positioning rules),
  not a guess or an overextended English rule; the misconception
  survives because the L1 placement pattern is itself fully
  grammatical and natural-sounding in that language, giving the learner
  no internal signal that English requires something different.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when producing a
  fresh sentence with both a descriptive adjective and a manner adverb,
  spontaneously places the adjective before its noun and the adverb
  after the verb+object without being told is showing the placement-
  convention discipline has generalized beyond the specific TA-3/TA-4
  practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the word-order train cars itself is the analogy, not
  a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically arranging
  "SUBJECT," "VERB," "OBJECT" cars into correct order, then scrambling
  them and observing the meaning completely flip, directly embodies
  word order as a physically manipulable, meaning-determining sequence
  rather than an abstract positional rule. Breaking point: the train-
  car anchor demonstrates the core SVO dependency well but doesn't on
  its own address adjective/adverb placement (TA-3/TA-4), which needs
  its own separate, dedicated demonstration once the core skeleton is
  secure.
- **Alternative — a road with fixed lanes vs. an open field**: English
  sentences are like a road with fixed, meaningful lanes (subject lane,
  verb lane, object lane) — being in the wrong lane changes your
  destination, not just your route; a case-marking language is more
  like an open field where markers (not position) tell you where
  everyone is headed regardless of where they're standing. Breaking
  point: useful for the position-determines-meaning insight, but
  doesn't address the adjective/adverb-specific placement conventions,
  which need the train-car anchor's own dedicated extension.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT frame English's SVO rigidity as "stricter" or
  "less expressive" than flexible-word-order languages**: this framing
  risks discouraging L1-transfer learners by implying their native
  language's flexibility is a superior feature English lacks; instead,
  frame SVO rigidity as English's OWN internally consistent strategy
  for signaling grammatical roles — a genuine structural feature that
  compensates for the absence of case-marking endings, not a
  deficiency.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the word-order train cars)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any simple sentence about a real action the
  learner performed (a pet, a chore, a game) supplies real TA-1/TA-2
  material for SVO identification and scrambling; any object the
  learner can describe supplies real TA-3 adjective-placement material.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-5's error-correction task
  (identifying and repairing scrambled SVO order or misplaced
  adjectives/adverbs) is inherently hands-on diagnostic practice, not
  mere recognition of a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the who-did-it test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking, for "The
  ball threw the boy," who the sentence's WORD ORDER says did the
  throwing (regardless of what makes logical sense), making the
  position-carries-meaning rule directly audible and testable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a word-
  order identification, placement, or correction attempt (TA-1 through
  TA-5), ask the learner to state their own attempt before the tutor
  confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions, with extra
weight given to the Blueprint's own explicit flag that L1-transfer
learners need substantial, sustained practice beyond a single discovery
moment.** The position-carries-meaning insight and the specific
adjective/adverb placement conventions are both genuine, directly
testable phenomena well-suited to guided discovery: **need** (the
learner, having secured TA-1's basic SVO identification, is asked what
"the ball threw the boy" means, discovering their instinct to interpret
it logically — "the boy threw the ball" — conflicts with what English
word order actually signals); **playground/invention** (TA-1's basic
SVO practice builds initial confidence with correctly-ordered sentences
before the scrambled contrast is introduced); **collision** (the
Blueprint's own "the ball threw the boy" probe breaks the flexible-
order assumption; the "I like very much chocolate" probe breaks the
place-anywhere assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's own-words
statement of both the position-carries-meaning principle and the
adjective/adverb placement conventions); **compression** (TA-5's fluent
error-correction across all three dimensions, applied directly without
each instance separately re-derived). The specific terms "Subject-Verb-
Object," "SVO," and "case-marking" are convention-bound grammatical
terminology best introduced by direct instruction once the learner has
already felt the underlying distinctions through the train-car anchor
and TA-1–2.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (basic SVO order in simple sentences) is placed first as the
concept's most stable, concrete entry point, per the Blueprint's own
train-car anchor — establishing correct order recognition before any
scrambled contrast is introduced. TA-2 (scrambled order meaning-change
demonstration) follows immediately, directly targeting
MC-WORD-ORDER-IS-FLEXIBLE-LIKE-OTHER-LANGUAGES — per the Blueprint's own
session architecture, this conflict-evidence probe is placed right
after TA-2, once the learner has secure correct-order practice to
contrast the scrambled version against. TA-3 (adjective placement)
introduces a SECOND, independent dimension (modifier positioning, not
core SVO structure), building on the now-secure core skeleton. TA-4
(adverb placement) extends the same modifier-placement dimension,
directly targeting MC-ADJECTIVES-AND-ADVERBS-CAN-GO-ANYWHERE. TA-5
(correcting scrambled or misordered sentences) is placed last as the
concept's integration capstone, requiring the learner to diagnostically
apply all three word-order disciplines (SVO, adjective placement,
adverb placement) to flawed sentences. This entry does not restate the
Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see
Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously identifies
  the word-order-determined subject of a fresh scrambled sentence
  (rather than the logically sensible one), and whether the learner
  spontaneously places a fresh adjective before its noun and a fresh
  manner adverb after the verb+object without prompting — these are the
  primary diagnostic signals for whether TA-2 and TA-3/TA-4's
  disciplines have genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice
  items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before answering "who
  did it" for a scrambled sentence, followed by the word-order-
  determined (not logically sensible) answer, suggests the learner is
  consciously applying the position rule rather than defaulting to
  logic — a good sign. Instant, confident logical (not positional)
  interpretation of a scrambled sentence is the concerning pattern for
  MC-WORD-ORDER-IS-FLEXIBLE-LIKE-OTHER-LANGUAGES.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, and flagged as needing the most
  substantial dedicated practice in the whole Blueprint — for S9
  learners from case-marking or different-word-order L1 backgrounds,
  this is one of the most persistent, well-documented L2 English
  challenges across ALL proficiency levels, not just early stages;
  budget significantly more practice time than the standard session
  suggests.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "who does the word order say did it,
  even if it doesn't make logical sense?" is this concept's load-
  bearing sentence for
  MC-WORD-ORDER-IS-FLEXIBLE-LIKE-OTHER-LANGUAGES, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh scrambled or ambiguous-order sentence is introduced,
  making the position check a routinely-invited response rather than
  an afterthought.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a word-order identification or correction task
  shrinks to the Component 3 train-cars anchor using the simplest, most
  familiar correctly-ordered sentence available, with the tutor
  modeling the SVO arrangement before the learner attempts a fresh
  scrambled or modifier-placement sentence independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for
  MC-WORD-ORDER-IS-FLEXIBLE-LIKE-OTHER-LANGUAGES, producing or
  interpreting a scrambled-order sentence logically rather than
  positionally is more likely than "I don't know" — route directly to
  the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence probe rather than treating it
  as a generic comprehension gap, and explicitly validate the L1-
  transfer origin per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags rather than
  treating it as a careless error. For
  MC-ADJECTIVES-AND-ADVERBS-CAN-GO-ANYWHERE, misplacing a modifier
  according to L1 conventions is more likely than confusion — route
  directly to TA-3/TA-4's placement-convention practice.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously self-corrects a misordered sentence mid-production
  ("The ball — I mean, the boy threw the ball") before being told is
  showing the position-carries-meaning discipline has generalized —
  this should be named and praised explicitly, per the Blueprint's own
  regulation-tail guidance (praising checking word order and placement
  rules over relying on L1 intuition).

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedural/framework — a position-based grammatical-
role signaling system (SVO) combined with two modifier-placement
conventions (adjective-before-noun, adverb-after-verb+object), applied
to genuinely novel sentences; mastery is demonstrated by correctly
identifying, producing, and correcting fresh, previously-unseen
sentences (MP-1 through MP-4, and TA-5's diagnostic correction) rather
than by recognizing a fixed, previously-taught example list.
Interleaving partners: mix core-SVO items (testing TA-1/TA-2) with
modifier-placement items (testing TA-3/TA-4) in ongoing review, so the
learner continues exercising both the foundational and the extension
dimensions of English word order.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: identifying SVO order and correctly placing adjectives/
  adverbs in fresh sentences; correcting fresh scrambled or misordered
  sentences across all three dimensions.
- **Far**: this concept's SVO framework is the direct baseline
  `eng.grammar.sentence-types-by-function` depends on to recognize order
  DEVIATIONS (such as subject-auxiliary inversion) as signals of a
  different sentence type; it is also the direct frame
  `eng.grammar.phrases` builds on for phrase-level word-grouping
  analysis within the larger sentence structure.
- **Real-world**: every sentence the learner produces — in speech or
  writing — depends on correct SVO order and modifier placement to
  convey the intended meaning clearly and avoid genuine ambiguity or
  reversal of meaning.
- **Expert-transfer**: clear, unambiguous communication at any advanced
  level depends on reliable SVO production and correct modifier
  placement without conscious deliberation, since word-order errors
  (especially scrambled core structure or misplaced adverbs splitting a
  verb from its object) remain among the most immediately noticeable
  markers of non-native production, even at otherwise advanced
  proficiency, consistent with the Blueprint's own framing of this as
  one of the most persistent transfer challenges.
