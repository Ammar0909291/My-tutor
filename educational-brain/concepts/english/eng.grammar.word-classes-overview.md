# Overview of Word Classes — `eng.grammar.word-classes-overview`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.word-classes-overview` (canonical
  English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Overview of Word
  Classes" — the domain's zero-prerequisite entry node, introducing the
  eight traditional parts of speech as functional categories determined
  by a word's grammatical role in a specific sentence, not by a fixed
  label or surface meaning.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.word-recognition` — load-bearing
  because classifying a word's grammatical function presupposes the
  learner already treats that word as a meaning-bearing unit connected
  to known vocabulary, not merely a decodable string; a learner who has
  not yet secured basic word recognition has no stable unit to classify
  in the first place.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.nouns`, `eng.grammar.verbs`,
  `eng.grammar.prepositions`, `eng.grammar.conjunctions`,
  `eng.grammar.interjections`. This concept is the entry point for the
  entire grammar domain — every downstream word-class concept
  presupposes the learner already has the basic eight-category
  framework and, critically, the function-over-meaning classification
  discipline this concept installs.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: remember · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.80 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify nouns (naming words) and verbs (action/being words) in
   simple sentences — the two most foundational word classes.
2. Identify adjectives (describing nouns) and adverbs (describing
   verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs) in sentences.
3. Identify pronouns (standing in for nouns), prepositions (showing
   relationships, often of location/time), and conjunctions (joining
   words or clauses).
4. Correctly classify the same word differently across two sentences,
   based on its grammatical function in each, rather than assuming a
   word has one permanently fixed class.
5. Classify a word using grammatical signals (determiners, tense
   marking, modification target) rather than surface meaning or topic,
   even when meaning might mislead.

## Core Understanding

Word class — the traditional parts of speech (noun, verb, adjective,
adverb, pronoun, preposition, conjunction, interjection) — is
determined by how a word FUNCTIONS in a specific sentence, not by some
fixed label permanently attached to the word. Many common English words
(run, light, fast, cut, book) can serve as different word classes
depending on their role in context: "run" is a verb in "I run every
day" (an action) but a noun in "I went for a run" (a thing/activity
being referred to) — this class-shifting is a standard, expected
feature of English, not a rare exception. A second, equally load-
bearing fact is that word class must be determined by grammatical
FUNCTION and POSITION in a sentence — does it take a determiner like
"the" or "a"? does it show tense? does it modify another word? — not by
whether the underlying concept feels action-like or thing-like:
"arrival" names an event and might feel action-related, but it
functions grammatically as a noun (it takes "the" and acts as a
sentence's subject), while "arrive" is the verb form (it shows tense:
"arrived," "arrives"). Reliable classification always checks the
concrete grammatical signal, never the surface meaning alone.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "each word has one fixed job it always does, and I
  can tell what job a word does just by thinking about what it means"**:
  the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct
  — a natural default since many common words genuinely DO stay in one
  class most of the time, and since meaning is the most immediately
  available cue when a word is first encountered, giving no built-in
  signal that either assumption breaks down in specific, common cases.
- **Intermediate model — "I can identify nouns, verbs, adjectives, and
  adverbs in simple sentences, chunked by function-type"**: installed
  by TA-1's naming/action-word identification and TA-2's describing-
  word identification, using the Component 3 sorting-stations anchor to
  make each class's function concrete. Per the Blueprint's own
  Adaptive Flags, these are deliberately introduced in chunked
  function-groups (naming/action; describing; connecting/substituting)
  rather than all eight classes at once, managing cognitive load.
  Upgrade trigger: correctly sorting a fresh sentence's words into the
  first four classes without confusing noun/verb or adjective/adverb
  roles.
- **Advanced model — "I can also identify pronouns, prepositions, and
  conjunctions — the connecting/substituting classes"**: installed by
  TA-3's practice, completing the eight-class framework in its third
  chunked group. Upgrade trigger: correctly identifying all three
  connecting/substituting classes in a fresh sentence without
  confusing their distinct functions (standing-in-for-a-noun vs.
  showing-relationship vs. joining).
- **Expert model — "word class is determined by function, not fixed
  identity or surface meaning — I can classify the same word
  differently across contexts and use grammatical signals when meaning
  might mislead"**: installed by TA-4's context-dependent
  classification (resolving MC-WORD-CLASS-IS-FIXED-PER-WORD) and TA-5's
  grammatical-signal-detection practice (resolving MC-WORD-CLASS-
  DETERMINED-BY-MEANING-ALONE). This is the concept's genuine mastery
  bar — moving from category memorization to a reliable, function-based
  classification procedure.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 learners whose L1 grammar system organizes word classes very
  differently (e.g., no article system affecting noun-signal
  detection, or a different core parts-of-speech framework), TA-5's
  grammatical-signal detection in particular relies on English-specific
  signals (articles, tense marking) that may not have a direct L1
  parallel — this concept should provide careful, explicit contrastive
  teaching of these specific signals rather than assuming intuitive
  transfer.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of how words are typically first encountered and processed.
The fixed-class-assumption failure exists because a genuine majority of
common words DO stay reliably in one class across most everyday
sentences a learner meets, so the learner reasonably extends "this word
is always this class" from that overwhelmingly consistent early
experience, with nothing marking the class-shifting exception until a
specifically constructed sentence pair (like "I run" vs. "I went for a
run") directly demonstrates it. The meaning-based-classification
failure exists because meaning is the most immediately available,
lowest-effort cue when identifying a word — checking a word's
grammatical signals (determiners, tense marking, sentence position)
requires a further, more deliberate analytical step that meaning-based
judgment simply skips, so a learner reasonably relies on the faster
meaning-based heuristic, with nothing in ordinary reading marking the
specific cases where meaning and grammatical function diverge (like
"arrival" feeling action-related but functioning as a noun) until
directly tested against such a case.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.word-classes-overview.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-WORD-CLASS-IS-FIXED-PER-WORD (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — the large
  majority of common words a learner encounters genuinely DO stay in
  one class consistently, so the learner extends this reliable pattern
  into an unconditional rule, with nothing in typical early sentence
  exposure marking the specific, common class-shifting cases (run,
  light, book) until directly presented as a contrastive pair.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, upon meeting a
  word they've previously classified one way used differently in a new
  sentence, spontaneously says something like "wait, is it doing a
  different job here?" before assuming an error is showing the
  function-over-fixed-label model has generalized beyond the specific
  TA-4 practice pairs.

**MC-WORD-CLASS-DETERMINED-BY-MEANING-ALONE (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 2, perceptual intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a genuine perceptual default rather than
  a wrongly-taught rule — meaning is simply the most immediately
  available, lowest-effort signal when identifying a word, and checking
  grammatical signals (determiners, tense marking, sentence role)
  requires a further deliberate step meaning-based judgment bypasses
  entirely; the learner is applying a reasonable, fast heuristic that
  simply has no built-in check against the cases where it fails.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when classifying a
  tricky word like "arrival," spontaneously checks "does it take 'the'
  in front of it?" before relying on whether the concept feels action-
  like is showing the grammatical-signal-checking discipline has
  generalized beyond the specific TA-5 practice examples.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the word-class sorting stations anchor itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically
  sorting word cards from a cut-up sentence to labeled stations, then
  re-sorting a second sentence reusing one word (like "run") in a
  different station, directly embodies both the eight-category
  framework and the class-shifting insight in one continuous activity.
  Breaking point: physical stations imply each word ultimately belongs
  to exactly one station per instance, which is accurate for a single
  sentence but can obscure that the SAME word-form could sort to a
  different station in a different sentence — this entry treats the
  station-sorting anchor as the introductory frame for both the basic
  categories and the per-sentence, function-based sorting principle
  together.
- **Alternative — an actor playing different roles in different
  productions**: the same actor (word-form) can play a hero in one play
  and a villain in another (verb in one sentence, noun in another) —
  the actor's identity doesn't determine the role; the specific
  production's casting does. Breaking point: useful for reinforcing
  that identity and role are separate, but less concrete and hands-on
  than the physical sorting-stations anchor for building the
  grammatical-signal-checking habit specifically.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe a word's "default" or "primary"
  class as somehow more correct than its other possible classes**:
  framing one class as a word's "real" identity and other classes as
  secondary or borrowed risks reinforcing MC-WORD-CLASS-IS-FIXED-PER-
  WORD by implying a hierarchy exists; every instance of a word in a
  sentence should be classified fresh, by that sentence's function,
  with no class treated as more authoritative than another.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (word-class sorting stations)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: index cards, sticky notes, or simply spoken
  word-by-word sorting substitute for physical stations; any sentence
  from a book, sign, or conversation supplies real TA-1–3 practice.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's context-dependent
  classification and TA-5's grammatical-signal-detection tasks are
  both hands-on, requiring the learner to actively apply the function-
  based test, not merely recognize a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (fixed-class and meaning-based contrasts)**: the tutor
  models the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstrations —
  asking whether "run" is doing a verb's or noun's job in "I went for a
  run," and asking whether "arrival" takes "the" the way nouns do —
  making both distinctions directly and memorably visible.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  word-class identification (TA-1–3) or a context-dependent/signal-
  based classification (TA-4/TA-5), ask the learner to state their own
  judgment before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
function-over-fixed-label principle and the function-over-meaning
principle are both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to
guided discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured word
recognition, is asked whether "run" is doing the same job in "I run
every day" and "I went for a run"); **playground/invention** (TA-1
through TA-3's chunked identification across several sentences lets
the learner build confidence with the basic eight-category framework);
**collision** (the Blueprint's own run/run contrast breaks the fixed-
class assumption; the "does 'arrival' take 'the'?" probe breaks the
meaning-based-classification assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's
own-words statement of both the function-based-classification
principle and the possibility of class-shifting); **compression**
(TA-5's fluent grammatical-signal-based classification without each
signal separately re-derived). The eight specific CLASS LABELS (noun,
verb, adjective, adverb, pronoun, preposition, conjunction,
interjection) are convention-bound terminology best introduced by
direct instruction alongside the sorting-stations anchor, since there
is no regularity to discover in the labels themselves, only in the
functional categories they name.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (naming words vs. action words: nouns and verbs) is placed first
as the concept's most foundational, intuitive pair, per the Blueprint's
own chunked-introduction Adaptive Flag. TA-2 (describing words:
adjectives and adverbs) follows as the second function-group, building
on the now-secure noun/verb foundation since adjectives modify nouns
and adverbs modify verbs (among other targets). TA-3 (connecting and
substituting words: pronouns, prepositions, conjunctions) completes the
eight-category framework as the third, more abstract function-group,
per the Blueprint's own explicit sequencing rationale (S6 protocol)
that these are introduced last to manage cognitive load. TA-4 (context-
dependent classification) is placed next, directly targeting MC-WORD-
CLASS-IS-FIXED-PER-WORD, sequenced after the learner already has secure
practice with all eight categories individually — attempting class-
shifting examples before the categories themselves are secure would
give the learner nothing stable to shift between. TA-5 (grammatical
signal detection) is placed last, directly targeting MC-WORD-CLASS-
DETERMINED-BY-MEANING-ALONE, as the concept's most demanding,
analytically deliberate skill — requiring the learner to override the
faster, meaning-based default with a slower, signal-based check. This
entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script
(Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously checks a
  word's grammatical signal (does it take "the"? does it show tense?)
  rather than defaulting to a meaning-based judgment when classifying a
  fresh, tricky word — this is the primary diagnostic signal for
  whether TA-5's discipline has genuinely generalized beyond the
  specific practice examples.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before classifying a
  word that has been previously seen in a different class, followed by
  a correct, function-based re-classification for the new sentence, is
  a good sign — it suggests the learner is consciously checking THIS
  sentence's function rather than recalling a remembered label.
  Instant, confident misclassification based on a remembered "usual"
  class is the concerning pattern for MC-WORD-CLASS-IS-FIXED-PER-WORD.
- **Cognitive-load stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags — introducing all eight word classes
  simultaneously risks overwhelming the learner; the chunked
  function-group sequence (TA-1/TA-2/TA-3) should be respected even
  under time pressure, rather than compressed into a single
  presentation.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "what job is this word doing in THIS
  sentence?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for both
  misconceptions together, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh word-classification task arises, making the
  function-check a routinely-invited response rather than a default
  fixed-label or meaning-based guess.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a word-class identification task shrinks to the
  Component 3 sorting-stations anchor using the simplest, most familiar
  sentence available, with the tutor modeling the sort before the
  learner attempts a fresh sentence independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for MC-WORD-CLASS-IS-
  FIXED-PER-WORD, confidently misclassifying a class-shifted word based
  on its "usual" class is more likely than "I don't know" — route
  directly to the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence probe rather than
  treating it as a generic classification gap. For MC-WORD-CLASS-

- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously points to or names a specific grammatical signal
  (e.g., "it has 'the' in front of it, so it's a noun") when justifying
  a classification, without being asked to explain, is showing TA-5's
  discipline has generalized — this should be named and praised
  explicitly, per the Blueprint's own regulation-tail guidance
  (praising checking grammatical function over guessing from meaning).

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework — an eight-category classification
system (noun, verb, adjective, adverb, pronoun, preposition,
conjunction, interjection) combined with a function-based
classification procedure (check grammatical signals and sentence role,
not fixed labels or meaning alone), applied to genuinely novel
sentences; mastery is demonstrated by correctly classifying fresh,
potentially class-shifted or meaning-misleading words (MP-4/MP-5)
rather than by recognizing a fixed, previously-taught example set.
Interleaving partners: mix single-category identification items
(testing TA-1/TA-2/TA-3 individually) with context-dependent and
signal-detection items (testing TA-4/TA-5 together) in ongoing review,
so the learner continues exercising both basic categorical recognition
and the harder, function-based judgment as distinct, separately-
verified competencies.
