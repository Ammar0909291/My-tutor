## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Classify a vowel's tongue height (high, mid, low) by feel, contrasting a given pair.
2. Classify a vowel's tongue backness (front, back) by feel.
3. Classify a vowel's lip rounding (rounded, unrounded/spread) by touch or mirror-check.
4. Use the slow-stretch technique to determine whether a vowel is a monophthong (steady) or a diphthong (gliding), and describe a diphthong by its start and end points.
5. Give a complete three-dimension classification (height + backness + rounding) for a monophthong, or a start/end description for a diphthong, without scaffolding.

## Core Understanding

Vowels are produced with **no constriction narrow enough to create the turbulence or blockage that defines consonants** — the airstream flows freely through an open vocal tract — and this absence of closure is precisely what makes vowels *feel* indescribable to a learner arriving from consonant work, where "place" meant a locatable touch-point. The resolution is that vowels have a different, but equally real and equally locatable, articulatory description: the **position of the tongue body within the open mouth**, described along two continuous axes — **height** (how close the tongue body is to the roof of the mouth: high, mid, low) and **backness** (how far forward or back in the mouth the tongue body sits: front, central, back) — plus a third, independent parameter, **lip rounding** (rounded or unrounded/spread), which is not a tongue position at all but adds a further acoustic distinction at a given height-backness position. Together these three parameters define a **continuous two-dimensional space** (traditionally drawn as a quadrilateral, the vowel diagram, with the four "corner" vowels — high-front /iː/, low-front /æ/, high-back /uː/, low-back /ɑː/ — anchoring the most extreme, most easily felt positions), and every vowel occupies a point or, for the second major category, a **path** within it. Most English vowels are **monophthongs**: the tongue holds one steady position for the vowel's entire duration. A genuine subset are **diphthongs**: the tongue and/or lips *glide* from one position toward another within a single vowel nucleus, as one continuous, unsegmented sound (the glide is not two vowels in sequence — /aɪ/ in "time" is one phoneme, not /a/ followed by /ɪ/). The height/backness/rounding system and the monophthong/diphthong distinction are the two independent pieces of content this concept must establish, and confusing "vowels have no place" with "vowels have no describable structure at all" is the single most consequential error to prevent.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "vowels are just open mouth noises; there's nothing specific happening."**
The runnable simulation: to distinguish vowels, listen to how they sound overall, with no attention to any physical correlate — this is a reasonable extrapolation *from* the consonant work just completed, where "place" meant a felt point of contact, and vowels have no such point. The model is not wrong that vowels lack closure; it is wrong that lacking closure means lacking a describable position at all.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — saying "ee" then "ah" and attending specifically to tongue height and frontness rather than to the overall sound.
*Shelf-life warning at replacement*: "There's no touch-point for vowels like there was for consonants. There is still a real tongue position — you just have to feel it a different way, by height and by front/back."

**Intermediate — "vowels have a felt tongue position, and I can compare two vowels on one dimension at a time."**
The simulation: given a pair, decide whether they differ mainly in height, or mainly in backness, or mainly in rounding — one axis at a time, matching the Blueprint's own TA-1/TA-2/TA-3 progression. This model is genuinely productive and is the necessary step before combination, but on its own it treats the axes as separate comparison tasks rather than as co-ordinates of one point.
*Upgrade trigger*: asked to fully classify a single vowel (not compare a pair) — the learner must now supply all three values at once rather than judging one relative difference.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Comparing two vowels on one thing at a time is a great way to learn the axes. A single vowel's full description needs all three, together."

**Advanced — "a vowel is a point (or a path, for diphthongs) in a continuous height/backness space, with rounding as an extra parameter — and monophthongs versus diphthongs is a question about whether that point moves."**
The simulation: any vowel, familiar or not, is located as a coordinate (or a start-to-end trajectory) using the slow-stretch technique to determine motion and the corner-vowel landmarks to calibrate position. This is the target model, matching TA-4 and TA-5's combination and diphthong-integration tasks.
*Upgrade trigger*: encountering a vowel system with more distinctions than English makes (many languages distinguish vowel length independently of quality, or have front rounded vowels English lacks, like French /y/) — the space extends; the specific English inventory does not.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This mouth-space has more positions than English actually uses. Other languages fill in different spots — including some English leaves completely empty."

**Expert — "vowel quality is a continuous, gradient phonetic space that individual languages carve into a discrete number of contrastive categories, and those category boundaries are language-particular and somewhat arbitrary."**
The learner recognises that the height/backness/rounding grid is a simplification of a genuinely continuous articulatory-acoustic space (formant frequencies, precisely), and that the number and placement of category boundaries within that space is a fact about a specific language's phonology, not a universal given. Named here to mark where this arc leads; it belongs to advanced phonetics and dialectology.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant and most specific failure mechanism is a **direct, structurally-caused carryover from the immediately preceding work**: a learner who has just spent significant time on consonants, where "place" meant a discrete, locatable point of contact, arrives at vowels expecting the same kind of evidence and, finding none, concludes there is nothing to find — rather than concluding the evidence takes a different, gradient form. This is not a failure of attention or effort; it is a reasonable but incorrect generalisation from a genuinely different case, and the Blueprint's adaptive flags correctly identify that this must be countered *upfront*, before any classification work begins, rather than allowed to surface and then be corrected.

The second mechanism is that **height and backness are genuinely gradient, not categorical**, in a way place-of-articulation for consonants mostly is not — English distinguishes several vowel heights and several degrees of frontness/backness along what is physically a smooth continuum, and the boundaries between adjacent categories (mid-front /ɛ/ versus low-front /æ/, for instance) are subtler and less perceptually crisp than the sharp place contrasts among consonants. The Blueprint's own S6 protocol routing names this explicitly and correctly: the difficulty is real, not a sign the learner is failing at something that should be easy.

The third mechanism is **treating diphthongs as single, static targets** — a monophthong-shaped mental model applied to what is, physically, a gliding motion — because a diphthong genuinely does function as a single vowel phonologically (occupying one syllable nucleus, one "vowel slot" in a word), even though it is dynamically two-target physically. The learner's phonological intuition ("this is one vowel sound") and the physical reality ("this is actually a movement between two positions") point in different directions, and resolving that tension requires the specific slow-stretch technique — ordinary-speed listening cannot resolve it, because at normal speed a glide and a steady vowel are both perceived, correctly, as "one sound."

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns the trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-VOWELS-HAVE-NO-ARTICULATION-PLACE
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-VOWELS-HAVE-NO-ARTICULATION-PLACE — includes the /iː/-vs-/ɑː/ and /uː/-vs-/iː/ discrimination pairs.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), and specifically an overgeneralisation from the immediately preceding concept's own correct content — "no closure to feel" (true) generalised to "no position to feel" (false). This is a particularly clean case of a misconception whose seed is a *correct* fact from the adjacent concept, extended one step too far.
- **Teaching consequence of the birth type**: because the source belief (no closure = no locatable touch-point) is true and should not be undermined, the repair must **introduce a genuinely new kind of evidence** rather than argue the learner out of something they observed correctly — the Blueprint's height/backness/rounding framework is exactly this: a real, felt, gradient position, described with different instruments (relative height and frontness rather than a touch-point) than consonant place used.
- **Verification of death**: given two vowels that are close in the vowel space but not identical (a mid-front versus a slightly-higher-front vowel, rather than the maximally-contrasting corner pairs used in initial teaching), the learner attempts a height/backness judgement rather than defaulting to "they just sound different" — evidence the *method* has transferred beyond the easy contrastive cases it was taught on.

### MC-MONOPHTHONGS-AND-DIPHTHONGS-ARE-THE-SAME
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-MONOPHTHONGS-AND-DIPHTHONGS-ARE-THE-SAME.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), reinforced by genuine phonological structure — at normal speaking speed, a diphthong is correctly perceived as a single, unified vowel sound (it occupies one syllable slot), so the learner's ordinary listening experience gives no natural cue that anything is moving. This is not a careless error; it is what accurate perception at normal speed actually delivers.
- **Teaching consequence**: because normal-speed perception genuinely cannot resolve this, the repair depends entirely on the **slow-stretch technique** as an instrument that changes the evidence available, not on convincing the learner to listen more carefully at normal speed (which would not help, since the information genuinely is not separable at that speed). This is analogous to needing a slowed-down recording to see that a fast-moving object's path is curved rather than straight — the technique is not optional scaffolding, it is the only way to access the relevant evidence.
- **Verification of death**: given a novel word, the learner independently reaches for the slow-stretch technique *before* answering monophthong-or-diphthong, without being told to use it — evidence the technique itself, not just a specific memorised list of which words contain diphthongs, has been internalised.

## Analogies

**Best — the mouth as an open room, and the tongue as a person standing in it.** Height is how close to the ceiling the person stands; backness is how close to the back wall. No walls to touch (no closure), but a definite, describable position in the room all the same. This directly targets MC-VOWELS-HAVE-NO-ARTICULATION-PLACE by supplying spatial position without requiring contact, exactly matching the physical reality.
*Breaking point*: a person in a room can stand still or walk — useful, in fact, for extending to diphthongs (a person walking from one spot toward another) rather than a weakness, but be explicit that most vowels are the "standing still" case and diphthongs are the exception, not vice versa.

**Alternative — a compass and an elevation map combined.** Backness is like east-west position, height is like elevation; every vowel is a specific grid reference. Strong for learners who respond well to spatial/graphical framing and maps directly onto the vowel-quadrilateral diagram the Blueprint's Component 3 anchor uses.
*Breaking point*: compass/elevation implies fixed, externally measured coordinates; vowel position is felt and somewhat relative to the individual speaker's mouth. Don't let the learner think there's a single "correct" millimetre position — there's a category, with real speaker-to-speaker variation inside it.

**Story analogy — a slow-motion replay of a golf swing or a dance move, for diphthongs specifically.** At full speed, a swing or a dance step looks like one fluid action; slow it down and you see it's actually a sequence of positions the body moves through. This is a strong parallel to the slow-stretch technique's actual function: revealing motion that normal-speed perception genuinely cannot separate out.
*Breaking point*: a swing has many intermediate positions; a diphthong is described with just two endpoints (start and end), a simplification worth stating explicitly so the learner doesn't over-elaborate the description.

**Visual analogy — the vowel-space quadrilateral map** (Blueprint Component 3), built corner-by-corner as the learner produces each corner vowel and feels its extreme position. Not a metaphor but the actual working diagram this concept is organised around, and per Component 8's sequencing flag it should be built starting from the corners before any closer, subtler contrasts are attempted.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Vowels are just the sounds between consonants."** A common informal description (and etymologically related to how many learners have already heard "vowel" defined) that says nothing about articulation and actively reinforces MC-VOWELS-HAVE-NO-ARTICULATION-PLACE by defining vowels purely negatively, in terms of what they are not, rather than what they are.
- **"Diphthongs are just two vowels squished together."** This is the near-opposite error to MC-MONOPHTHONGS-AND-DIPHTHONGS-ARE-THE-SAME, and it is just as damaging: it suggests a diphthong is phonologically two units (which would predict it behaves like two syllable nuclei, which it does not — a diphthong occupies one syllable slot). The correct framing is one phoneme with an internal glide, not two phonemes concatenated.
- **"Open your mouth more for some vowels and less for others."** Conflates jaw opening (a real but secondary correlate) with tongue height (the actual primary parameter) in a way that will mislead a learner trying to feel the difference between, say, two vowels with similar jaw opening but different tongue height.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The height-and-frontness check (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 1's own conflict evidence: say "ee" then "ah," attending to tongue position rather than only to sound. *Predict first*: "Do you think your tongue is in the same place for both, or somewhere different?"
2. **The vowel-space corner tour (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*, before each corner: "Where do you think your tongue will need to go to make this next sound?"
3. **The slow-stretch test (learner activity, the core instrument for the second half of this concept).** *Predict first*: "If I stretch out the vowel in 'time' really slowly, do you think my tongue will stay still or move?" Then demonstrate, and have the learner repeat it themselves — this must be a learner-performed test to be diagnostic, not merely a tutor demonstration to be watched.
4. **The rounding check by mirror or touch (learner activity).** *Predict first*: "Do you think your lips are rounded or spread for 'oo' compared to 'ee'?" Then verify by mirror or by touching the corners of the mouth.

## Discovery Questions

As with the consonant-classification concept, this is a split case: **the underlying insight that vowels have a real, felt structure is worth letting the learner discover through the height/frontness conflict evidence, but the specific inventory (which English vowel sits where) is conventional and should be surveyed systematically, not hunted for.**

The monophthong/diphthong distinction, however, is genuinely well suited to discovery, because the evidence (motion or its absence) is directly available through the learner's own slow production, requiring no prior knowledge of the category system:

1. **Need**: "Stretch out the vowel in 'see' as long as you can. Now stretch out the vowel in 'time.' Do they behave the same way?"
2. **Playground**: the learner stretches several vowels from familiar words freely, without being told which category to expect.
3. **Invention**: "Some of these stayed put and some moved. Can you sort them into those two groups?" The learner constructs the monophthong/diphthong boundary from their own felt evidence.
4. **Collision**: present a genuinely ambiguous or unfamiliar case (a vowel with a very slight, easy-to-miss glide) — this forces the learner to sharpen their own sorting criterion rather than rely on an obvious contrast.
5. **Formalization**: name the two categories — monophthong (steady) and diphthong (gliding) — for groups the learner has already built.
6. **Compression**: "Stretch it slowly. Does it stand still, or does it walk somewhere?"

## Teaching Sequence

The pedagogical logic behind this arc:

- **The "vowels are describable too" reframe must come first, before any classification task is attempted** (Blueprint's own Component 8 adaptive flag, stated as a binding sequencing rule rather than a remediation-only move). Attempting height/backness classification while the learner still holds "there's nothing to find" as a working assumption will produce guessing rather than genuine testing.
- **Corner vowels before subtler mid-vowel contrasts** (Blueprint's own explicit sequencing flag). The four extreme corners of the vowel space are the most perceptually and proprioceptively distinct positions available, and establishing the *outer boundaries* of the space first gives the learner a reliable frame of reference for judging where subtler, more central vowels fall relative to those known extremes.
- **Height, then backness, then rounding, as separate sweeps before combination** — directly parallel to the consonant-classification concept's place-then-manner-then-voicing sequencing, and for the same reason: each dimension needs to be independently secure before the harder task of holding all three (or, for diphthongs, describing a two-point trajectory) is asked of the learner.
- **The monophthong/diphthong distinction should be introduced only after the height/backness/rounding system for monophthongs is reasonably secure**, not interleaved with it from the start — introducing motion as a variable before position itself is settled would compound two new ideas at once.
- **The full-classification task (TA-5) must include both a monophthong and a diphthong**, since these require structurally different answers (a single three-value coordinate versus a start/end trajectory description) — certifying only on monophthongs would leave the diphthong half of this concept's objective completely unverified.

Turn-by-turn scripting, protocol routing, and adaptive flags: Blueprint **Component 7 — Session Architecture** and **Component 8 — Adaptive Flags** (in particular the describability-framing flag, the slow-stretch-as-standing-method flag, corner-vowel sequencing, and the S9 L1-vowel-comparison flag).

## Tutor Actions

In recommended order, from `../teaching-actions/`:

1. **Demonstration** (SHOW) — learner-performed, exactly as at the consonant-classification and articulation-organs concepts; this remains proprioceptive evidence throughout the phonetics strand.
2. **Concept Map** (ORGANIZE) — the vowel-space quadrilateral, built progressively as each new vowel is classified and plotted, functioning here exactly as the cognate-pair table does for consonants: the artefact that makes the structure visible and reusable rather than a list to memorise.
3. **Matching** (DO) — vowel-to-classification and classification-to-vowel, bidirectionally, with the same diagnostic value as at the consonant concept: production-from-description ("say a high, back, rounded vowel") is the harder and more valuable direction to certify.
4. **Prediction** (TEST-THINKING) — predicting a vowel's classification before testing it, and predicting monophthong-versus-diphthong before stretching a word, both directly exercising the target skills.
5. **Error Analysis** (TEST-THINKING) — presenting a vowel described with only two of the three dimensions and asking what's missing, parallel to the consonant concept's use of this action for the equivalent misconception (though note this concept's Misconception Register does not itself carry an "independent dimensions" misconception the way the consonant concept does — see Curriculum Feedback below).

**Does not fit**: **Worked Example** in the derivation sense, for the same reasons as throughout this phonetics strand. **Game**, for the same chocolate-covered-broccoli reasoning as at the consonant concept — speed rewards recall of drilled vowels over genuine height/backness testing, and the risk is arguably higher here given how gradient and subtle the distinctions are; speed pressure on a genuinely gradient perceptual judgement is more likely to produce guessing than on the more categorical consonant contrasts.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Like the two phonetics concepts immediately upstream, this concept's core evidence is **proprioceptive**, with the added complication that vowel positions are gradient rather than categorical, making the proprioceptive signal itself subtler and more effortful to attend to than consonant place. (Channel reality: `../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`.)

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **Confident overall-sound judgements with no reference to tongue position** ("they just sound different") on a height/backness item — the direct auditory signature of MC-VOWELS-HAVE-NO-ARTICULATION-PLACE, and it should be met not by supplying the answer but by redirecting attention back to the physical check.
- **Slowed, effortful production during a height/backness comparison**, distinct from the fluent effortlessness of ordinary speech. This is a *positive* sign here, unlike some concepts where slowness signals difficulty — it indicates the learner is genuinely attending to tongue position rather than relying on acoustic impression alone, which is the entire point of the exercise.
- **Spontaneous stretching before answering a monophthong/diphthong item**, without being told to stretch. This is the single strongest voice-channel evidence that the slow-stretch technique has been internalised as a standing method rather than a one-off demonstrated trick.
- **Vowel quality drifting during an attempted sustained hold** (a learner trying to hold "ee" steady but sliding toward a different vowel over a few seconds) — this is a genuine articulatory-control observation, not a knowledge error, and should be treated as a motor-control note rather than a conceptual correction.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"No touch-point doesn't mean no position — stretch it out and feel where your tongue actually sits."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads, which the Blueprint does not own:

- **Single-dimension probes (MP-1, MP-2, MP-3).**
  - *Correct, reached via a felt comparison* (the learner references tongue position or lip shape when answering) → genuine method use.
  - *Correct, reached via pure acoustic impression with no physical reference* → potentially correct by ear alone without the underlying method; this is a real risk specific to vowels (much harder to fake for consonant place, where the physical test is nearly unavoidable) and should be spot-checked by asking "how did you know?" rather than accepted at face value on accuracy alone.
- **Diphthong discrimination (MP-4).**
  - *Correct, with the learner audibly stretching the word before answering* → the technique has transferred; strong evidence.
  - *Correct without stretching* → treat as **unverified**, not as failure — a learner may correctly know a specific word contains a diphthong from prior exposure without having used or needing the general technique on this particular item. Follow up with a genuinely unfamiliar word to confirm the technique itself, not just this item's answer, is present.
- **Full classification (MP-5).**
  - *All three dimensions supplied for a monophthong* → strong evidence for that half of the objective.
  - *A correct start/end description for a diphthong* → this is this concept's genuine dual-target certification requirement (see Teaching Sequence) — a certification based only on monophthong items would systematically miss this half.

**Mastery certification trigger**: correct height, backness, and rounding judgements across separate novel monophthong pairs, each reached with an audible or reported physical reference rather than acoustic impression alone; correct monophthong/diphthong discrimination on a novel word, with the slow-stretch technique used spontaneously; and a complete classification — full three-dimension for a monophthong, start/end description for a diphthong — on one item of each type. The dual monophthong-and-diphthong requirement on the final item is non-negotiable, matching the same principle applied to the consonant concept's novel-sound requirement: certifying on the easier half alone would leave the harder half's objective entirely unverified.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"They just sound the same to me"** on a subtle mid-vowel contrast — take this seriously as a genuine perceptual difficulty (per the Blueprint's own S6 validation), not as insufficient effort. Vowel distinctions genuinely are more gradient than consonant ones, and the correct response validates this explicitly before retrying: "That's a fair thing to find hard — some vowels really are close together. Let's go back to the corners first and work our way in."
- **"I can't feel my tongue move"** during a slow-stretch attempt — often a genuine difficulty distinct from not understanding the task; some learners need the motion externally referenced before they can detect it internally. Offer a mirror or have the tutor produce the same glide alongside the learner so they can watch a visible cue (lip movement, jaw shift) that correlates with the tongue motion they cannot yet isolate proprioceptively.
- **The smaller question to shrink to**: from full classification, down to **the single height-only judgement between the two most extreme corner vowels** (/iː/ high-front vs. /ɑː/ low-back — maximal contrast on every dimension at once, so the learner cannot fail to notice *something* different even if they can't yet isolate which dimension). This is close to the floor of what this concept can be about and re-establishes that vowels do have a felt difference before returning to subtler contrasts.
- **Never shrink to "just listen for the difference."** This is the exact strategy MC-VOWELS-HAVE-NO-ARTICULATION-PLACE consists of, and reverting to it under difficulty will reinforce rather than repair the misconception.

## Memory Hooks

- **Concept type**: concept (a continuous classification space) with an embedded **perceptual-motor skill** (feeling tongue position and applying the slow-stretch technique). The space itself, once understood, is durable; the felt-judgement skill, being gradient and subtler than consonant place, likely needs more sustained practice to stay reliable.
- **Review form**: spaced classification items using **near-contrast pairs** (vowels close in the space) rather than only corner-vowel maximal contrasts, once the corners are secure — reviewing only easy contrasts will look like retention while leaving the harder discriminations, which are this concept's actual difficulty, untested and likely to decay unnoticed.
- **Concept-specific deviation**: the monophthong/diphthong discrimination specifically benefits from review using the learner's own recently-encountered vocabulary (words from current reading or conversation) rather than a fixed drilled list, since the target behaviour (spontaneously reaching for the slow-stretch technique on *any* new word) is best verified on material the learner has not pre-classified.
- **Interleaving partners**: `eng.phonetics.consonant-sounds`, once both are underway — interleaving strengthens the boundary between the two systems and specifically guards against a learner attempting to apply consonant-style place/manner/voicing tests to a vowel, or vice versa. Height/backness/rounding items should themselves be interleaved with each other rather than drilled one dimension exhaustively before the next, mirroring the consonant concept's cognate-pair interleaving rationale.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: `eng.phonics.short-vowels` and `eng.phonics.long-vowels-silent-e` — the letter-level phonics work downstream benefits from a learner who already has *some* felt vocabulary for why vowel sounds differ, though (as with the consonant/digraphs relationship noted upstream) that downstream concept does not require this analytic depth to succeed on its own more print-focused terms.
- **Near**: `eng.phonetics.ipa-basics` — the vowel quadrilateral built here is a direct, hand-built precursor to the IPA vowel chart's formal grid.
- **Far**: `eng.phonetics.syllable-stress` — stressed and unstressed vowels in English differ systematically in quality (unstressed vowels frequently reduce toward a central, neutral position, the schwa) — a learner with a secure felt sense of the vowel space has a real physical basis for noticing and later describing that reduction, rather than only hearing "it sounds weaker."
- **Real-world**: understanding accent and dialect variation in vowels specifically — regional and social accent differences in English are very substantially vowel-based (the same word's vowel occupying a measurably different point in the vowel space across dialects), and this concept supplies the descriptive vocabulary to characterise that variation precisely rather than only impressionistically.
- **Expert transfer**: the durable skill is **locating a continuous, gradient phenomenon within a structured multi-dimensional space using calibrating reference points (the corners) before attempting finer discriminations** — the same strategy used in colour perception (anchoring to primary hues before discriminating near-shades), in music (anchoring to a tonic before judging microtonal or expressive pitch deviation), and in any domain where a continuum is being learned via a small number of well-chosen landmark cases.
