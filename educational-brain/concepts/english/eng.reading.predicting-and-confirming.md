## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Form a prediction about upcoming text content grounded in specific, available clues (title, headings, opening details), naming the specific clue behind it.
2. Distinguish a new text detail that supports a prediction from one that contradicts it.
3. Write a genuinely revised prediction when new evidence contradicts an earlier one, rather than merely noting the contradiction.
4. Recognize and name selective reading that ignores contradicting evidence to preserve an original guess.
5. Complete a full predict-read-confirm-or-revise cycle across a multi-part text.

## Core Understanding

A prediction is a **reasoned inference about upcoming content, grounded in specific clues already available** (the title, headings, opening details, illustrations) — not an ungrounded guess disconnected from any evidence, and not something that could be equally true of almost any text. This is the concept's first load-bearing fact, extending `eng.reading.inference-in-reading`'s clue-based reasoning forward in time: just as an inference draws a conclusion from clues already present, a prediction draws a conclusion about clues not yet revealed, using the same evidentiary discipline. The concept's second, equally load-bearing fact is that a prediction is a **working hypothesis to be revised, not a claim to be defended** — as new text arrives, the strong reader actively checks whether it supports or contradicts the existing prediction, and when contradicted, genuinely updates the prediction to reflect the new evidence rather than reading selectively to preserve the original guess or explaining away the contradiction. Confirming and revising are therefore not separate skills bolted onto prediction — they are what makes prediction a genuine reading strategy rather than a one-time guess with no ongoing function.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "predicting means making any guess about what happens next — it doesn't need to be based on anything specific in the text."**
The runnable simulation: state a plausible-sounding guess with no reference to the title, headings, or opening details actually present, treating "prediction" as synonymous with "any guess." This is a reasonable default, since the everyday word "predict" carries no built-in requirement that a guess be grounded in specific evidence.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — being shown an ungrounded guess ("the story is about a dragon") against a title-and-opening-grounded one ("the story is probably about water shortage"), for a text titled "The Drought That Changed the Village," and being asked which prediction actually uses the available clues.
*Shelf-life warning at replacement*: "A real prediction reasons from the evidence already in front of you — the title, headings, opening details — not from anything that popped into your head."

**Intermediate — "I ground my predictions in specific text clues, but once I've made a prediction, I want to defend it rather than change my mind."**
The simulation: correctly identifies a specific clue to ground an initial prediction, but reads selectively in favor of that prediction once made, resisting or explaining away later contradicting evidence rather than genuinely revising.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a prediction that a character will "succeed easily," followed by a paragraph showing the character failing repeatedly, and being asked whether the new information supports or contradicts the prediction, and what should happen to it now.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A prediction is a working guess, not a commitment. Check new information against it, and if it contradicts, change the prediction to match what the text is now actually showing you."

**Advanced — "I ground predictions in specific clues, actively check new text against them, and genuinely revise when contradicted, across a full predict-read-confirm cycle."**
The simulation: forms a clue-grounded initial prediction, checks each subsequent stage of a text against it, writes a specific revision when contradicted, and confirms or finalizes at the end. This is the target model, matching TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: none within the current curriculum — this concept is terminal, with no further KG unlocks.
*Shelf-life warning*: none required; this concept's predict-confirm-revise cycle is intended to generalize directly to ordinary independent reading without a further formal teaching step.

**Expert — "at full reading fluency, predicting and revising happen continuously and largely unconsciously throughout a text, as an ongoing background process rather than a discrete, deliberately-paused step."**
Named here to mark the arc; this concept's explicit, staged predict-check-revise routine is a scaffold for a process that, at true reading fluency, runs continuously and mostly beneath conscious awareness — named here so the tutor understands the routine's eventual goal is a constant, low-effort background process, not a permanently maintained deliberate pause-and-check procedure.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant failure mechanism is that **the everyday meaning of "predict" carries no evidentiary requirement**, so a learner reasonably imports that loose, ungrounded sense of the word into the reading-strategy context, where the term is being used in a much more specific, evidence-disciplined way — nothing in ordinary conversational use of "predict" signals that a text-based prediction must be traceable to a specific clue.

The second mechanism is that **defending an initial commitment is a natural, general human tendency** (not specific to reading), and a learner who has invested effort in forming a prediction reasonably feels some resistance to abandoning it — the failure surfaces specifically when new text evidence genuinely contradicts the prediction, and the psychologically easier path (read selectively, downplay the contradiction) competes directly with the harder, more effortful path (genuinely revise).

The third mechanism, specific to this concept's terminal position in the curriculum, is that **without further scaffolded unlocks to reinforce the skill**, a learner exiting with only partial mastery of the revision half of this concept has no built-in downstream concept designed to catch and repair the gap — making the Blueprint's own mandatory dual-pass gate (requiring both grounding and revision skills before certification) especially important here.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns the trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-A-PREDICTION-IS-JUST-A-GUESS-AND-DOESNT-NEED-TO-BE-BASED-ON-TEXT-CLUES
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-A — includes the dragon-guess/water-shortage-prediction discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 3 (language contamination), where the everyday, loose sense of "predict" (any guess about the future) gets carried into a technical reading-strategy context that specifically requires evidentiary grounding.
- **Teaching consequence of the birth type**: because the contaminating source (ordinary conversational "predict") isn't itself wrong, only mismatched to this stricter technical use, the repair requires **a concrete, checkable test** (can you name the specific clue behind this prediction?) rather than simply asserting predictions must be "grounded" in the abstract.
- **Verification of death**: given a novel title and opening, the learner states a prediction and can name the specific clue (a word, phrase, or detail) that led to it, rather than offering a plausible-sounding but untraceable guess.

### MC-B-ONCE-A-PREDICTION-IS-MADE-IT-SHOULD-BE-DEFENDED-RATHER-THAN-REVISED
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-B — includes the stubborn-defense/genuine-revision discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the generally reasonable instinct to follow through on a stated commitment, extended into a reading context where the "commitment" (a prediction) is specifically meant to be provisional and revisable.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct, personal experience of holding a contradicted prediction up against new evidence**, since an abstract instruction to "stay flexible" is less convincing than the felt moment of recognizing a specific, concrete contradiction the learner cannot easily explain away.
- **Verification of death**: given a prediction clearly contradicted by new text evidence, the learner writes a specific revised prediction reflecting that evidence, rather than defending, ignoring, or vaguely acknowledging the contradiction without updating anything.

## Analogies

**Best — the Random-Card Weather-Forecaster and Stubborn-Detective anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a forecaster predicting rain by picking a random card, rather than reading the clouds and pressure already visible, directly seeds MC-A's fix (a real prediction uses available evidence); a detective who ignores every new clue pointing elsewhere just to avoid admitting an early-named suspect was wrong directly seeds MC-B's fix (a prediction is a hypothesis to revise, not a claim to defend).
*Breaking point*: a real forecaster's prediction is checked against actual future weather with a single, unambiguous outcome, while a reading prediction is checked against a text whose "confirmation" can itself require the same inferential judgment the prediction was built on — useful for the grounded-versus-random distinction, less precise for implying prediction-checking is always as clean as a forecast verifying against tomorrow's actual weather.

**Alternative — adjusting a travel route as new traffic information arrives.** An initial route (the prediction) is chosen based on available information (the map, current conditions); as new traffic reports arrive (new text evidence), a good driver adjusts the route rather than stubbornly continuing the original plan into a known jam.
*Breaking point*: a traffic report is an unambiguous, externally verified fact, while a text's "contradicting" evidence sometimes requires its own careful, inferential reading to correctly identify as contradicting — useful for the revise-on-new-information intuition, less precise for implying contradiction-detection is always as immediate as a traffic alert.

**Visual anchor — the Random-Card Weather-Forecaster and Stubborn-Detective** as already described above, doubling as both the primary teaching metaphor and the actual working instrument this concept's core teaching is organized around.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Any guess about what happens next counts as a prediction."** Directly installs MC-A as a stated rule, precisely the ungrounded-guess error this concept's first misconception consists of.
- **"Once you've made your prediction, stick with it — changing your mind means you weren't paying attention."** Directly installs MC-B as explicit, stated advice, exactly the defend-rather-than-revise framing this concept's second misconception's repair must counter.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case — a structural irony worth noting explicitly, since this concept's own core content is the discipline of predicting and confirming.

1. **The dragon-guess/water-shortage-prediction comparison (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-A. *Predict first*: "Which of these two predictions do you think actually uses the clues in this title and opening?" Then compare and resolve.
2. **The succeed-easily/repeated-failure contradiction (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-B. *Predict first*: "Does this new paragraph support or contradict the original prediction?" Then confirm the contradiction and revise together.
3. **The full three-part predict-read-confirm cycle (learner activity, TA-4).** *Predict first*: "Based on the opening, what do you predict happens next?" Then read each subsequent part, checking and revising as needed.

## Discovery Questions

This concept is well suited to **guided discovery for both structural facts**, since the evidence for each (clue-grounding; genuine revision) is directly available through the learner's own prediction-and-check attempts on real text.

1. **Need**: "If you had to predict what a story is about just from its title, could you do it randomly, or is there a better way?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely predicts from a title and opening, then states which specific clue their prediction is based on.
3. **Invention**: "Now that you've read further, does this new detail agree with your prediction, or not?" The learner checks their own prediction against real evidence.
4. **Collision**: present a clearly contradicting detail and ask whether the original prediction should be kept or changed.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — ground predictions in specific clues, and revise them when contradicted — from the learner's own evidence.
6. **Compression**: "Predict from the clues, then check and change your mind when the text tells you to."

## Teaching Sequence

The pedagogical logic behind this arc:

- **Grounding a prediction in available clues (TA-1) is established first**, directly confronting MC-A before any revision content is introduced, since the revision skill (TA-2/TA-3) presupposes the learner already has a genuinely clue-grounded prediction to check and potentially revise — there's nothing meaningful to revise if the starting prediction wasn't grounded in evidence to begin with.
- **Treating a prediction as a working hypothesis (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting MC-B by teaching the checking step (does new evidence support or contradict?) before the harder generative step (writing an actual revision).
- **Revising a prediction when contradicted (TA-3) comes third**, extending TA-2's checking judgment into the generative act of writing a specific, evidence-reflecting revision — this ordering matters because writing a good revision presupposes the learner can already correctly identify that a contradiction exists.
- **The full predict-read-confirm cycle (TA-4) comes last**, requiring the learner to apply clue-grounding (TA-1), contradiction-checking (TA-2), and revision-writing (TA-3) across a complete, multi-part text — matching this program's established sequencing principle that a concept's most integrative task is reserved for its culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

This concept's core evidence is **spoken reasoning that names specific textual evidence** — both the clue behind an initial prediction and the specific new detail prompting (or not prompting) a revision — since a correct-sounding prediction or revision with no cited evidence cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated clue accompanying an initial prediction** ("I think this because the title mentions...") — the direct behavioral signature of genuine clue-grounding rather than an ungrounded guess.
- **A visible, spoken moment of reconsidering a stated prediction upon encountering new, contradicting text** ("wait, that doesn't fit what I said before...") — the direct target evidence for MC-B's repair, and this should be explicitly praised as a sign of careful reading, not a reversal to be embarrassed about.
- **Continued restatement or defense of an original prediction after being shown a clearly contradicting detail, without acknowledging the contradiction** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-B still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Predict from the clues you can see — and change your mind the moment the text shows you something different."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads, which the Blueprint does not own:

- **Grounding a prediction (MP-1) and distinguishing supporting from contradicting details (MP-2).** Per the Blueprint's own S9 protocol routing, an MP-1 failure indicates residual MC-A while MP-2/MP-3/MP-4 failures indicate residual MC-B — these are diagnostically separable, and remediation should target the specific failing skill rather than re-teaching both misconceptions generically.
- **Writing a genuine revision (MP-3).** Listen for a revision using the Blueprint's own recommended structure ("I originally thought ___, but now I think ___ because ___") — a response that merely restates the contradiction without producing a new, updated prediction has not passed, even if it correctly identifies the contradiction.
- **Detecting selective reading (MP-4).** This concept's most metacognitive item; a learner who can correctly identify someone *else's* selective-reading error but continues to defend their own contradicted predictions has an incomplete transfer of the underlying skill.
- **Full predict-read-confirm cycle (MP5).** The Blueprint's own routing notes an MP-5 failure with MP-1 through MP-4 passing indicates an integration gap under combined, multi-stage demands, warranting one more guided full-cycle example before re-probing.

**Mastery certification trigger**: a clue-grounded prediction with the specific clue named on a novel text; correct support/contradiction classification on a novel detail; a genuine, evidence-reflecting revision (not a restatement) on a contradicted prediction; correct identification of someone else's selective-reading error; and a full, independent predict-read-confirm-or-revise cycle across a novel multi-part text.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But I already said [my prediction], I don't want to seem wrong"** — take this as a genuine, understandable reluctance, not a mistake to be dismissed; respond by reframing directly: "Changing a prediction when the text shows you something new isn't being wrong — it's exactly what a strong reader does. The prediction was always meant to be updated as you learned more."
- **"I don't see how this new detail is different from what I already thought"** — a genuine difficulty distinguishing a supporting detail from a subtly contradicting one; respond by naming the actual test directly: "Ask specifically: does this new detail agree with what you said, or does it point somewhere different? Read the exact words again and check."
- **The smaller question to shrink to**: from a full three-part predict-read-confirm cycle, down to **a single, clearly contradicting detail presented right after a single, simple prediction, with no multi-stage complexity at all.** This removes the extended-cycle demand entirely, isolating the most basic revision skill before returning to the harder, full-cycle task.
- **Never shrink to "just say the story could be about anything."** This directly licenses MC-A's ungrounded-guessing pattern, undermining the clue-based prediction habit this concept specifically builds.

## Memory Hooks

- **Concept type**: sequential, self-monitoring inferential skill (clue-grounded prediction, ongoing evidence-checking, genuine revision) requiring honest metacognitive follow-through, not just an initial correct guess.
- **Review form**: spaced items always mixing clue-grounding tasks with contradiction-detection and revision-writing tasks in the same review cycle, since this concept's own mandatory dual-pass gate treats these as separately diagnosable skills, and reviewing only the easier grounding skill would understate the actual skill being maintained.
- **Concept-specific deviation**: because this concept is terminal (no further KG unlocks), review should track long-term retention of the revision half specifically, rather than relying on a downstream concept to naturally re-exercise it, as most non-terminal concepts in this program can.
