## Learning Objective
The learner will explain why plagiarism is a genuine ethical violation rather than a technical formatting rule, distinguish plagiarism from an honest citation error, and identify self-plagiarism as a related but distinct integrity concern.

## Core Understanding
Plagiarism is fundamentally about misrepresenting authorship — falsely claiming someone else's ideas, research, or words as one's own — which is a form of dishonesty with real consequences for the original author and the shared intellectual record, not an arbitrary formatting rule to follow. The severity distinction that matters is INTENT and PRESENCE OF A GENUINE ATTRIBUTION ATTEMPT: a citation with a minor technical slip (a missing page number) made while genuinely trying to credit a source is fundamentally different in kind, not just degree, from an entire uncredited paragraph presented as original work. Self-plagiarism (resubmitting one's own previous work as new, without disclosure) is a related but distinct concern resolved by disclosure, not by avoiding all reuse.

## Mental Models
- **Signed-Check Anchor**: signing someone else's name on a check is not a "technical paperwork issue" — it's falsely claiming ownership of something that isn't yours. Plagiarism is the same underlying ethical violation, just occurring in academic writing.
- **Parking-Permit-vs-Stolen-Car Anchor**: a minor parking-permit paperwork error and an actual stolen car are both "car-related mistakes," but the intent and harm are completely different. An honest citation slip within a genuine attribution attempt and deliberate uncredited copying are different in KIND, not degree.

## Why Students Fail
Learners taught citation rules mechanically (as formatting requirements to follow) never connect the rule to the underlying ethical principle it protects, so plagiarism registers as "a rule I might break" rather than "a form of dishonesty." Once the ethical weight IS understood, learners often overcorrect into treating every technical slip as equally serious as deliberate copying, because distinguishing intent from outcome is a harder judgment than simply flagging any citation irregularity as bad.

## Misconceptions
- **MC-A (Type 3, language contamination from "citation rules" framing)**: "Plagiarism is just a technical formatting rule, not a real ethical issue." Diagnostic: present a copied-with-no-attribution essay alongside a business colleague taking credit for someone else's idea, and ask whether these are the same underlying issue in different contexts. Repair: signed-check anchor — plagiarism is about falsely claiming ownership, and the harm (to the original author, the reader, and shared trust) is real regardless of context.
- **MC-B (Type 1, overgeneralization from "plagiarism is serious, so all citation issues are serious")**: "Any citation mistake, even a minor formatting error, is equally serious as deliberate plagiarism." Diagnostic: present a citation missing a page number (but correctly crediting the source) alongside an entire uncredited paragraph, and ask whether these represent the same kind of violation. Repair: parking-permit-vs-stolen-car anchor — ask whether there was a genuine, honest attempt at attribution, or no attempt at all.

## Analogies
- Signed-check anchor (above) for the ethical-violation-not-formatting-rule framing.
- Parking-permit-vs-stolen-car anchor (above) for the severity distinction.
- Anti-analogy: this is not "any imperfection in citation is forgivable" — a pattern of repeated, uncorrected minor errors after feedback becomes a different (negligence) concern, though still distinct in kind from deliberate copying.

## Demonstrations
- Present a copied-with-no-citation essay and a colleague-idea-theft scenario side by side; identify the shared underlying ethical violation.
- Rank five citation scenarios by severity, from an honest missing page number to an entirely uncredited paragraph, justifying the ranking by presence/absence of a genuine attribution attempt.
- Present a self-plagiarism scenario (resubmitting a prior essay for a new class without disclosure) and identify what disclosure step would resolve the concern.

## Discovery Questions
- "Who is specifically harmed when a piece of writing is plagiarized?"
- "Is this citation issue a genuine attempt to credit the source with a minor slip, or is there no attempt at attribution at all?"
- "Would disclosing that you're reusing your own prior work resolve this situation — or is something else going on?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Diagnostic: academic-writing-conventions check (citation purpose).
2. Anchor: signed-check and parking-permit-vs-stolen-car framing together.
3. TA-1: identify the shared ethical violation across academic and non-academic scenarios (targets MC-A).
4. TA-2: rank citation scenarios by severity based on presence of genuine attribution attempt (targets MC-B).
5. TA-3: introduce self-plagiarism and the disclosure resolution.
6. TA-4: full scenario classification (genuine plagiarism / honest error / self-plagiarism) with justification.

## Tutor Actions
- SHOW: academic-plagiarism vs. business-idea-theft scenario pair; minor-slip vs. uncredited-paragraph pair.
- TEST-THINKING: rank citation scenarios by severity and justify.
- DO: classify a mixed scenario set (genuine plagiarism, honest error, self-plagiarism).
- ORGANIZE: a "genuine attribution attempt present? yes/no" decision check before any severity judgment.

## Voice Teaching Notes
If a learner shows visible anxiety about ever making an honest citation mistake after this lesson, reassure explicitly: the goal is careful, good-faith attribution practice, not fear — an honest slip is categorically different from what this concept treats as a real integrity concern.

## Assessment Signals
- Probe: "Explain who is harmed by plagiarism and why it's an ethical issue, not just a formatting one." A response naming only "you could get in trouble" without naming the original author/reader/community signals residual MC-A.
- Probe: given two scenarios (genuine plagiarism, honest minor error), "classify each and explain the key distinguishing factor." Equal severity ratings signal MC-B.
- Confident-wrong signature: a learner who confidently over-penalizes a genuine, minor formatting slip as "just as bad" as uncredited copying — flag MC-B even when the underlying ethical seriousness is well understood.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If a learner conflates disclosed, permitted group-work collaboration with plagiarism, clarify explicitly that properly disclosed collaboration is a distinct, non-problematic category from misrepresenting sole, uncredited authorship — this is a common false-positive after learning the ethical framing.
