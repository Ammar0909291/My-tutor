## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify the specific tone/action/emotional information lost when stage directions are removed from a dramatic excerpt.
2. Identify an appropriate device (soliloquy, aside, revealing dialogue, or stage direction) a playwright could use to convey a private thought, given the absence of narration.
3. Identify act and scene boundaries in a play excerpt.
4. Infer a character trait using only dialogue and stage-direction evidence, with no narrated help.
5. Fully analyze a novel dramatic excerpt: act/scene structure, what specific stage directions convey, and a character trait inferred from dramatic-genre-specific evidence.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **stage directions are not optional, decorative details like descriptive passages in prose fiction — they are essential functional instructions carrying information a prose narrator would otherwise provide**, since drama has no narrator at all to describe action, tone, or setting directly to the reader or audience; a line like "Get out." reads as simple frustration without its stage direction, but with "[She says this calmly, almost gently, then turns away.]" the reader understands a complex mix of restraint and hurt — information entirely lost without the direction. Second, **a play is not simply a story with the quotation marks removed** — drama is a genuinely distinct genre built around live performance, structured in acts and scenes rather than prose chapters, with a complete absence of a narrator's voice; everything the audience learns, including a character's private thoughts, must come through dialogue, action, or stage directions, using genre-specific devices like a soliloquy or aside since inner thought can't simply be narrated the way prose does. Both corrections center on the same underlying fact: drama's narrator-less structure fundamentally reshapes how every kind of information reaches the reader or audience.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "stage directions are just optional extra details like descriptions in a story; a play is basically a story with quotation marks removed."**
The simulation: skip past bracketed stage directions as skippable flourishes, and read dramatic dialogue expecting the same narrative access to inner thought and description that prose provides.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — "Get out." with and without its stage direction, asked how differently the emotional state reads; and a prose passage directly narrating a character's private thought, asked how a playwright would need to convey the same thing with no narrator available.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Stage directions are the ONLY channel for tone/action/setting information in drama. Drama has no narrator — private thoughts need a genre-specific device."

**Intermediate — "I read stage directions carefully now, but I still sometimes expect narration-like access to a character's inner thoughts in the main dialogue itself."**
The simulation: correctly attends to stage directions but hasn't fully internalized that ALL information, including inner thought, must route through dialogue/action/stage directions with no exception.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct practice identifying how a playwright would convey a specific private thought without any narrator available.
*Shelf-life warning*: "There's no narrator anywhere in drama — even private thoughts need a soliloquy, an aside, or a revealing line."

**Advanced — "I read every stage direction as essential, correctly identify act/scene structure, recognize the narrator-substitute devices drama uses, and infer character traits purely from dialogue and stage directions."**
The simulation: integrates stage-direction attentiveness, structural identification, and dialogue-only inference into one coherent genre-specific analysis. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the two direct downstream cross-links — dramatic structure (how the plot arc manifests within drama's toolkit) and literary genres overview (situating drama among other genres).
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you drama's basic toolkit. Dramatic structure examines how the plot arc you already know works specifically within acts and scenes."

**Expert — "at fluency, reading stage directions as essential and inferring from dialogue-only evidence happens as an integrated part of ordinary dramatic reading."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit removal-test and narrator-substitute-identification routine is a scaffold for a reading habit that, at true fluency, operates as an integrated part of dramatic literacy.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **bracketed or italicized text visually resembles formatting conventions students associate with supplementary, skippable material** (like a footnote or a stage note in a textbook), so the visual convention itself invites the skip-past habit, independent of the actual functional importance of the content.

The second mechanism is that **most students' primary experience with narrative fiction is prose**, where a narrator's voice is the default, invisible channel for tone and inner thought; drama's complete absence of that channel is a structural fact easy to overlook precisely because it's an absence — there's no explicit signal announcing "there is no narrator here," unlike a presence a reader might notice.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-STAGE-DIRECTIONS-ARE-JUST-OPTIONAL-EXTRA-DETAILS-LIKE-DESCRIPTIONS-IN-A-STORY
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry — the with/without stage-direction discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 4 (notation-induced), from the bracketed/italicized visual formatting of stage directions resembling other skippable supplementary-text conventions, mistaken for evidence that the content itself is optional.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **directly removing and restoring the stage direction on the same line**, since the fix requires the learner to experience the specific information loss firsthand, not merely be told stage directions matter.
- **Verification of death**: given a dramatic excerpt with and without its stage directions, the learner identifies the specific tone/action/emotional information lost when directions are removed.

### MC-A-PLAY-IS-JUST-A-STORY-WRITTEN-WITH-QUOTATION-MARKS-REMOVED
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — the narrated-inner-thought/no-narrator-available discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 6 (analogy overextension), from prose fiction's narrative conventions (a default, invisible narrator voice) being extended by analogy onto drama, where that channel is structurally absent entirely.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct confrontation with a case demanding inner-thought conveyance**, since the absence of a narrator is easy to overlook until the learner is forced to solve the specific problem of how a playwright would communicate what prose simply narrates.
- **Verification of death**: given a scenario requiring a character's private thought to be conveyed in drama, the learner identifies an appropriate device (soliloquy, aside, revealing dialogue, or stage direction) given the absence of narration.

## Analogies

**Best — the No-Narrator Booth** (Blueprint Component 3): a "Prose Narrator Booth" where a hidden narrator whispers directly to the reader, versus a "Drama Stage" with no booth at all, only visible actors and a stage-directions scroll, directly seeding both fixes — the removal of the whisper channel forces reliance on stage directions and genre-specific devices.
*Breaking point*: a physical booth-versus-no-booth setup is a binary, visually obvious structural fact, while the actual craft skill of writing an effective stage direction or soliloquy requires nuanced authorial judgment the anchor doesn't itself teach — useful for the no-narrator-channel intuition, less precise for implying the SKILL of using the remaining channels well is equally simple.

**Alternative — a silent film versus a novel.** A silent film (like drama) must convey emotion and information entirely through visible action, facial expression, and intertitle cards (like stage directions) since there's no narrating voice-over — while a novel can simply state a character's internal state directly.
*Breaking point*: silent film's intertitle cards are a historically specific, relatively blunt device, while drama's stage directions and soliloquies are a more flexible, continuously-available toolkit throughout the text.

**Visual anchor — the No-Narrator Booth**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Stage directions are just extra flavor text you can skip if you're short on time."** Directly installs the optional-decoration misconception as a stated instruction.
- **"A play is just a story where the dialogue doesn't have quotation marks."** Directly installs the reformatted-prose misconception as a stated definition.

2. **The with/without stage-direction collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "How differently do you interpret this line with versus without its stage direction?"
3. **The narrated-thought/no-narrator collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "How would a playwright convey this same private thought with no narrator to state it directly?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both genre-defining facts are directly checkable by removing/restoring stage directions and by attempting to solve the narrator-substitute problem directly.

1. **Need**: "If you skip the bracketed text in a play script, do you lose anything important?"
2. **Playground**: the learner reads several dialogue lines with and without their stage directions freely before being told the official terminology.
3. **Invention**: "How could a playwright show us this character is lying to themselves, if there's no narrator to just tell us?"
4. **Collision**: present a prose passage's narrated inner thought and ask how a playwright would need to convey the same information.
5. **Formalization**: name the two genre-defining facts — stage directions are the essential substitute for narration; drama has no narrator anywhere, requiring genre-specific devices for inner thought — from the learner's own problem-solving.
6. **Compression**: "No narrator, ever — everything comes through dialogue, action, and stage directions."

- **The stage-direction-removal test (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the first misconception with the most concrete, immediately felt demonstration.
- **Identifying acts, scenes, and basic play structure (TA-2) comes second**, establishing drama's own structural vocabulary before the deeper no-narrator principle is generalized.
- **How drama conveys information without a narrator (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting the second misconception once structural vocabulary is available to frame the discussion.
- **Dialogue as the primary information channel (TA-4) and full novel-excerpt analysis (TA-5) come last**, extending the no-narrator principle to character-trait inference and integrating everything into a generative capstone — matching this program's established sequencing principle.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for what information a stage direction carries, or how a playwright would substitute for narration**, since a correct-looking answer with no articulated genre-convention reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated information-loss check** ("without this stage direction, we wouldn't know she's actually hurt underneath the laugh") applied to a new line — the direct behavioral signature of the stage-direction fix taking hold.
- **Spontaneous proposal of a genre-specific device** (a soliloquy, an aside) when asked how a private thought would be conveyed in drama — direct evidence the no-narrator fix is internalized.
- **An expectation that a play's dialogue alone will reveal a character's inner state**, with no attention to stage directions or genre-specific devices — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A-PLAY-IS-JUST-A-STORY-WRITTEN-WITH-QUOTATION-MARKS-REMOVED still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"There's no narrator in drama — everything comes through what's said, done, or noted in stage directions."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"I skipped the bracketed part, it's just extra stuff, right?"** — take this as a reasonable inference from the visual formatting convention; respond by demonstrating the loss directly: "Let's read the line without it, then with it — what changes?"
- **"How do we know what this character is REALLY thinking if nobody says so?"** — a genuine, reasonable question given prose's usual narrator access; respond by naming the genre constraint: "Drama has no narrator at all — so the playwright has to use a device like a soliloquy, an aside, or a revealing line instead. What might work here?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full novel-excerpt analysis down to **identifying the information lost in a single with/without stage-direction pair, with no structural or trait-inference task demanded.** Isolates the most concrete, immediately felt demonstration before returning to fuller analysis.
- **Never shrink to "just skip the bracketed parts and focus on the dialogue."** This directly licenses the optional-decoration pattern, undermining the essential-information discipline this concept specifically builds.
