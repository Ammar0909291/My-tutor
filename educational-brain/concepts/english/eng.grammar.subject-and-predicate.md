# Subject and Predicate — `eng.grammar.subject-and-predicate`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.subject-and-predicate` (canonical
  English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Subject and Predicate"
  — the two fundamental structural parts of an English sentence: the
  subject (who or what) and the predicate (what is said about it).
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.nouns`, `eng.grammar.verbs` — both
  load-bearing: the subject is typically a noun (or noun phrase), and
  the predicate is built around a verb (or verb phrase); a learner who
  cannot yet reliably identify nouns and complete verb phrases has no
  stable components to assemble into a subject/predicate split.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.word-order`. Understanding where
  the subject and predicate genuinely fall in a sentence — as opposed
  to simply the first word and the rest — is the direct foundation for
  analyzing and manipulating English word order more broadly.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Split simple sentences into their subject and predicate using the
   "who/what is this about?" test.
2. Correctly identify the true subject of a sentence beginning with an
   introductory phrase, rather than treating the introductory phrase
   itself as the subject.
3. Identify the FULL predicate — the main verb plus all its objects,
   complements, and modifiers — not just the single verb word.
4. Identify compound subjects and compound predicates.
5. Apply the full subject/predicate identification skill to longer,
   more varied sentences combining introductory phrases, compound
   structures, and extended predicates.

## Core Understanding

The subject is WHO or WHAT the sentence is about — usually the doer of
the action or the thing being described — not simply whatever word or
phrase happens to appear first in the sentence. Introductory phrases
(In the morning, After school, Quickly) often come before the true
subject and answer a different question (when? how? where?), not
"who/what is this sentence about?" — asking "who or what is doing the
action?" reliably locates the subject even when it doesn't come first.
A second, equally load-bearing fact is that the predicate is EVERYTHING
in the sentence except the subject — it includes the main verb PLUS any
objects, complements, and modifiers that complete the statement about
the subject: in "The dog chased the ball across the yard," "chased" is
only the main verb WITHIN the predicate, while "chased the ball across
the yard" is the FULL, correct predicate — everything that tells us
what the dog did. Identifying the predicate as everything except the
subject, not merely the single verb word, gives a complete and
functionally useful picture of sentence structure.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "the subject is whatever word comes first, and the
  predicate is just the verb"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model
  this concept exists to correct — a natural default since simple
  subject-verb-first sentences ARE the most common early example type,
  and since the verb genuinely is the predicate's most salient, action-
  carrying word.
- **Intermediate model — "I can split a simple sentence into its
  subject and predicate using the who/what test"**: installed by TA-1's
  simple-sentence-splitting practice, using the Component 3 sentence-
  split anchor to make the physical division concrete. Upgrade
  trigger: correctly splitting a fresh simple sentence without needing
  the who/what question spelled out.
- **Advanced model — "I can find the TRUE subject even when a sentence
  starts with an introductory phrase"**: installed by TA-2's practice,
  resolving MC-SUBJECT-IS-ALWAYS-THE-FIRST-WORD through direct contrast
  between an introductory phrase and the actual subject that follows
  it. Upgrade trigger: correctly identifying the true subject of a
  fresh sentence with a fronted introductory phrase, without
  hesitating at the first word.
- **Expert model — "I identify the FULL predicate, including compound
  structures, in genuinely varied and complex sentences"**: installed
  by TA-3's full-predicate-identification practice (resolving MC-
  PREDICATE-IS-JUST-THE-VERB), TA-4's compound-subject/predicate
  practice, and TA-5's application to longer, varied sentences. This is
  the concept's genuine mastery bar — moving from single-word
  identification to full-phrase structural analysis.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 learners whose L1 has different word order (verb-first or verb-
  final languages) or is a pro-drop language where the subject can be
  omitted/implied, subject identification specifically should be
  expected to require more explicit contrastive teaching — English's
  requirement for an always-explicit subject (even in imperatives,
  where "you" is implied) may not map cleanly from certain L1
  structures, warranting dedicated practice rather than assumed
  intuitive transfer.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of how simple sentences are typically first encountered.
The first-word-as-subject failure exists because the earliest, simplest
sentences a learner meets genuinely DO have the subject as the first
word ("The cat slept"), so the learner reasonably extends "the subject
is the first word" from this consistently reinforced pattern, with
nothing marking the introductory-phrase exception until directly
tested against a sentence like "In the morning, the birds sing." The
predicate-as-verb-only failure exists because the verb genuinely IS the
predicate's single most salient, action-carrying word — a learner's
attention naturally focuses there first, with the objects, complements,
and modifiers receiving less perceptual weight as "extra" rather than
integral parts of the predicate, so nothing in ordinary sentence
processing marks the full predicate as one unified unit until the
sentence is tested for what "everything else" actually includes.

**MC-SUBJECT-IS-ALWAYS-THE-FIRST-WORD (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — the earliest,
  simplest sentences a learner meets genuinely DO have the subject as
  the first word, so the learner extends "subject = first word" from
  this consistently reinforced pattern, with nothing in that early
  exposure marking the introductory-phrase exception until directly
  tested against a sentence with a fronted phrase.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, upon meeting a
  fresh sentence beginning with an introductory phrase, spontaneously
  asks "who or what is actually doing something here?" before
  answering is showing the who/what-test discipline has generalized
  beyond the specific TA-2 practice sentences.

**MC-PREDICATE-IS-JUST-THE-VERB (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 2,
perceptual intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a genuine perceptual default rather than
  a wrongly-taught rule — the verb genuinely carries the most salient,
  action-defining meaning in a sentence, so a learner's attention
  naturally settles there, with the objects/complements/modifiers
  receiving less perceptual weight as elaborating detail rather than
  integral parts of one unified grammatical unit; the learner isn't
  applying a false belief, just following natural attentional focus
  toward the single most content-rich word.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when asked for the
  predicate, spontaneously extends their answer to include the full
  remaining phrase ("chased the ball across the yard, not just
  'chased'") without being separately prompted is showing the full-
  predicate discipline has generalized beyond the specific TA-3
  practice sentences.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the sentence split anchor itself is the analogy, not
  a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically cutting a
  written sentence into subject and predicate parts, then repeating
  with an introductory-phrase sentence (showing the cut still falls at
  "the birds | sing," not the very first word) and a long-predicate
  sentence (showing the whole remaining phrase belongs to the
  predicate), directly embodies both misconception fixes in one
  continuous, hands-on activity. Breaking point: a single physical cut
  implies subject and predicate are always cleanly, unambiguously
  separable at one point, which holds for the sentences this concept
  addresses but can become genuinely more complex with certain advanced
  structures (like inverted sentences) beyond this concept's scope —
  this entry treats the cutting anchor as the introductory frame for
  the standard, reliable cases this concept covers.
- **Alternative — a news headline (subject) and the article body (what
  it says)**: a headline names WHO or WHAT the story is about; the
  article body tells everything else about that subject — parallels
  the subject-then-everything-else structure. Breaking point: useful
  for reinforcing the who/what-first, everything-else-second
  structure, but doesn't directly address the introductory-phrase
  complication (a headline doesn't typically open with a "when"
  phrase) the sentence-split anchor's second demonstration covers.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe introductory phrases as "not part of
  the sentence" or "extra, unimportant words"**: framing introductory
  phrases as unimportant filler risks implying they can be ignored
  entirely, when in fact they carry real information (when, how, where)
  — the correct distinction is that they answer a DIFFERENT question
  than "who/what is this about," not that they are unimportant or
  optional.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the sentence split)**: the Blueprint's Component 3
  anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: a strip of paper and scissors, or simply
  drawing a vertical line through a written sentence, substitutes for
  prepared cutting materials; any book or article supplies real TA-1–5
  practice across sentence varieties.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's compound-structure
  identification and TA-5's longer-sentence application are both
  hands-on, requiring the learner to actively analyze increasingly
  complex sentences, not merely recognize a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (introductory-phrase and full-predicate contrasts)**:
  the tutor models the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence
  demonstrations — asking who is doing the singing in "In the morning,
  the birds sing," and asking whether "the ball across the yard" tells
  us more about what happened in "The dog chased the ball across the
  yard" — making both distinctions directly and memorably visible.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  subject identification (TA-1/TA-2) or a full-predicate identification
  (TA-3), ask the learner to state their own attempt before the tutor
  confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
who/what-test principle and the full-predicate principle are both
genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured nouns and verbs, is asked who is
doing the singing in "In the morning, the birds sing"); **playground/
invention** (TA-1's simple-sentence splitting builds initial
confidence with the basic subject/predicate divide); **collision** (the
Blueprint's own "In the morning" probe breaks the first-word assumption;
the "chased the ball across the yard" probe breaks the verb-only
assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of both the
who/what test and the full-predicate principle); **compression** (TA-5's
fluent identification across longer, varied sentences without each
component separately re-derived). There is no convention-bound
terminology here requiring separate direct instruction beyond the
terms "subject" and "predicate" themselves, best introduced once the
learner has already felt the underlying structural distinction through
the anchor and TA-1.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (identifying the simple subject and simple predicate) is placed
first as the concept's most accessible entry point, using
straightforward subject-verb(-object) sentences before any complication
is introduced. TA-2 (sentences with introductory phrases) follows
immediately, directly targeting MC-SUBJECT-IS-ALWAYS-THE-FIRST-WORD —
per the Blueprint's own session architecture, this conflict-evidence
probe is placed right after TA-2, once the learner has secure simple-
sentence practice to contrast against. TA-3 (full predicate
identification) is placed next, directly targeting MC-PREDICATE-IS-
JUST-THE-VERB, addressing the SECOND, independent dimension (predicate
completeness, not subject position) once the learner already has a
secure sense of subject identification across both simple and
introductory-phrase sentences. TA-4 (compound subjects and predicates)
extends the now-secure basic structure to a genuinely more complex but
natural next case. TA-5 (subject/predicate identification in longer,
varied sentences) is placed last as the concept's integration capstone,
combining introductory phrases, compound structures, and extended
predicates all together. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's
own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint
References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously applies the
  who/what test to a fresh sentence beginning with an introductory
  phrase (rather than defaulting to the first word), and spontaneously
  extends their predicate answer to the full remaining phrase (rather
  than stopping at the verb) — these are the primary diagnostic signals
  for whether TA-2 and TA-3's disciplines have genuinely generalized
  beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before identifying the
  subject of a sentence with a fronted phrase, followed by a correct
  who/what-based identification, suggests the learner is consciously
  applying the test rather than defaulting to the first word — a good
  sign. Instant, confident identification of an introductory phrase as
  the subject is the concerning pattern for MC-SUBJECT-IS-ALWAYS-THE-
  FIRST-WORD.
- **Structural-completeness stakes**: real and central to TA-3/MP-3 —
  a learner who identifies only the main verb, without the full
  remaining phrase, when asked for the predicate is showing MC-
  PREDICATE-IS-JUST-THE-VERB actively, warranting immediate correction
  toward the full phrase.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "who or what is this sentence actually
  about?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for MC-SUBJECT-IS-
  ALWAYS-THE-FIRST-WORD, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh sentence's subject is being identified, making the
  who/what check a routinely-invited first response rather than a
  position-based default.

  the first word, and full predicate over just the verb).

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedure — a two-part structural analysis routine
(locate the subject via the who/what test, not position; identify the
full predicate as everything except the subject) applied to genuinely
novel sentences of increasing complexity; mastery is demonstrated by
correctly splitting unfamiliar sentences, including those with
introductory phrases and compound structures (MP-2/MP-3/MP-4) rather
than by recognizing a fixed, previously-taught example list.
Interleaving partners: mix simple-sentence items (testing TA-1) with
introductory-phrase and full-predicate items (testing TA-2/TA-3) and
compound-structure items (testing TA-4) in ongoing review, so the
learner continues exercising all three distinct dimensions as separately-
verified competencies.
