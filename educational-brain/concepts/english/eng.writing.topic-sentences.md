## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Reject a sentence that merely announces a subject and identify what claim is missing.
2. Reject an overly vague, broad claim and identify what specific content it fails to preview.
3. Sort candidate sentences as announcements vs. genuine claims.
4. Write an original, specific topic sentence from a given set of supporting details.
5. Revise a weak topic sentence into a specific claim and verify it against the paragraph's actual details.

## Core Understanding

A topic sentence must do two things a mere subject announcement does not: it must **state a specific, developable claim about the subject**, not just name the subject ("this paragraph is about dogs" names a subject and asks nothing of the reader; "dogs make excellent family pets because of their loyalty" gives the paragraph something to prove). This is the concept's first load-bearing fact, directly extending `eng.writing.paragraph-structure`'s controlling-idea requirement into the craft of writing that idea as one precise sentence. The second, equally load-bearing fact is that **specificity is a strength, not a limitation** — a topic sentence does not need to be broad enough to "cover" every conceivable detail about the general subject; it needs to precisely preview the particular content of *this* paragraph. A vague sentence that could introduce almost any paragraph on the topic ("dogs are interesting animals") previews nothing specific and therefore fails the same test as an announcement, even though it superficially looks more claim-like. Both failures — announcing instead of claiming, and hedging broadly instead of focusing — are corrected by the same underlying test: does this sentence give the reader something specific to expect from the sentences that follow?

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "a topic sentence tells the reader what the paragraph is about."**
The simulation: write a sentence naming the general subject, treating that naming as sufficient regardless of whether it states any claim.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — "This paragraph is about dogs" versus "Dogs make excellent family pets because of their loyalty and trainability," asked which one tells the reader what the paragraph will actually argue or show.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Naming the subject isn't enough — state a specific claim about it."

**Intermediate — "I state a claim now, but I make it broad enough to 'cover' anything the paragraph might say."**
The simulation: correctly avoids bare announcements but still writes overly general claims, reasoning that broader coverage is safer than narrow specificity.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — "Dogs are interesting animals with many qualities" versus "Golden retrievers are especially well-suited to families with young children," asked which actually helps a reader predict the paragraph's specific content.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Broad doesn't mean safe — it means uninformative. Be as specific as the paragraph actually is."

**Advanced — "I write and revise topic sentences that state a specific, developable claim, verified against the paragraph's actual supporting details."**
The simulation: composes original topic sentences from a given detail set and revises weak ones, checking the result against the specific details it must preview. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: the direct unlock `eng.writing.supporting-details`, where this concept's specificity discipline becomes the standard for selecting details sharp enough to develop the claim.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you a sharp, specific claim. The next step is choosing details specific enough to actually develop it."

**Expert — "at fluency, a sharp topic sentence emerges as a natural byproduct of already knowing precisely what the paragraph will argue."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit claim-vs-announcement and specific-vs-vague checks are a scaffold for a judgment that, at fluency, is inseparable from having a genuinely focused idea to write about in the first place.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **naming a subject is a genuinely easier task than stating a claim about it**, and in casual or spoken framing ("today I want to talk about...") announcement-style openings are common and socially unremarkable, so the habit transfers into writing where it doesn't serve the same function.

The second mechanism is that **learners conflate "broad enough to be safe" with "broad enough to be useless"** — believing a vague sentence protects them from being "wrong" about what the paragraph contains, when in fact the vagueness itself is the failure, since it previews nothing the reader can use to anticipate the paragraph's actual content.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-TOPIC-SENTENCE-JUST-ANNOUNCES-THE-TOPIC-RATHER-THAN-MAKING-A-CLAIM
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-A — includes the "this paragraph is about dogs"/"dogs make excellent family pets" discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from casual spoken framing ("I'm going to talk about...") being a socially normal opener that carries over into writing, where it fails to do a topic sentence's actual job.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **contrast an announcement directly against a claim on the identical subject**, since the difference isn't the subject named but whether a specific, provable idea about it is stated — an abstract rule ("make a claim") is less effective than the paired contrast.
- **Verification of death**: given a bare announcement, the learner identifies the missing claim and revises it into one.

### MC-A-TOPIC-SENTENCE-MUST-BE-VERY-GENERAL-AND-VAGUE-TO-COVER-EVERYTHING
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — includes the "interesting animals"/"golden retrievers" discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of a reasonable caution against overcommitting to specifics, extended into treating vagueness itself as a virtue rather than a failure to preview.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair reframes specificity as **helpful precision, not risk** — the Camera Focus anchor's sharp-photo framing directly targets this by showing the specific image communicates more, not less, safely.
- **Verification of death**: given a vague, broad claim, the learner identifies what specific content it fails to preview and revises it into a focused one.

## Analogies

**Best — the Camera Focus** (Blueprint Component 3): a blurry, zoomed-out photo (an announcement — "technically" shows the subject, tells nothing) versus a sharp, focused close-up of one specific moment (a genuine claim); directly seeds both fixes, since the blurry photo also stands in for the vague-claim failure — neither naming the subject nor zooming out to blur everything counts as a real topic sentence.
*Breaking point*: a photograph's "sharpness" is a purely visual property with no analogue to logical provability, while a topic sentence's specificity must also be something the paragraph can actually go on to prove or develop — useful for the vague-vs-focused intuition, less precise for the claim's evidentiary demand.

**Alternative — a movie trailer versus a movie poster with just the title.** A poster with only the title ("A Film About Dogs") announces the subject with no preview of content; a trailer previews specific scenes and stakes, giving the viewer something concrete to expect — mirroring claim vs. announcement.
*Breaking point*: a trailer can tease without fully resolving, while a topic sentence's claim should be genuinely developable by the specific sentences that follow, not merely teased.

**Visual anchor — the Camera Focus**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Just tell the reader what you're going to talk about."** Directly installs the announcement pattern as the definition of a topic sentence.
- **"Make it general so it covers everything the paragraph might say."** Directly installs the vague-claim misconception as a stated strategy.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Camera Focus (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Which photo tells you more — the blurry wide shot or the sharp close-up?" Then connect to topic-sentence specificity.
2. **The announcement/claim collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-A. *Predict first*: "Does this sentence tell you what the paragraph will prove?"
3. **The vague/specific collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Which sentence actually tells you what's coming?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both structural facts are directly checkable by comparing paired sentences against the paragraph's actual content.

1. **Need**: "If I only tell you the subject of my paragraph, do you know what I'm going to say about it?"
2. **Playground**: the learner compares several sentence pairs (announcement/claim, vague/specific) freely before being told the official test.
3. **Invention**: "Can you write a sentence that tells me exactly what this paragraph's details will show?"
4. **Collision**: present a broad claim and ask what specific content it fails to preview.
5. **Formalization**: name the test — state a specific, developable claim, not a subject label or a broad hedge.
6. **Compression**: "Name the claim, not the subject — and make it sharp, not safe."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Claim vs. announcement (TA-1) comes first**, establishing the baseline requirement (a claim, not a label) before refining how specific that claim must be.
- **Specific vs. vague claim (TA-2) comes second**, refining the claim requirement established in TA-1 into the sharper specificity standard.
- **Revising a weak topic sentence (TA-3) comes third**, applying both tests diagnostically to an existing flawed sentence before generative composition is required.
- **Writing an original topic sentence (TA-4) comes last**, the generative capstone requiring both tests applied prospectively from a set of supporting details.

Turn-by-turn scripting, protocol routing, and adaptive flags: Blueprint **Component 7 — Session Architecture** and **Component 8 — Adaptive Flags** (the mandatory MP-1/MP-2 dual-pass gate, the TA-4-required prerequisite for the downstream unlock, and the camera-focus-framing reuse for `eng.writing.supporting-details`).

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for why a candidate sentence passes or fails**, since a correctly-sorted example with no articulated reason cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated claim-check** ("does this actually prove something, or just name the subject?") applied to the learner's own draft — direct evidence the announcement test is internalized.
- **Voluntary tightening of a broad sentence into a narrower one** without being told to — direct evidence of the specificity fix taking hold.
- **A revised sentence checked back against the paragraph's actual supporting details**, not just checked for "sounding specific" — the direct behavioral signature of MP-5's verification step.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"State the claim, not the subject — and make it sharp enough to preview exactly what comes next."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads:

- **Rejecting an announcement (MP-1) and rejecting a vague claim (MP-2).** Per the Blueprint's S9 routing, mastery must NOT be certified unless both pass.
- **Sorting candidates (MP-3).** Confirms recognition; necessary but not sufficient without MP-1/MP-2's generative revision.
- **Original composition from details (MP-4).** This concept's generative capstone; listen for the learner checking their own sentence against the given details, not just producing something claim-shaped.
- **Revision with verification (MP-5).** The full diagnostic-and-generative loop; the verification step against actual details is the key evidence, not just the revised sentence's surface quality.

**Mastery certification trigger**: correct rejection-and-revision of an announcement; correct rejection-and-revision of a vague claim; correct sort of 4 candidates; an original, specific topic sentence matching given details; a revised weak sentence verified against its paragraph's actual details.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But I did say what the paragraph is about"** — take this as accurate but incomplete; respond: "You named the subject — now add what you're going to say ABOUT it. What's your specific point?"
- **"Isn't it safer to keep it general so I don't leave anything out?"** — a reasonable-sounding caution; respond by naming the actual cost: "General doesn't protect you — it just tells the reader nothing. What's the one specific thing THIS paragraph shows?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from full original composition or verified revision down to **sorting 2 clearly contrasting candidates (one bare announcement, one clear claim) with no revision demanded.** Isolates the most basic distinction before returning to generative tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just add a few more words to make it sound like a sentence."** This licenses surface padding without addressing the underlying claim-vs-announcement or specificity failure.
