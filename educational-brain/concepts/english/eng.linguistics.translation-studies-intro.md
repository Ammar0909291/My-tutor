## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Explain that translation is not a mechanical, word-for-word substitution process, since languages rarely map perfectly onto each other.
2. Identify a specific translation trade-off in a given example, explaining what was gained and lost in the choice made.
3. Explain that different translations of the same source text can each be legitimate, defensible choices reflecting different priorities, rather than one being simply "correct."
4. Produce and evaluate two original translations of a new passage, each prioritizing a different element, confirming both are legitimate.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **a "good" or "accurate" translation is not a mechanical, word-for-word substitution of each source word with its closest target-language equivalent** — this approach frequently produces awkward, unnatural, or misleading results, since languages rarely have perfectly matching vocabulary, idiom, grammatical structure, or cultural connotation for every word and phrase; good translation requires conveying the SAME MEANING and effect to a target-language reader, even if that requires departing from a literal, word-by-word match. Second, **there is not always exactly one correct translation for a given passage with all others simply wrong** — translation frequently involves genuine, defensible trade-offs (literal meaning versus rhythm, a pun versus its literal content, formality versus naturalness) where different translators can make different, EQUALLY LEGITIMATE choices depending on which priority they emphasize, with no single "correct" answer existing independent of those priorities.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "a good translation replaces each word with its closest equivalent, and for any passage there's exactly one correct translation."**
The simulation: judge translation quality by how closely each word matches the original, and insist a differing published translation must simply be "wrong."
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a word-for-word idiom substitution producing a confusing result compared against a meaning-focused translation, and two published translations of the same poem making different, defensible trade-off choices.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Converting a recipe's units requires recalculating proportions, not swapping numbers directly. And a novel adapted by two different filmmakers can produce two equally defensible films."

**Intermediate — "I can now recognize that meaning matters more than word-matching and identify specific trade-offs, but I still sometimes default to treating one published version as more 'official' than another."**
The simulation: correctly identifies trade-offs in provided examples but still implicitly ranks one existing translation as the "real" one when comparing.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct legitimacy-explanation practice, given two published translations making different but equally defensible choices.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Ask what priority each translation reflects, not which one is the 'official' answer — different, defensible choices can coexist."

**Advanced — "I produce two original translations of a new passage, each prioritizing a different element, and explain what was gained and lost in each choice."**
The simulation: integrates meaning-focused translation practice and multiple-legitimate-choices reasoning into one coherent, generative translation exercise. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: none within the current curriculum sequence — this concept is confirmed terminal.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the full toolkit for evaluating translation choices critically, in any language pair you encounter."

**Expert — "at fluency, evaluating translation choices for meaning-fidelity and recognizing legitimate trade-offs happens as an integrated part of ordinary cross-linguistic reasoning, without a separate deliberate checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit attempt-tradeoff-compare routine is a scaffold for an evaluative habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of cross-linguistic literacy.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **word-for-word substitution is the most immediately available strategy for someone with limited bilingual experience**, and it genuinely works adequately for many simple, non-idiomatic phrases, so the strategy goes uncorrected until it collides with an idiom or culturally-specific expression that exposes its limits.

The second mechanism is that **school translation exercises are frequently graded against one "answer key" translation**, reinforcing an implicit single-correct-answer framing that doesn't reflect how professional, published translation actually involves genuine, often publicly debated interpretive choices.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-A-GOOD-TRANSLATION-IS-A-MECHANICAL-WORD-FOR-WORD-SUBSTITUTION-FROM-ONE-LANGUAGE-TO-ANOTHER
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from word-for-word substitution being the most immediately available strategy and working adequately for simple, non-idiomatic cases, masking its failure on idiomatic or culturally-specific ones.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **show a word-for-word substitution actually failing on a real idiom**, since the meaning-over-substitution principle is far more convincing demonstrated through a concrete failure than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given an idiomatic expression, the learner attempts a literal word-for-word translation, identifies why it fails to convey the intended meaning, and constructs a meaning-focused alternative.

### MC-B-THERE-IS-ALWAYS-EXACTLY-ONE-CORRECT-TRANSLATION-FOR-A-GIVEN-PASSAGE-AND-ALL-OTHERS-ARE-WRONG
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from school translation exercises being graded against a single "answer key," reinforcing a single-correct-answer framing.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct comparison of two published translations making different, defensible choices**, since the pluralism is far more convincing demonstrated through real, legitimate published examples than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given two different, defensible translations of the same passage, the learner explains why neither should be considered simply "wrong," identifying the different priority each reflects.

## Analogies

**Best — the Recipe-Unit-Conversion and Novel-to-Film-Adaptation Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): converting a recipe's measurements requiring genuine recalculation for actual proportions, not direct number substitution — directly seeding the fix for MC-A. A novel adapted into film by two different filmmakers, each making different, equally defensible priority choices — directly seeding the fix for MC-B.
*Breaking point*: a recipe's proportions have one objectively correct recalculated answer, while a translation's "correct meaning transfer" involves more genuinely subjective, culturally-embedded judgment than a purely mathematical unit conversion — the recipe analogy risks implying more objectivity than translation actually has.

**Alternative — interpreting a piece of music for a different instrument.** Transcribing a violin piece for piano isn't a note-for-note mechanical transfer — the arranger makes real interpretive choices about what the piano can and should capture, and two different arrangers' transcriptions can both be musically legitimate.
*Breaking point*: musical transcription operates within a shared notational and harmonic system across instruments, while two human languages' semantic and cultural systems can differ far more fundamentally, a difference in the depth of the "translation gap" the music analogy understates.

**Visual anchor — the Recipe-Unit-Conversion and Novel-to-Film-Adaptation Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"A good translator just swaps each word for its equivalent in the other language."** Directly installs the mechanical-substitution misconception as a stated definition.
- **"If two translations differ, one of them has to be wrong."** Directly installs the single-correct-translation misconception as a stated definition.

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by attempting a literal translation and comparing published translations' trade-off choices.

1. **Need**: "If you translated an idiom word-for-word into another language, would it actually make sense to a speaker of that language?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely attempts a literal translation of an idiom before being told the official meaning-focused test.
3. **Invention**: "Can you think of a pun or wordplay that couldn't be translated while keeping both its literal meaning AND its wordplay effect?"
4. **Collision**: present two published translations of the same poem, one prioritizing rhythm and one prioritizing literal meaning, and ask whether one is simply wrong.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — translation requires meaning-transfer, not word substitution, and multiple legitimate translations can coexist — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Translate meaning, not words; different priorities can both be legitimate."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Translation as meaning-transfer, not word substitution (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the first misconception before trade-offs are introduced.
- **Identifying specific translation trade-offs (TA-2) comes second**, building the vocabulary of trade-off types needed before the second misconception can be addressed.
- **Multiple legitimate translations can coexist (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting the second misconception once trade-off vocabulary is established.
- **Full application: producing and evaluating original translation choices (TA-4) comes last**, the generative capstone integrating both misconception fixes — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for why a translation choice preserves meaning, and for why two differing translations can both be legitimate**, since a correct-looking translation with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated meaning-check** ("does this actually convey the same sense, even though the words don't match exactly?") applied to a new translation attempt — the direct behavioral signature of the meaning-over-substitution fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary identification of the specific priority each of two differing translations reflects**, without declaring one "wrong" — direct evidence the multiple-legitimate-choices fix is internalized.
- **Insisting a translation is wrong purely because it doesn't match a word-for-word rendering, or because it differs from another published version** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A or MC-B still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Translate meaning, not words — and different priorities can both be legitimate."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But shouldn't the translation use the exact same words the original does?"** — take this as an accurate, reasonable expectation of fidelity, not a mistake; respond by adding the meaning test: "Wanting fidelity is right, yes — but is the fidelity to the exact WORDS, or to the MEANING and effect? Sometimes those pull in different directions."
- **"This translation is different from the one I read before, so it must be wrong"** — a genuine, reasonable assumption given how school exercises are often graded; respond by naming the additional layer: "It is different, that's true — but does it convey the meaning well, just with a different priority? Could both be legitimate?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full translation analysis down to **attempting one single word-for-word translation of an idiom and identifying why it fails, with trade-off production set aside.** Isolates the most basic meaning-over-substitution discipline before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just find the one right way to say it."** This directly licenses the single-correct-answer pattern, undermining the multiple-legitimate-choices discipline this concept specifically builds.
