# Prefixes — `eng.vocab.prefixes`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.prefixes` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Prefixes" — word parts
  added to the beginning of a base word or root that change its
  meaning, taught with explicit attention to the multiple meaning
  categories prefixes cover and the discipline of verifying a genuine
  base word before confirming prefix status.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.word-families` — load-bearing because
  that concept's core insight (words are built from a shared root plus
  modifying pieces, and family members span different word classes) is
  the direct conceptual foundation for prefixes as one specific,
  namable kind of modifying piece attached to the front of a base word.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.vocab.roots-and-origins`. Once the
  learner can reliably identify a prefix and separate it from a base
  word, examining the historical origin and deeper meaning of that base
  word or root itself becomes the natural next layer of analysis.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify negation prefixes (un-, dis-, non-, in-/im-) and the
   resulting meaning change in a base word.
2. Identify prefixes from non-negation categories — location (sub-,
   super-, inter-), time/order (pre-, post-, re-), number (bi-, tri-,
   multi-), and degree (over-, under-) — recognizing negation as only
   one of several distinct meaning categories.
3. Check whether a suspected prefix genuinely attaches to a real,
   recognizable base word before confirming prefix status, correctly
   rejecting coincidental letter sequences that merely resemble a
   prefix (relax, uncle).
4. Infer the meaning of an unfamiliar word by combining a known
   prefix's category meaning with a recognizable base word's meaning.
5. Generate multiple genuine words using a given prefix correctly.

## Core Understanding

A prefix is a word part added to the beginning of a base word or root
that systematically changes its meaning — but genuine prefix competence
requires two disciplines beyond simple recognition. First, prefixes
span multiple distinct meaning CATEGORIES, not only negation: alongside
negation prefixes (un-, dis-, non-, in-/im-, reversing or negating the
base meaning), prefixes also express location/direction (sub- = under,
super- = above, inter- = between), time/order (pre- = before, post- =
after, re- = again), number (bi- = two, tri- = three, multi- = many),
and degree (over- = too much, under- = too little) — negation prefixes
are simply the most commonly taught and memorable category, not the
dominant or default pattern. Second, and equally load-bearing, a given
prefix does not always carry its usual meaning: it only functions as
that prefix when the remaining letters form a genuine, recognizable
base word that the prefix is actually modifying — "redo" genuinely
means "do again" because "do" is a real base word, but "relax" is NOT
"re-" + a base word "lax" meaning "lax again," because there is no such
base word being modified; the letters "re-" there are a coincidental
initial sequence, not the functioning "again" prefix. Reliable prefix
analysis therefore always checks for a real base word underneath before
trusting the prefix's usual meaning.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "prefixes make a word mean the opposite, and a
  given prefix always contributes the same meaning wherever it
  appears"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists
  to correct — negation prefixes (un-, dis-) are typically the first and
  most memorable examples in early vocabulary instruction, giving no
  signal that other meaning categories exist or that the same letter
  sequence can sometimes fail to function as a prefix at all.
- **Intermediate model — "I can identify negation prefixes and the
  resulting meaning change"**: installed by TA-1's negation-prefix
  practice, using the Component 3 building-blocks anchor to make the
  attachment-and-meaning-change process concrete. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly identifying a fresh negation prefix and stating the exact
  resulting meaning change without hesitation.
- **Advanced model — "prefixes cover multiple distinct meaning
  categories — location, time, number, degree — not only negation"**:
  installed by TA-2's non-negation-category practice, resolving
  MC-PREFIXES-ONLY-NEGATE-MEANING through the felt surprise that
  "submarine" has nothing to do with negation at all. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly naming a prefix's meaning CATEGORY (not just its
  translation) for a fresh word, distinguishing negation from location/
  time/number/degree.
- **Expert model — "I always check for a genuine base word before
  trusting a prefix's usual meaning, and I can productively infer
  meanings and generate new words using known prefixes"**: installed by
  TA-3's base-word-verification task (resolving MC-A-PREFIX-ALWAYS-HAS-
  ONE-FIXED-MEANING), TA-4's meaning-inference task, and TA-5's
  generation task. This is the concept's genuine mastery bar — treating
  the base-word check as a standing discipline applied to every
  suspected prefix, not merely when a "trick word" is flagged in
  advance.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 learners whose L1 has a very different morphological system (an
  isolating or root-and-pattern language with no prefix-based word
  formation), the underlying assumption that words are built by adding
  meaningful pieces to a base may require more foundational scaffolding
  before assuming the pattern-recognition skill transfers readily from
  their existing language intuitions.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of how prefixes are typically first encountered. The
negation-dominance failure exists because negation prefixes (un-, dis-)
are genuinely the most commonly taught and memorable early examples —
"unhappy" and "disagree" are intuitive, high-frequency words that make
the negation pattern feel like THE defining feature of prefixes, with
nothing in that early exposure marking that location, time, number, and
degree prefixes are equally common and legitimate categories until
directly presented. The fixed-meaning-assumption failure exists because
a prefix's usual meaning genuinely does hold reliably across the large
majority of words containing that letter sequence at the start (most
"re-" words really do mean "again"), so a learner reasonably extends
this reliable pattern into an unconditional rule, with nothing in
typical prefix practice — which usually presents only clean, genuine
examples — marking the coincidental exception (relax, uncle, prefer)
where the letter sequence is present but not functioning as a modifying
prefix at all.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.vocab.prefixes.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions this
concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-A-PREFIX-ALWAYS-HAS-ONE-FIXED-MEANING (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — a prefix's
  usual meaning genuinely holds reliably across the many genuine
  prefix-uses a learner encounters (most "re-" words really do mean
  "again"), so the learner extends this reliable correlation into an
  unconditional rule, with nothing in typical instruction — which
  rarely tests coincidental non-prefix cases — marking the boundary
  exception until directly presented.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, upon meeting a new
  "re-"-initial word, spontaneously asks "wait, is there actually a real
  base word here?" before assuming the "again" meaning applies is
  showing the base-word-check discipline has generalized beyond the
  specific TA-3 practice items.

**MC-PREFIXES-ONLY-NEGATE-MEANING (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: also a Type 1 signature, distinct
  mechanism — negation prefixes are disproportionately emphasized in
  early vocabulary instruction because they are highly memorable and
  produce clear, intuitive opposite-meaning pairs, so the learner
  extends "prefixes I've mostly seen negate meaning" into "prefixes
  negate meaning," with nothing in that early exposure marking the
  location/time/number/degree categories until directly presented.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when asked what a
  fresh non-negation prefix means, spontaneously names its CATEGORY
  (e.g., "that's a location one, like 'under'") rather than assuming
  negation by default is showing the multi-category model has been
  genuinely internalized.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the prefix building blocks themselves are the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically
  attaching a prefix block to a base-word block and discussing the
  resulting meaning change directly embodies genuine prefix combination;
  the "trick word" demonstration (uncle, where no base word "cle"
  exists) directly embodies the base-word-verification test. Breaking
  point: a physical building-block snap-together implies every
  attachment attempt either clearly works or clearly fails, which can
  understate that some borderline cases (like Latin-derived words such
  as "prevent," per MP-3) are genuinely ambiguous even to careful
  analysis — this entry treats the anchor as the introductory frame for
  clear cases, with TA-3/MP-3 explicitly acknowledging the limits of
  prefix analysis for historically opaque words.
- **Alternative — labeled toolbox drawers, not one single tool**: a
  toolbox has separate drawers for different tool categories (cutting
  tools, measuring tools, fastening tools); prefixes similarly sort into
  separate meaning-category "drawers" (negation, location, time, number,
  degree), and reaching for the wrong drawer (assuming negation for
  everything) misses the tool actually needed. Breaking point: useful
  for making the multi-category structure feel organized and
  intentional, but should not be extended to suggest the categories are
  perfectly separate and non-overlapping — some prefixes (e.g., "over-"
  for both location and degree) can straddle more than one category
  depending on context.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe a "trick word" like "uncle" or
  "relax" as containing a "fake" or "broken" prefix**: framing these
  words as deceptive or defective risks implying the language itself is
  unreliable, rather than that the specific letter sequence simply isn't
  functioning as a prefix in that word at all — the correct frame is
  "this isn't a prefix here," not "this prefix is broken here."

- **Home/no-equipment**: word cards, sticky notes, or simply spoken

  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether
  "submarine" means "not a marine" — making the non-negation category
  directly and memorably visible.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  prefix's meaning category (TA-2) or a base-word-verification judgment
  (TA-3), ask the learner to state their own judgment before the tutor
  confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
multi-category structure and the base-word-verification test are both
genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured negation-prefix identification, is
asked whether "submarine" means "not a marine"); **playground/
invention** (TA-2's non-negation examples across several categories let
the learner notice multiple distinct patterns exist); **collision** (the
Blueprint's own submarine probe breaks the negation-only assumption;
the "is there a base word 'lax'?" probe breaks the fixed-meaning
assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of both the
multi-category structure and the base-word-check discipline);
**compression** (TA-5's fluent generation of multiple genuine words with
a given prefix without needing each one's base-word status separately
re-verified). The specific CATEGORY LABELS (location, time, number,
degree) are convention-bound terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying pattern
diversity through TA-2's examples.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (negation prefixes) is placed first as the concept's most familiar,
memorable entry point, giving the learner a secure foundation using the
category they are most likely to already have some intuitive sense of.
TA-2 (non-negation prefix categories) follows immediately, directly
targeting MC-PREFIXES-ONLY-NEGATE-MEANING — per the Blueprint's own
session architecture, this conflict-evidence probe comes right after
TA-2, once the learner has concrete non-negation examples already in
hand to reason from. TA-3 (checking for a genuine base word) is placed
next, addressing the SECOND, independent discipline (verification, not
category) only after the learner already has a secure, broad sense of
what genuine prefixes look like across multiple categories (TA-1/TA-2)
— attempting the base-word check before the learner has enough genuine
prefix examples to contrast against would give them nothing reliable to
verify against. TA-4 (using prefix knowledge to infer meaning) is placed
after TA-3 specifically because productive inference presupposes the
learner can already distinguish a genuine prefix from a coincidental
letter sequence — inferring meaning from a "prefix" that isn't
genuinely functioning as one would produce systematically wrong
inferences. TA-5 (generating words with a given prefix) is placed last
as the concept's most demanding, fully productive task, requiring the
learner to generate multiple genuinely correct prefix-uses rather than
only recognize or infer from given examples. This entry does not
restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) —
see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously checks for a
  real base word when meeting a fresh "re-"/"un-"/similar-initial word
  (rather than assuming the usual meaning by default) — this is the
  primary diagnostic signal for whether TA-3's verification discipline
  has genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before confirming a
  borderline prefix case (e.g., "prevent," per MP-3), followed by an
  honest "I'm not sure that decomposes cleanly" judgment, is a good sign
  — it suggests the learner recognizes the genuine limits of prefix
  analysis for historically opaque, Latin-derived words rather than
  forcing a confident but incorrect decomposition. Instant, confident
  category assignment for every "re-"/"un-"/"pre-"-initial word with no
  hesitation at all is the concerning pattern for MC-A-PREFIX-ALWAYS-
  HAS-ONE-FIXED-MEANING.
- **Multi-category stakes**: real and central to TA-2/MP-2 — a learner
  who defaults to "not" or "opposite" for every prefix, even after
  correctly identifying category examples in structured practice, may
  still be under MC-PREFIXES-ONLY-NEGATE-MEANING's influence in
  unstructured, spontaneous use.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "is there actually a real base word
  here?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for MC-A-PREFIX-
  ALWAYS-HAS-ONE-FIXED-MEANING, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh prefix-like word is introduced, making the
  verification check a routinely-invited response rather than an
  afterthought.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a prefix-identification task shrinks to the
  Component 3 building-blocks anchor using the simplest, most familiar
  negation prefix available (e.g., "un-" + "happy"), with the tutor
  modeling the attachment and meaning change before the learner attempts
  a fresh prefix independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for MC-PREFIXES-ONLY-
  NEGATE-MEANING, a confident "it means not/opposite" response to a
  fresh non-negation prefix is more likely than "I don't know" — route
  directly to the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence probe rather than
  treating it as a generic vocabulary gap. For MC-A-PREFIX-ALWAYS-HAS-
  ONE-FIXED-MEANING, confidently applying the usual meaning to a
  coincidental non-prefix case (e.g., claiming "relax" means "lax
  again") is more likely than admitting uncertainty — route directly to
  TA-3's base-word-verification check.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who pauses and
  says something like "hmm, is there really a word underneath this?"
  when meeting an unfamiliar prefix-like word, without being prompted,
  is showing TA-3's verification discipline has generalized — this
  should be named and praised explicitly, per the Blueprint's own
  regulation-tail guidance (praising checking for a genuine base word
  over assuming a fixed prefix meaning).
