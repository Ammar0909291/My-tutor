# Connected Speech — `eng.phonetics.connected-speech`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.phonetics.connected-speech` (canonical English
  KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / phonetics / "Connected Speech" —
  the concept that reframes elision, assimilation, and linking not as
  errors or noise, but as the systematic, rule-governed mechanisms
  through which stress and intonation actually manifest in fluent,
  real-time speech.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.phonetics.intonation-patterns` — specifically
  the meaning-carrying-pitch model and the general habit of listening
  for sentence-level phonetic phenomena rather than word-by-word
  citation forms; load-bearing because connected speech is where
  stress and intonation actually occur — in the merged, reduced,
  linked stream of fluent utterances, not in isolated, carefully
  pronounced individual words.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): this concept has no `unlocks` entry within
  the phonetics domain in the raw KG data available; it functions as
  this domain's terminal concept ahead of `eng.phonetics.rhythm-and-
  timing`'s own separate branch, with its skills (recognizing and
  appropriately using elision, assimilation, and linking) consumed
  implicitly by every subsequent listening, speaking, and conversational
  concept across the English curriculum.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: analyze · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 3

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Recognize elision (sound-dropping) in common connected-speech
   forms and identify the underlying full-form words.
2. Recognize assimilation (a sound changing to match a neighboring
   sound) in common connected-speech forms.
3. Recognize linking (a consonant joining across a word boundary into a
   following vowel-initial word).
4. Judge whether a connected-speech reduction is appropriate for a
   given communicative context (casual conversation versus formal
   presentation), rather than treating any one register as universally
   correct.
5. Comprehend natural, connected fast speech containing multiple
   elision/assimilation/linking instances, correctly identifying the
   underlying full-form words despite the reductions.

## Core Understanding

Connected speech — the elision, assimilation, and linking that occur
when fluent speakers produce words in a continuous stream rather than
as isolated citation forms — is not careless or degraded pronunciation;
it is a REGULAR, RULE-GOVERNED feature of natural spoken English,
produced identically by careful and casual speakers alike, and its
absence (rigid, fully-separated word-by-word pronunciation even in
casual conversational contexts) is itself what sounds unnatural. A
second, equally load-bearing fact is that these changes are
SYSTEMATIC, not an unbounded list of one-off memorized forms — a small
number of underlying pattern TYPES (elision commonly drops certain
unstressed vowels or consonant clusters; assimilation commonly shifts a
sound's place of articulation to match a following sound; linking
commonly joins a final consonant into a following vowel-initial word)
recur across many different word combinations, so recognizing the
PATTERN TYPE, not merely memorizing "wanna" and "gonna" as isolated
forms, is what actually generalizes to new material. The concept's
primary practical value, per its own difficulty-appropriate
positioning at the analyze level of Bloom's taxonomy, is LISTENING
comprehension of real, natural speech — a learner whose prior exposure
has been primarily written or classroom-formal English will find
connected speech the single largest barrier to understanding fluent
native or near-native conversation.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "connected speech reductions are physical merges
  I can see and hear happening between words"**: installed by the
  Component 3 word-blender anchor, which makes an otherwise abstract,
  purely auditory phenomenon (two words physically brought together
  producing a merged form) visually and kinesthetically concrete.
- **Intermediate model — "these reductions follow a small number of
  systematic pattern TYPES, not isolated one-off forms"**: installed by
  TA-1 through TA-3, which explicitly name and categorize elision,
  assimilation, and linking as distinct, generalizable mechanisms,
  resolving MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-IS-RANDOM. Upgrade trigger: correctly
  predicting a novel word pair's connected-speech form (e.g., "kind
  of" → "kinda") by recognizing it as the same elision pattern already
  seen in "going to" → "gonna," rather than needing the specific new
  form separately taught.
- **Advanced model — "connected-speech reduction is a legitimate,
  context-appropriate register choice, not an error"**: installed by
  TA-4's register-appropriateness judgment, resolving
  MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-CHANGES-ARE-SLOPPY-SPEECH — the learner holds
  both the reduced casual form and the fuller formal form as equally
  correct, differing only in which communicative context calls for
  which. Upgrade trigger: correctly judging that a casual-register form
  ("wanna") would be inappropriate in a specific formal context, while
  still affirming that the casual form itself is not an error in its
  own context.
- **Expert model — "I can comprehend natural fast speech despite heavy
  reduction, correctly recovering the underlying full-form words"**:
  installed by TA-5, the concept's genuine functional capstone and, per
  the Blueprint's own explicit Adaptive Flag, this concept's primary
  real-world value — production awareness (TA-4) matters, but
  comprehension of real, natural speech is the skill this entire
  concept most directly serves.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner not yet reliably recognizing
  elision and assimilation as SYSTEMATIC pattern types (TA-1/TA-2)
  should not be given TA-4's register-judgment task — appropriately
  choosing between a reduced and a fuller form presupposes the
  reduced form is already understood as a genuine, patterned
  alternative rather than an error to be corrected uniformly.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here. The sloppy-speech
failure exists because a learner's formal English instruction —
including much of the careful, citation-form pronunciation modeled
throughout the earlier phonics and phonetics concepts in this very
curriculum — has consistently reinforced full, separated, "correct"
pronunciation as the standard to aim for, so encountering systematic
deviation from that standard in fluent native speech is reasonably, if
incorrectly, interpreted as a departure FROM correctness rather than a
different, equally legitimate register operating by its own rules. The
random-noise failure exists because, without the pattern-type
vocabulary this concept provides, each individual connected-speech form
("gonna," "wanna," "gimme," "didja") genuinely does present as an
unrelated, arbitrary-seeming memorization item — a learner has no
structural reason to suspect that dozens of seemingly distinct reduced
forms are actually small variations on three underlying mechanisms
until those mechanisms are explicitly named and demonstrated to recur.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.phonetics.connected-speech.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-CHANGES-ARE-SLOPPY-SPEECH (Blueprint Component 1)
— Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — "careful,
  full, separated pronunciation is the correct standard" is genuinely
  valid and consistently reinforced across nearly every prior phonics/
  phonetics concept in this curriculum (where citation-form,
  articulated-clearly production was always the teaching target), and
  the learner extends that standard, unmodified, into the domain of
  natural casual speech, where a genuinely different, equally
  legitimate register operates by its own rules.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, after hearing a
  fluent speaker use natural connected forms, asks "wait, is that
  actually okay to say?" (rather than assuming it must be an error) is
  showing genuine, productive uncertainty — a more advanced state than
  a learner who confidently insists the fluent speaker's natural speech
  is simply incorrect.

**MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-IS-RANDOM (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 2,
perceptual intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: this is a genuine perceptual/structural
  gap rather than an overextended rule — without the specific pattern-
  type vocabulary (elision, assimilation, linking) this concept
  provides, a learner has no available category system to organize
  what they hear, so each new reduced form genuinely does present as
  an isolated surprise; the misconception exists not because the
  learner applied a wrong framework, but because no framework was yet
  available to apply.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, on meeting a
  genuinely novel connected-speech form for the first time, spontaneously
  says "oh, that's like the 'going to'/'gonna' thing" (correctly
  transferring the pattern-type recognition to new material without
  being told to) is showing the systematic model has fully
  generalized — a stronger and more advanced signal than correctly
  identifying only the specific examples explicitly taught.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the word-blender anchor itself is the analogy, not a
  supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): two hands physically
  brought together while saying two words quickly, producing a merged
  form, directly instantiates the concept's central mechanism.
  Breaking point: a simple hand-merge gesture captures elision and
  linking reasonably well but understates assimilation's specific
  mechanism (a sound CHANGING to resemble its neighbor, not merely
  merging with it) — this entry supplements the word-blender anchor
  with TA-2's own explicit sound-change framing for assimilation
  specifically, rather than relying on the merge gesture to convey all
  three pattern types identically.
- **Alternative — a river merging with tributaries, still the same
  water, just flowing together**: individual words remain
  recognizable "underneath" the merged surface form, the way tributary
  water is still water even after joining the main river — useful for
  reinforcing that the underlying full-form words are recoverable, not
  destroyed. Breaking point: rivers merge permanently, whereas a fluent
  speaker can readily choose to "un-merge" back into the fuller,
  formal form when register calls for it — the analogy should not
  suggest the reduction is irreversible.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe connected-speech forms as
  "shortcuts" or "corner-cutting"**: this framing directly installs
  MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-CHANGES-ARE-SLOPPY-SPEECH by implying the reduced
  form represents an incomplete or inferior version of the "real"
  pronunciation. Connected-speech forms should always be introduced as
  a genuine, complete, rule-governed register in their own right, never
  as an abbreviated or degraded version of something else.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/auditory (word-blender)**: the Blueprint's Component 3
  anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: the two-hands-merging gesture requires nothing
  beyond the learner's own hands and voice; any two words can be
  practiced this way.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's register-judgment task and
  TA-5's listening-comprehension task are both hands-on, evaluative
  activities requiring the learner to judge appropriateness or recover
  meaning, not merely recognize pre-labeled examples.
- **Teacher-demo (register contrast)**: the tutor models the same
  content ("I want to go") first in a casual, reduced register
  ("I wanna go") and then in a formal, fully-separated register,
  explicitly naming both as correct for their respective contexts —
  directly instantiating the Blueprint's own register-appropriateness
  framing.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  connected-speech pattern's classification (TA-1/TA-2/TA-3) or a
  register judgment (TA-4), ask the learner to predict before the tutor
  confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits the systematic-pattern insight;
direct instruction is correct for the specific pattern-type
definitions.** The insight that seemingly unrelated reduced forms share
a small number of underlying mechanisms is a genuine, statable
regularity well-suited to felt discovery: **need** (the learner,
confused by "gonna" and separately confused by "gimme," is asked
whether these are related or unrelated phenomena); **playground/
invention** (TA-1's several elision examples let the learner notice a
shared reduction pattern across different word pairs); **collision**
(the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence probe, directly comparing
"going to"→"gonna" and "give me"→"gimme" for their shared mechanism);
**formalization** (naming the three pattern types explicitly — elision,
assimilation, linking); **compression** (TA-5's fluent listening
comprehension of natural speech without needing to consciously
classify every reduction encountered). The specific DEFINITIONS of
elision, assimilation, and linking themselves are convention-bound
linguistic terminology best introduced by direct instruction once the
underlying phenomena have already been noticed and felt.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (elision) must precede TA-2 (assimilation), which must precede
TA-3 (linking), introducing the three pattern types one at a time so
each can be recognized and generalized in isolation before being
combined — attempting all three simultaneously would risk the learner
conflating genuinely distinct mechanisms (dropping a sound entirely
versus a sound changing to resemble a neighbor versus two sounds
joining across a boundary). TA-4 (register-appropriateness judgment) is
placed after all three pattern types are recognized, since judging
WHEN a reduction is appropriate presupposes the learner already
recognizes reductions as legitimate, patterned phenomena rather than
errors — introducing register judgment before this recognition is
secure would risk the learner concluding formal register is simply
"more correct" rather than differently appropriate. TA-5 (listening
comprehension) is placed last as the concept's genuine functional
capstone, per the Blueprint's own explicit statement that this is the
concept's primary real-world value — comprehending natural fast speech
requires all three pattern types and the register-appropriateness
framing to be operating together. This entry does not restate the
Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see
Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner, when asked to describe
  or predict a connected-speech form, references the PATTERN TYPE
  (elision, assimilation, linking) rather than treating the specific
  instance as an isolated fact — this categorical framing is the
  primary diagnostic signal for whether MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-IS-RANDOM
  has resolved.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a pause before judging register
  appropriateness (TA-4), followed by a reasoned explanation
  referencing the communicative context, is a good sign — it suggests
  the learner is weighing context deliberately rather than applying a
  single "correct form" reflex.
- **Pronunciation stakes**: distinctive for this concept — unlike most
  prior phonics/phonetics work, the goal here explicitly includes
  RECOGNIZING and appropriately using REDUCED forms, not always
  producing the fullest, clearest pronunciation; a learner who insists
  on fully separated forms even in casual contexts is demonstrating
  the concept's own targeted misconception, not simply being careful.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "is this a mistake, or is it just a
  different, correct way of saying it in this context?" is this
  concept's load-bearing sentence, directly addressing the register-
  legitimacy question, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3`.

## Assessment Signals

- **Diagnostic (golden probe)**: the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence
  probe (Component 1) — comparing "going to"→"gonna" against "give
  me"→"gimme" for a shared underlying mechanism — cleanly separates a
  learner still treating each reduction as an isolated memorized item
  from one recognizing the systematic pattern type.
- **Guided practice → independent practice**: the Blueprint's WE-1 → WE-2
  → WE-3 → MP-1 through MP-5 fading ladder is reused directly
  (Components 5–6) — not re-authored here.
- **Mastery gate set** (`../../assessment/05-mastery-verification.md
  §3`): elision recognition (fresh example, MP-1); assimilation
  recognition (MP-2); linking recognition (MP-3); register judgment
  (MP-4 — a strong diagnostic item, since correctly reasoning about
  context-appropriateness rather than declaring one form universally
  correct demonstrates the advanced, non-judgmental model); explanation
  (MP-5, requiring articulation that connected speech is systematic and
  legitimate, and that understanding it serves listening comprehension).
- **Interpretation of response pattern**: fast-and-correct
  identification of a novel connected-speech form's pattern TYPE
  (not merely its surface form) confirms the systematic model is
  internalized. A response that correctly recognizes a reduced form
  but still judges it as an "error" or "incorrect" is the dangerous
  pattern for MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-CHANGES-ARE-SLOPPY-SPEECH, warranting
  a return to TA-4's register-legitimacy framing rather than more
  pattern-recognition drilling. A response treating a novel reduction
  as a wholly unrelated, unpredictable surprise (rather than
  recognizing it as an instance of an already-known pattern type) is
  the dangerous pattern for MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-IS-RANDOM.
- **Transfer items**: near (a fresh, previously-unpracticed elision/
  assimilation/linking example); far (a genuinely novel listening
  passage containing multiple connected-speech instances simultaneously,
  testing whether comprehension generalizes to real, unscripted-feeling
  speech); real-world (identifying connected-speech reductions the
  learner encounters in media, conversation, or podcasts already
  consumed outside the lesson).

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a pattern-recognition task shrinks to the
  Component 3 word-blender demonstration using the most familiar
  example available (typically "want to"→"wanna," the first taught),
  with the tutor modeling the two-hands-merging gesture and the
  natural pronunciation together.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: a learner treating a
  novel reduced form as confusing or "wrong" (rather than "I don't
  know") is more likely, per MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-CHANGES-ARE-SLOPPY-
  SPEECH's mechanism — route directly to that misconception's recovery
  (the register-legitimacy framing) rather than treating it as a
  generic comprehension gap. A learner treating a novel form as an
  unrelated surprise (rather than connecting it to an already-known
  pattern type) is more likely than genuine confusion about the sounds
  themselves — redirect explicitly to the pattern-type classification
  question ("is this elision, assimilation, or linking, like the
  examples we've already seen?").
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously attempts the two-hands-merging gesture themselves when
  encountering a new reduced form, without being prompted, is showing
  the word-blender model has already generalized into a self-directed
  analysis tool — this should be named and praised explicitly.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: concept/procedure hybrid — the three pattern-type
CATEGORIES (elision, assimilation, linking) are concepts reviewed
through continued application to novel word combinations (not simple
recall, since the underlying mechanism must be re-recognized each
time), while listening comprehension of connected speech is a
procedure/tool-skill benefiting from ongoing, varied exposure rather
than one-time correct demonstration, since real fluent speech continues
to present new specific instances of the same underlying patterns
throughout a learner's ongoing language development. Interleaving
partners: mix all three pattern types within the same practice and
listening materials (never block by type), and periodically mix
register-judgment tasks with pure pattern-recognition tasks so neither
skill is practiced in isolation from the other.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: recognizing fresh elision, assimilation, and linking
  instances in new word combinations.
- **Far**: this concept's systematic recognition of connected-speech
  patterns is the direct foundation for genuine listening comprehension
  of natural, unscripted spoken English across every subsequent
  `eng.listening.*` concept in the curriculum, none of which can be
  fully mastered while a learner still perceives fluent native speech
  as an unpredictable stream of dropped or merged sounds.
- **Real-world**: virtually every instance of fluent, natural spoken
  English the learner encounters — conversation, media, podcasts, film
  dialogue — contains connected-speech phenomena, making this concept's
  practice domain genuinely constant and ungated.
- **Expert-transfer**: fully fluent, register-flexible spoken production
  (naturally shifting between casual and formal forms as context
  requires) and effortless comprehension of fast, natural speech, both
  well beyond this node, depend on this concept's systematic framework
  becoming fully automatic.
