## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify elision (sound-dropping) in a connected-speech example and recognise that the same reduction pattern generalises across similar word combinations.
2. Identify assimilation (a sound changing to match a neighbouring sound) and generalise the pattern to novel word pairs.
3. Identify linking (a consonant joining across a word boundary into a following vowel-initial word).
4. Judge whether a connected-speech reduction is appropriate for a given register (casual conversation versus formal presentation), recognising both as legitimate.
5. Comprehend a natural, connected-speech utterance by correctly recovering its full-form words despite the reductions present.

## Core Understanding

Connected speech is the set of **systematic, rule-governed sound changes** that occur when words are produced in natural sequence rather than in careful isolation, and its three named categories each describe a genuine, generalisable pattern rather than an unrelated list of special cases: **elision** is the dropping of a sound (or unstressed vowel) at a word boundary or within a cluster ("want to" → "wanna"; "next day" → "nex' day"); **assimilation** is a sound changing to become more similar to a neighbouring sound ("did you" → "didja," where /d/ and /j/ merge toward /dʒ/); **linking** is a sound joining smoothly across a word boundary, most often a final consonant carrying into a following vowel-initial word ("turn it off" → "tur-ni-toff"). The concept's second, equally load-bearing structural fact is that these changes are **not corruptions of "correct" English** — they are regular, describable features of fluent speech produced by careful and casual speakers alike, and their appropriateness is a matter of **register**, not correctness: "wanna" is the natural, expected form in casual conversation, and "want to" is the natural, expected form in a formal presentation, with neither one more "correct" than the other in an absolute sense. This concept's primary, most consequential practical value is **listening comprehension**: a learner who has only ever practised recognising words in their careful, citation-form pronunciation will find genuine, natural connected speech - the actual speech most fluent conversation consists of - systematically harder to understand than isolated-word listening practice would predict, independent of vocabulary or grammar knowledge.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "connected-speech reductions are sloppy pronunciation to avoid; the 'real,' correct pronunciation is the careful, separated, citation form."**
The runnable simulation: treat any reduced or blended form as an error to be corrected toward the fuller, separated pronunciation, even in casual contexts where the reduced form is the actual native-speaker default. This is not tutor-installed — it is a reasonable and common consequence of formal pronunciation instruction, which very often teaches and models only the careful, citation form, leaving the learner with no positive model for (and sometimes an explicit prior warning against) the equally legitimate reduced forms.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — listening to a natural or fluent speaker say "What are you going to do?" and noticing that sounds blend, drop, and link even in careful, non-careless conversational speech.
*Shelf-life warning at replacement*: "The careful, separated form isn't more correct — it's the right choice for some contexts. The blended form is equally correct for others."

**Intermediate — "I can recognise that connected-speech changes are legitimate, not sloppy, and I treat each new reduction I encounter as its own separate thing to learn and remember."**
The simulation: correctly abandon the correctness-based objection to reduced forms, while still processing each new example (wanna, gimme, didja) as an isolated, unconnected fact rather than an instance of one of three named, generalisable pattern types. This model has resolved the register misconception and not yet met the systematicity insight.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — noticing that "going to" → "gonna" and "want to" → "wanna" share the same underlying reduction pattern, rather than being two unrelated memorised chunks.
*Shelf-life warning*: "These aren't separate facts to memorise one by one. A small number of pattern types — elision, assimilation, linking — cover almost everything you'll meet."

**Advanced — "connected speech is a systematic, three-category phenomenon (elision, assimilation, linking), register-appropriate rather than correct-or-incorrect, and its primary practical value is enabling comprehension of natural, fast, real speech."**
The simulation: given a novel connected-speech example, the learner correctly classifies it into one of the three pattern types, judges its register-appropriateness for a given context, and — most practically — can recover the intended full-form words from a natural utterance despite the reductions present. This is the target model, matching TA-4 and TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: `eng.phonetics.rhythm-and-timing` and `eng.speaking.oral-fluency` (the two direct unlocks), where connected speech's individual sound changes must now be coordinated with the further, larger-scale phenomena of overall speech rhythm and fluent, real-time production.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gets you the individual sound changes. There's a further layer of overall timing and rhythm across extended speech, and a further step of producing all of this yourself, fluently, in real time."

**Expert — "connected-speech phenomena are cross-linguistically common (most, if not all, spoken languages exhibit elision, assimilation, and linking in some form), and their specific triggering environments (which consonant clusters elide, which consonants assimilate to which following sounds) are governed by genuine articulatory-phonetic principles (ease of articulation, minimisation of gestural effort) rather than being English-specific arbitrary conventions."**
Named here to mark the arc; this concept deliberately teaches the English-specific patterns as a bounded, practical set rather than attempting the general cross-linguistic articulatory account, which belongs to more advanced phonetics.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant failure mechanism is that **formal pronunciation instruction has, for many learners, exclusively modelled the careful, citation form**, with no positive exposure to reduced forms as anything other than an error to be corrected — a learner who has been repeatedly told to "pronounce it properly, don't say 'gonna'" has received consistent, well-intentioned instruction that happens to be incomplete: it correctly identifies the careful form as appropriate for some contexts while incorrectly implying it is the only correct form for all contexts. This is not a comprehension failure on the learner's part; it is the direct, expected residue of a genuinely common instructional gap.

The second mechanism, once the register misconception is resolved, is that connected-speech examples are frequently taught and encountered **as an unstructured list** ("wanna," "gonna," "gimme," "didja," each introduced as its own item), with the underlying pattern-type structure left implicit or unstated — a learner meeting each new reduced form as an isolated surprise has no basis for predicting or recognising a genuinely novel reduction they have not specifically been taught, precisely because nothing has connected the specific examples to the smaller number of general pattern types that would let them generalise.

The third mechanism, and the one the Blueprint's own Component 8 correctly identifies as this concept's highest-stakes practical consequence, concerns **listening comprehension specifically for classroom-taught L2 learners**: a learner whose primary English exposure has been formal, written, or careful spoken instruction has, in a very concrete sense, never been taught to *hear* connected speech, meaning genuine, natural conversation — full of exactly the reductions this concept describes — can remain a persistent comprehension barrier even after vocabulary and grammar are otherwise strong, since the barrier is perceptual and pattern-recognition-based, not knowledge-based in the usual sense.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns the trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-CHANGES-ARE-SLOPPY-SPEECH
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-CHANGES-ARE-SLOPPY-SPEECH — includes the "want to"/"wanna" and mumbling-versus-systematic-reduction discrimination pairs.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced) — directly analogous to the same birth type already identified at `eng.phonetics.intonation-patterns`' all-questions-rise misconception: a genuinely well-intentioned prior instructional emphasis (careful, citation-form pronunciation) has correctly taught one register while implicitly or explicitly under-representing the other, equally legitimate register.
- **Teaching consequence of the birth type**: exactly as at the intonation-patterns concept, the repair must **add a second, register-specific legitimacy alongside the first, not overturn the first as an error** — the Blueprint's own replacement text does this precisely, explicitly preserving "very formal or deliberately clear speech may use fuller, separated forms" as fully correct, which protects the learner's prior careful-speech instruction from feeling invalidated.
- **Verification of death**: given a genuinely novel casual-register context, the learner both recognises a natural reduction as expected (rather than as an error to note) and, separately, correctly identifies a formal-register context where the fuller form would be more appropriate — the *dual* recognition, not merely acceptance of reduced forms generally, is the actual target evidence, since accepting reduced forms as always appropriate would be an equally incomplete, merely inverted, misconception.

### MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-IS-RANDOM
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-IS-RANDOM — includes the gonna/gimme and didja/wouldja generalisation examples.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 3 (language contamination), of a specific and instructive kind — not contamination of a single term's meaning, but contamination by *presentation format*: connected-speech examples are conventionally taught and encountered as a list of individually-named colloquial forms ("gonna," "wanna," "gimme"), and the list format itself, absent any explicit pattern-type structure imposed on it, invites the reasonable inference that each item is its own independent, unrelated fact.
- **Teaching consequence**: because the contamination comes from *how the material is conventionally organised* rather than from any single misleading word or symbol, the repair requires **explicitly restructuring the presentation around the three pattern types from the very first examples**, exactly as the Blueprint's own TA-1 does (deliberately pairing "going to"→"gonna" with "want to"→"wanna" as two instances of the same elision pattern, rather than introducing them as separate, sequential items) — this is a presentation-design repair as much as a conceptual one.
- **Verification of death**: given a genuinely novel connected-speech example not previously drilled, the learner correctly names which of the three pattern types (elision, assimilation, linking) it belongs to, rather than only recognising or reproducing the specific reduced form — the *classification*, not merely correct recognition, is the evidence the underlying pattern-type structure (rather than a growing list of memorised specific forms) has been internalised.

## Analogies

**Best — formal handwriting versus quick, natural cursive, both used by the same person for different purposes.** A person's careful, deliberate print handwriting (used for a formal letter) and their fast, natural, letters-flowing-into-each-other cursive (used for a quick personal note) are both genuinely that person's real handwriting — neither is "sloppier" or more correct than the other; each simply suits a different occasion. Connected speech's reduced forms are the spoken equivalent of natural cursive: genuine, fluent, and entirely appropriate for their context, not a degraded version of the "real" careful form.
*Breaking point*: handwriting styles are a matter of personal choice and practice; connected-speech reductions are largely automatic, unconscious, register-triggered behaviour most fluent speakers don't consciously select — useful for the *both-genuine, neither-degraded* framing, not for implying equal deliberate control.

**Alternative — a river's main, direct channel versus its smaller, natural side-eddies and joins where tributaries merge in.** Water doesn't travel in a perfectly straight, separated line; where flows meet, they genuinely blend, and the blending is not a flaw in the river, it's how rivers actually behave. Elision, assimilation, and linking are the "natural blending points" of speech, not a flaw in how words are being said.
*Breaking point*: river-blending is a passive, physical process with no equivalent to *register* (a river doesn't blend differently depending on formality) — useful for the *natural, systematic blending* intuition only, not for the register-appropriateness half of this concept.

**Story analogy — three different, genuine reasons two dance partners' movements blend together (they lean into each other, they mirror the beat, they hold hands across the gap) rather than one vague "they're just dancing sloppily."** Elision (a step dropped when the rhythm doesn't need it), assimilation (one partner's movement shifting to match the other's), and linking (a hand bridging the space between two dancers) are each a distinct, nameable reason movement blends — exactly paralleling the three named connected-speech pattern types, giving the learner three specific things to listen for rather than one vague impression of "blurriness."
*Breaking point*: none serious; a reasonably close structural mapping to the three-category taxonomy this concept teaches.

**Visual analogy — the word-blender** (Blueprint Component 3): two hands, each representing a separate word, physically brought together while the words are spoken together naturally and quickly, producing the blended form. Not a metaphor but the actual working instrument this concept's early teaching is organised around, making an auditory blending process visible and concrete.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Connected speech is just lazy talking."** Directly installs MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-CHANGES-ARE-SLOPPY-SPEECH in exactly the words the misconception's own trigger signal describes — "lazy" carries the same correctness-judgement this concept exists to remove.
- **"There are dozens of these reductions to learn, one by one."** Presents the material in exactly the unstructured-list format identified above as the direct cause of MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-IS-RANDOM, reinforcing rather than repairing the pattern-blindness this concept must correct.
- **"Native speakers just talk fast and you have to get used to it."** Frames the entire phenomenon as an unanalysable speed effect rather than a set of specific, describable, rule-governed sound changes — this framing offers the learner nothing actionable and directly undercuts the concept's core claim that connected speech is systematic and learnable, not merely something to "get used to."

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The word-blender (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there, using "want" + "to," "going" + "to," and "give" + "me." *Predict first*, before each blend: "If I say these two words together, naturally and quickly, what do you think they'll turn into?"
2. **The careful-speaker collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence. *Predict first*: "Do you think a careful, fluent speaker pronounces every word separately in natural conversation?" Then listen to or model a natural utterance and identify the reductions present.
3. **The gonna/wanna pattern-generalisation task (learner activity, TA-1).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Do you think 'going to' becoming 'gonna' and 'want to' becoming 'wanna' are related in some way, or two separate, unrelated things?" Then confirm the shared elision pattern together.
4. **The register-judgement task (learner activity, TA-4).** *Predict first*: "Would you say 'gonna' while giving a formal presentation to executives, or while chatting with a friend?" Then discuss the reasoning, not merely the answer.

## Discovery Questions

Following the pattern established at multiple phonetics-domain concepts in this program, this concept splits: **the specific inventory of common reduced forms is largely surveyed directly** (told and demonstrated, since there is no discoverable reason "going to" specifically reduces to "gonna" rather than some other form), but **the underlying three-category pattern structure is genuinely well suited to guided discovery**, since the evidence (several examples sharing an underlying mechanism) is directly available to the learner without requiring the pattern names to be supplied in advance.

1. **Need**: "Are 'gonna,' 'wanna,' and 'gimme' three totally separate, unrelated things to remember, or is something similar happening in all three?"
2. **Playground**: the learner examines several reduced forms (gonna, wanna, gimme, didja) freely, comparing them against their full forms.
3. **Invention**: "Which of these seem to work the same way as each other?" The learner sorts the examples into groups based on their own noticed similarities — some involve a sound simply disappearing (elision), some involve a sound changing to match its neighbour (assimilation).
4. **Collision**: present a genuinely novel example (e.g., "kind of" → "kinda") and ask the learner to classify it using their own just-invented groupings, testing whether the grouping generalises.
5. **Formalization**: name the three pattern types — elision, assimilation, linking — as the small, learnable set of mechanisms underlying the whole phenomenon.
6. **Compression**: "Ask which of the three it is — dropped, changed-to-match, or joined-across — never memorise it as its own separate fact."

## Teaching Sequence

The pedagogical logic behind this arc:

- **Elision (TA-1) is introduced first, and deliberately used as the vehicle for confronting MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-IS-RANDOM before assimilation or linking are introduced** — the Blueprint's own sequencing pairs "going to"→"gonna" with "want to"→"wanna" specifically to establish the pattern-generalisation habit on the simplest, most familiar category before the learner meets the other two, structurally different pattern types.
- **Assimilation (TA-2) and linking (TA-3) follow, each introduced with the same pattern-first framing already established at TA-1**, rather than as a fresh, unstructured list — this consistency in presentation is itself the ongoing repair for the second misconception, not a one-time correction delivered only at TA-1.
- **Register-appropriateness judgement (TA-4) is deliberately sequenced after all three pattern types are recognised, not folded into the pattern-recognition work itself** — separating "can you identify what kind of change this is" from "is this change appropriate here" allows either skill to be diagnosed independently, and prevents the learner from conflating pattern-recognition ability with a judgement about correctness.
- **Listening comprehension of extended natural speech (TA-5) comes last and is explicitly named, per the Blueprint's own Component 8 flag, as this concept's primary real-world payoff** — reserving the most integrative, most practically valuable task for the concept's culminating stage matches the general sequencing principle established throughout this program, and the flag's explicit prioritisation (proportionate emphasis for classroom-taught L2 learners specifically) should shape how much session time this final stage receives relative to the earlier, more analytical stages.

Turn-by-turn scripting, protocol routing, and adaptive flags: Blueprint **Component 7 — Session Architecture** and **Component 8 — Adaptive Flags** (in particular the listening-comprehension-first priority, the systematic-not-exception-by-exception framing, the register-not-correctness frame, and the exposure-type-adjusted emphasis for S9 learners).

## Tutor Actions

In recommended order, from `../teaching-actions/`:

1. **Demonstration** (SHOW) — the word-blender and the careful-speaker collision, both benefiting from being heard aloud, with the blender ideally learner-performed (physically bringing hands together while producing the blend) rather than only observed.
2. **Concept Map** (ORGANIZE) — a running three-category classification (elision / assimilation / linking) of every example encountered, functioning as this concept's core organising artefact and the direct, ongoing repair for MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-IS-RANDOM.
3. **Prediction** (TEST-THINKING) — predicting a blended form before hearing it, and predicting register-appropriateness before confirming, both directly exercising the target skills.
4. **Matching** (DO) — full form to reduced form, and reduced form to correct pattern-type classification, with the bidirectional check (does the learner's classification generalise to a genuinely novel example) carrying real diagnostic value.
5. **Error Analysis** (TEST-THINKING) — the Blueprint's own TA-5 listening task is fundamentally an error-analysis-style exercise in reverse: recovering an intended full form from a reduced one, directly exercising the target comprehension skill.

**Does not fit**: **Worked Example** in the derivation sense — there is no multi-step calculation, only a perceptual-and-classification skill better served by Demonstration and Matching. **Game** before the three pattern types are independently secure — speed pressure risks rewarding surface-level recognition of specific drilled forms over genuine pattern-type classification.

## Voice Teaching Notes

This concept's core evidence is **audio**, and specifically the fine-grained acoustic detail of sound-boundary behaviour — exactly the kind of signal the runtime's plain-text STT capture gap (`../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §7`) is least equipped to preserve, since a transcription-only channel that normalises "gonna" to "going to" (or vice versa) discards precisely the phenomenon this concept studies before it ever reaches a decision layer.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **Careful, fully-separated pronunciation of every word in a casual, conversational practice context** ("want... to" produced crisply and separately when a natural, blended "wanna" would be expected). The direct behavioural signature of MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-CHANGES-ARE-SLOPPY-SPEECH, and it should be met by returning to the register-judgement framing rather than simply modelling the reduced form again.
- **Correct production or recognition of a specific drilled reduced form, with visible hesitation or an incorrect guess on a genuinely novel one of the same pattern type.** This differential pattern — success on familiar items, failure on structurally-identical novel ones — is the direct signature of MC-CONNECTED-SPEECH-IS-RANDOM, and it should route to explicit pattern-type re-classification practice, not to drilling the specific new example in isolation.
- **Confident recovery of full-form words from a natural, reduced connected-speech listening passage.** The target state for this concept's primary practical goal, and it should be distinguished from mere familiarity with a specific passage by periodically testing genuinely novel audio.
- **Register-appropriate code-switching between careful and reduced forms across different practice contexts within the same session** (correctly using "wanna" in a role-played casual conversation and "want to" in a role-played formal one). Strong positive evidence that both the systematicity and the register-appropriateness insights have jointly landed.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Ask which of the three patterns this is, and whether this moment calls for the careful form or the natural one — never whether it's correct."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads, which the Blueprint does not own:

- **Elision (MP-1) and assimilation (MP-2), novel examples.** *Correct, with the learner explicitly naming the pattern type (elision or assimilation)* → strong evidence the classification, not merely the specific form, has generalised. *Correct with no stated classification* → treat as weaker evidence per this program's established practice for novel-application items; the correct answer alone cannot distinguish genuine pattern generalisation from a lucky guess informed by surface similarity to a drilled example.
- **Linking (MP-3).** Linking is structurally the most different of the three categories (a boundary phenomenon rather than a within- or across-word sound change), and a learner who succeeds on elision and assimilation but struggles specifically here has a real, distinct gap worth addressing separately, not folded into general "connected speech is hard" feedback.
- **Register judgement (MP-4).** *Correct, with reasoning referencing the specific context (formal presentation, executives) rather than a blanket rule* → the strongest evidence for this misconception's repair, since a blanket rule ("always use the full form when speaking to important people") would itself be an oversimplification risking a new, if less damaging, overgeneralisation.
- **Explanation (MP-5).** Listen for whether the explanation names both required elements — that connected speech is systematic (not random) and register-appropriate (not simply correct or incorrect) — a response naming only one has an incomplete model even if prior items were answered correctly by other means.

**Mastery certification trigger**: correct identification and classification (elision, assimilation, or linking) of a novel example of each of the three pattern types; correct, contextually-reasoned register judgement on a novel scenario; and successful recovery of the intended full-form words from a genuinely novel natural connected-speech listening passage. The listening-comprehension item is essential and should not be treated as optional or secondary — per the Blueprint's own explicit Component 8 designation, it is this concept's primary real-world value, and a learner who can classify examples analytically but cannot yet comprehend natural speech has not met the concept's actual practical goal.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"I was taught that 'gonna' is bad English"** — take this as an accurate report of genuine, well-intentioned prior instruction, not a misconception to be corrected sharply; respond by adding rather than contradicting: "Whoever taught you that wasn't wrong that 'going to' is the right choice sometimes — for formal writing or speech, definitely. 'Gonna' is just the equally correct choice for casual conversation. Both are real, correct English, for different moments."
- **"There are too many of these to remember"** — a reasonable reaction if the learner is still tracking connected-speech examples as an unstructured, growing list; the correct response redirects to the three-category structure rather than offering reassurance about effort: "You're not tracking one huge list — you're tracking three types. Which of the three do you think this one is?"
- **The smaller question to shrink to**: from full pattern classification or listening comprehension, down to **the word-blender demonstration on a single, already-familiar pair, with the learner only confirming "did that blend into one thing, or stay two?"** This removes the classification and comprehension demands entirely, isolating the most basic perceptual fact (blending happens) before returning to the harder combined tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just try to remember the specific reduced forms you've heard before."** This directly reinstates the unstructured-list approach this concept's second misconception consists of.

## Memory Hooks

- **Concept type**: classification system (three pattern types, each covering many specific instances) plus a **listening-comprehension fluency skill** requiring genuine, real-time perceptual processing of natural speech, plus a **contextual judgement skill** (register-appropriateness) requiring situational reasoning rather than fixed recall.
- **Review form — pattern classification**: spaced items always presenting genuinely novel examples across all three categories in the same review cycle, never drilling one category exhaustively before moving to the next, mirroring the interleaving principle established throughout this program's rule-application concepts.
- **Review form — register judgement**: review using varied, specific scenarios (not a repeated abstract "formal versus casual" choice) to continue exercising contextual reasoning rather than a memorised blanket rule.
- **Review form — listening comprehension**: this is best reinforced through continued exposure to genuinely natural audio or speech (not scripted, deliberately-slowed examples), since the target skill is real-time comprehension under authentic conditions, and a review protocol using only careful, deliberate model speech would not exercise or reveal regression in the actual target skill.
