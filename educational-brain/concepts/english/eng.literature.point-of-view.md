## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify a text's point of view (first, second, third limited, third omniscient) using specific textual evidence.
2. Identify a textual clue revealing a first-person narrator's potential bias or incompleteness.
3. Classify third-person narration as limited or omniscient based on scope of access to characters' inner thoughts, not pronoun alone.
4. Explain what a reader can and cannot know as a result of a specific point-of-view choice.
5. Fully analyze a novel passage's point of view, including potential narrator unreliability and scope of knowledge.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **a first-person narrator's account is not automatically complete, objective, or fully reliable** — a first-person narrator's knowledge is inherently limited to their own perspective, and their account can be incomplete, mistaken, or even deliberately misleading; a careful reader watches for gaps, inconsistencies, or self-serving framing rather than accepting the narrator's own words at face value. Second, **third person limited and third person omniscient are not "basically the same" just because both use "he/she" instead of "I"** — the crucial difference is SCOPE OF KNOWLEDGE: limited restricts the narrator to one character's inner thoughts, while omniscient grants unrestricted access to multiple characters' thoughts and information beyond what any single character could know; the pronoun used is only the shared surface label, while the actual scope of access is the real distinguishing feature. Both corrections require attending to a text's actual narrative access and reliability rather than surface features (person, pronoun) alone.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "a first-person narrator always tells the complete, unbiased truth; third person limited and omniscient are basically the same, since both use he/she."**
The simulation: accept a first-person account at face value with no critical scrutiny, and classify any third-person narration as one undifferentiated category.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a first-person narrator's flattering self-description contradicted by another character's reaction, asked whether the narrator's account is necessarily complete; and two third-person passages, one revealing one character's thoughts and one revealing multiple characters' thoughts, asked whether the narrator's access works the same way in both.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A first-person narrator's account may be limited or biased. Check SCOPE of access, not just pronoun, for third-person narration."

**Intermediate — "I know first-person can be biased now, but I still sometimes classify third-person narration by pronoun alone rather than checking actual scope of access."**
The simulation: correctly reads first-person critically but hasn't yet reliably tracked scope of access across a third-person passage.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct scope-tracking practice, given three third-person passages requiring the learner to identify whose thoughts are revealed in each.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Don't classify by pronoun. Track whose inner thoughts the narrator actually reveals."

**Advanced — "I identify point of view by textual evidence, detect first-person bias, classify third-person scope correctly, and explain what the reader can and cannot know as a result — on a novel passage."**
The simulation: integrates identification, bias-detection, and scope-classification into one coherent point-of-view analysis. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: none within this concept — confirmed terminal, though the Blueprint frames it as a foundational analytical tool applicable to any narrative text going forward.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you a foundational lens for reading any narrative — apply it whenever you read fiction."

**Expert — "at fluency, point-of-view identification and its knowledge-limit implications happen as an integrated part of ordinary literary reading."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit identify-critique-explain routine is a scaffold for a reading habit that, at true fluency, operates as an integrated part of literary comprehension.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **first-person narration is presented in the character's own direct voice, which strongly resembles how genuinely truthful, unmediated communication normally sounds** (unlike, say, a clearly biased third-party account), so a reader's normal trust calibration for direct, first-person testimony transfers uncritically into fiction, where the narrator's own limitations remain hidden unless the reader actively looks for them.

The second mechanism is that **the pronoun system ("I" versus "he/she") is the single most visible, easily-noticed textual feature distinguishing narrative styles**, so it becomes a convenient but insufficient proxy for the actual underlying distinction (scope of access to characters' minds), which requires tracking information across a whole passage rather than spotting a single word.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-A-FIRST-PERSON-NARRATOR-ALWAYS-TELLS-THE-COMPLETE-UNBIASED-TRUTH-ABOUT-EVERYTHING-THAT-HAPPENS
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-A — includes the self-flattering-account/contradicting-detail discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 6 (analogy overextension), from real-world trust calibration for direct, first-person testimony being implicitly applied to fictional first-person narration, where the genre convention of potential unreliability doesn't automatically signal itself the way an obviously biased third-party account would.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **provide a contradicting textual detail the learner discovers themselves**, since the fix requires developing an active habit of cross-checking a narrator's claims against other textual evidence, not merely being told narrators can be unreliable.
- **Verification of death**: given a first-person passage with a subtle self-serving framing or inconsistency, the learner identifies the specific textual clue revealing the narrator's potential bias or incompleteness.

### MC-B-THIRD-PERSON-OMNISCIENT-AND-THIRD-PERSON-LIMITED-ARE-BASICALLY-THE-SAME-THING-JUST-USING-HE-SHE
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — includes the single-thought/multiple-thought discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 4 (notation-induced), from the shared surface notation (third-person pronouns) being mistaken for evidence of a shared underlying category, when the actual distinguishing feature (scope of access) is a separate, non-pronoun-marked dimension.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct tracking of whose thoughts are revealed across a passage**, since the fix requires replacing a pronoun-based classification habit with a scope-based one through repeated practice, not a single explanatory statement.
- **Verification of death**: given two third-person passages, the learner tracks whose inner thoughts each reveals and classifies each as limited or omniscient based on that scope.

## Analogies

**Best — the Single-Eyewitness and Security-Camera-Scope Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a single eyewitness's useful but inherently limited and potentially biased account directly seeds the first-person-reliability fix; a single fixed security camera showing one room versus a full system with access to every room directly seeds the limited-versus-omniscient scope fix.
*Breaking point*: an eyewitness's bias often stems from self-interest or limited perception, while a fictional first-person narrator's "bias" is an authorial craft choice serving the story's purposes — useful for the limited-and-possibly-unreliable intuition, less precise for implying the narrator is a real, self-motivated witness rather than a constructed literary device.
*Breaking point (camera)*: a security camera's "access" is a purely mechanical, binary fact (recording or not), while a narrator's scope of access to inner thoughts is a genre convention the author deliberately controls — useful for the scope-not-pronoun intuition, less precise for implying scope is as simply verifiable as checking camera coverage.

**Alternative — a diary entry versus an all-knowing historian's account of the same events.** A diary entry (first-person) reveals only the diarist's own perspective and potential self-justifications, while a historian with access to multiple diaries, letters, and records (omniscient) can present a fuller, multi-perspective picture.
*Breaking point*: a real historian's omniscience is built from genuinely gathered external evidence, while a fictional omniscient narrator's knowledge is simply an authorial convention with no equivalent evidentiary gathering process.

**Visual anchor — the Single-Eyewitness and Security-Camera-Scope Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"If a character is telling the story in their own words, you can trust what they say."** Directly installs MC-A as a stated rule.
- **"As long as it's not 'I,' it's all the same third-person style."** Directly installs MC-B as a stated equivalence.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Single-Eyewitness and Security-Camera-Scope Anchor (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Is a single eyewitness's account automatically the full, unbiased truth?" Then connect to first-person narration.
2. **The self-flattering-account/contradicting-detail collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-A. *Predict first*: "Is the narrator's account of events necessarily the complete, objective truth?"
3. **The single-thought/multiple-thought collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-B. *Predict first*: "Does the narrator's access to characters' inner thoughts work the same way in both passages?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both analytical facts are directly checkable by cross-referencing textual details and tracking scope of access across a passage.

1. **Need**: "If a character tells you their own side of an argument, is that automatically the whole, fair story?"
2. **Playground**: the learner reads several passages and classifies point of view freely before being told the official four-category system.
3. **Invention**: "Can you find a detail elsewhere in the text that doesn't quite match what the narrator claims happened?"
4. **Collision**: present two third-person passages, one revealing one character's thoughts and one revealing several; ask whether they're "the same" point of view.
5. **Formalization**: name the two analytical facts — first-person narrators may be limited or biased; third-person scope, not pronoun, determines limited versus omniscient — from the learner's own detective work.
6. **Compression**: "Question the narrator's account; track scope, not pronoun."

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's cited textual evidence for a point-of-view classification or a bias detection**, since a correct-sounding classification with no cited pronoun/scope evidence, or a bias claim with no cited contradicting detail, cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A specific contradicting detail cited when questioning a first-person account** ("the narrator says X, but earlier it says the friend looked upset, which doesn't quite match") — the direct behavioral signature of the first-person fix taking hold.
- **A stated scope-tracking check** ("whose thoughts does this passage actually reveal — just one character's, or more than one?") applied to a new third-person passage — direct evidence the scope-not-pronoun fix is internalized.
- **A third-person passage classified purely by the absence of "I,"** with no tracking of whose thoughts are revealed — the direct behavioral signature of MC-B still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Question the narrator's account, and track WHOSE thoughts are revealed, not just which pronoun is used."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But the narrator is telling us their own story, so it should be true"** — take this as a reasonable extension of real-world trust in direct testimony; respond by naming the fictional convention: "A narrator can only tell you their own limited view — let's check if anything else in the text complicates their account."
- **"They're both third person, so aren't they the same style?"** — a reasonable-sounding surface-level classification; respond by redirecting to scope: "Both avoid 'I,' that's true — but let's track whose private thoughts we actually get to see in each one."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full novel-passage analysis down to **classifying a single clearly-limited and a single clearly-omniscient passage by tracked scope, with no bias-detection or knowledge-limits task demanded.** Isolates the most basic scope distinction before returning to fuller analysis.
- **Never shrink to "just say if it uses 'I' or not."** This directly licenses the pronoun-based classification pattern, undermining the scope-based discipline this concept specifically builds.
