# Conditional Sentences — `eng.grammar.conditionals`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.conditionals` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Conditional Sentences" —
  the four-way "if" system (zero, first, second, third conditional),
  each expressing a genuinely different degree of reality/likelihood
  through a specific tense pairing.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.modals` AND `eng.grammar.past-tenses`
  — load-bearing on two independent fronts: modals because "will,"
  "would," "might," and "could" are the exact result-clause forms the
  four conditional types reuse, unchanged, from the modal system
  already secured; past tenses because the second and third
  conditionals specifically repurpose simple past and past perfect
  FORMS for non-past MEANING, a distinction a learner cannot judge
  without already having those forms fluently secure.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none listed. **Cross-links** (from KG):
  `eng.linguistics.semantics-intro` — the second/third conditional's
  past-tense-form-signals-unreality pattern is a concrete, load-bearing
  example of the form/meaning divergence that concept formally studies;
  this entry does not restate that concept's own content, only notes
  the connection.
- **Difficulty**: advanced · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 3

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify and produce zero conditional sentences (if + present,
   present) for general truths/facts.
2. Identify and produce first conditional sentences (if + present,
   will + base) for real future possibility.
3. Identify and produce second conditional sentences (if + past,
   would + base) for hypothetical present/future situations, correctly
   recognizing the past-tense verb form signals unreality, not past
   time.
4. Identify and produce third conditional sentences (if + past perfect,
   would have + past participle) for counterfactual past situations —
   things that did NOT actually happen.
5. Select the appropriate conditional type for a given real-world
   scenario based on its actual degree of reality/likelihood, and
   detect and correct tense-pairing mismatches in mixed conditional
   sentences.

## Core Understanding

English's conditional system is not one interchangeable "if" pattern —
it is four structurally distinct types, each signaling a genuinely
different degree of reality, and each requiring its own specific tense
pairing. ZERO conditional (if + present, present) expresses a general
truth or fact, always true ("If you heat water, it boils"). FIRST
conditional (if + present, will + base) expresses a real, still-possible
future situation ("If it rains, I will stay home"). SECOND conditional
(if + past, would + base) expresses a HYPOTHETICAL present or future
situation — critically, the PAST-TENSE verb form here does NOT mean past
TIME; it is a grammatical convention English uses to signal distance
from reality/unreality ("If I had more money, I would travel" means: I
don't currently have more money, but imagining that I did...). THIRD
conditional (if + past perfect, would have + past participle) expresses
a COUNTERFACTUAL PAST situation — something that definitely did NOT
happen ("If I had studied, I would have passed" means the exam is
already over, and the person did not study and did not pass). The core
mastery skill is matching the correct tense pairing to the intended
degree of reality, not producing "an if-sentence" in general.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "all 'if' sentences use the same structure, and
  past-tense verb forms in an 'if' sentence mean something happened in
  the past"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept
  exists to correct — a natural default because "if" is a single word
  most learners meet first in its simplest (often first-conditional)
  form, and because past-tense verb forms genuinely DO mean past time
  in every other context the learner has secured so far.
- **Intermediate model — "I distinguish zero (general truth) from first
  (real future possibility) conditionals"**: installed by TA-1, using
  the Component 3 reality-distance ladder's bottom two rungs, building
  initial confidence with the most literal, least counter-intuitive
  tense usage before the harder distinction is introduced.
- **Advanced model — "I recognize that second-conditional past-tense
  form signals hypothetical unreality, not actual past time, and I
  identify third-conditional counterfactual-past meaning"**: installed
  by TA-2 and TA-3, resolving
  MC-SECOND-CONDITIONAL-PAST-TENSE-MEANS-PAST-TIME — the concept's most
  counter-intuitive, load-bearing insight. Upgrade trigger: correctly
  explaining, without prompting, that "if I had a car" doesn't mean the
  speaker used to have one.
- **Expert model — "I select the correct conditional type for any given
  scenario, and I detect and correct tense-pairing mismatches"**:
  installed by TA-4 and TA-5, the concept's genuine mastery bar,
  resolving MC-ALL-CONDITIONALS-ARE-THE-SAME-STRUCTURE by requiring the
  learner to actively judge which of the four reality-degrees applies
  before selecting a structure.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 has a different or simpler conditional system —
  a single unified conditional mood marker, or no second/third
  conditional distinction at all — expect this to be one of the most
  advanced, challenging grammar areas in English, frequently among the
  last structures L2 learners fully master; treat partial mastery after
  one session as normal, not a sign of insufficient teaching.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and the Blueprint
itself explicitly frames this concept as genuinely one of English's most
advanced grammar topics. The past-tense-means-past-time failure exists
because past-tense verb forms genuinely DO mean past time in every other
grammatical context the learner has secured up to this point (simple
past, past continuous, past perfect all reliably signal actual past
events) — extending that reliable pattern to the second conditional is
a completely reasonable inference, with nothing marking the exception
until a learner is directly asked whether "if I had a car" implies the
speaker used to own one (it doesn't). The all-conditionals-are-the-same
failure exists because all four types genuinely DO share the surface
word "if," making them feel like one interchangeable pattern — nothing
marks the four-way reality-distance distinction until a learner directly
contrasts a still-possible future scenario against an already-over,
definitely-didn't-happen past scenario and notices the tense pairing
must shift to reflect that difference.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.conditionals.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions
this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-SECOND-CONDITIONAL-PAST-TENSE-MEANS-PAST-TIME (Blueprint Component
1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — past-tense
  verb forms reliably mean past time everywhere else in the tense
  system the learner has secured, so extending that reliable rule to
  the second conditional is a completely reasonable inference; the
  misconception survives because the exception (past form used for
  present unreality) is a distinctive, genuinely counter-intuitive
  English grammatical convention with no equivalent signal anywhere
  else in the learner's prior tense instruction.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when asked whether
  "if I had a car" implies they used to own a car, spontaneously and
  correctly says "no, it means I don't have one right now" before being
  told is showing the past-for-unreality convention has been genuinely
  internalized.

**MC-ALL-CONDITIONALS-ARE-THE-SAME-STRUCTURE (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — all four types
  genuinely share the surface word "if," making one unified pattern a
  reasonable first assumption; the misconception survives because the
  first-taught, most common conditional (typically the first) genuinely
  works correctly for many everyday real-possibility scenarios, giving
  no signal that a fundamentally different structure is needed for
  hypothetical or already-resolved-past scenarios until directly
  contrasted.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when given a
  clearly already-over, definitely-didn't-happen past scenario,
  spontaneously reaches for third-conditional past-perfect-plus-would-
  have structure rather than defaulting to the first-conditional pattern
  is showing the reality-distance discrimination has generalized beyond
  the specific TA-4 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the reality-distance ladder itself is the analogy,
  not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): placing each
  conditional example on the correct rung of a four-rung ladder ("Always
  True" → "Really Might Happen" → "Just Imagining" → "Already Over,
  Didn't Happen") directly embodies both the increasing distance from
  concrete reality AND the parallel "backward" shift in verb tense
  (present → past → past perfect) as a single, unified visual-conceptual
  structure. Breaking point: the ladder demonstrates the four-way
  reality-distance ordering well but does not on its own train the
  harder skill of detecting and repairing a mismatched tense pairing in
  a flawed sentence (TA-5), which needs its own separate error-
  correction practice once the four rungs are individually secure.
- **Alternative — a weather forecast confidence scale**: "it will rain"
  (confident, real prediction) vs. "it might rain" (tentative
  possibility) vs. "if only it had rained" (regret about something that
  didn't happen) mirrors the conditional system's confidence/reality
  gradient using a familiar everyday register. Breaking point: useful
  for building intuition about degrees of certainty, but doesn't map
  cleanly onto the required TENSE PAIRINGS themselves, which the ladder
  anchor addresses directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe the second and third conditionals
  as "the past tense" or "past-tense conditionals" in casual
  shorthand**: this framing directly reinforces
  MC-SECOND-CONDITIONAL-PAST-TENSE-MEANS-PAST-TIME by labeling the
  grammatical FORM as if it determined the MEANING; always name what the
  form is signaling (hypothetical present/future for second, counter-
  factual past for third) alongside or instead of the tense-form label.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the reality-distance ladder)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any genuine general truth supplies real TA-1
  zero-conditional material (heating/freezing, plant care); any real,
  still-open future possibility supplies real TA-1 first-conditional
  material; any genuine "what if" daydream supplies real TA-2 second-
  conditional material (winning the lottery, having more time).
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-5's error-correction task
  (identifying which conditional type was intended and repairing a
  mismatched tense pairing) is inherently hands-on diagnostic practice,
  not mere recognition of a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the used-to-have test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether
  "had" in "if I had more money" means the speaker HAD money in the
  past and doesn't anymore, or describes an imagined situation right
  now, making the past-for-unreality convention directly audible and
  testable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  conditional-type identification or production attempt (TA-1 through
  TA-5), ask the learner to state their own attempt before the tutor
  confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions, given the
Blueprint's own explicit framing of this as an advanced topic warranting
careful pacing.** The past-for-unreality convention and the four-way
reality-distance system are both genuine, directly testable phenomena
well-suited to guided discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured
TA-1's zero/first conditionals, is asked what "if I had a car" means,
discovering their instinct — "you used to have one" — feels wrong once
tested against context); **playground/invention** (TA-1's real/possible
practice builds initial confidence with the most literal tense usage
before the counter-intuitive exception is introduced); **collision**
(the Blueprint's own "did I HAVE money in the past" probe breaks the
past-means-past-time assumption; the "If it rains" vs. "If I had
studied" contrast breaks the all-conditionals-are-the-same assumption);
**formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of both the past-for-
unreality convention and the four-way reality-distance system);
**compression** (TA-5's fluent error-correction, applying the reality-
distance judgment directly to flawed sentences without each instance
separately re-derived from first principles). The specific terms "zero,"
"first," "second," and "third conditional," and "counterfactual," are
convention-bound grammatical terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying
distinctions through the reality-distance ladder and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (zero and first conditionals) is placed first as the concept's
most literal, least counter-intuitive entry point, per the Blueprint's
own reality-distance ladder — establishing the two conditional types
whose tense forms genuinely DO align with their surface meaning before
any exception is introduced. TA-2 (second conditional) follows,
directly targeting
MC-SECOND-CONDITIONAL-PAST-TENSE-MEANS-PAST-TIME — per the Blueprint's
own session architecture, this conflict-evidence probe is placed right
after TA-2, once the learner has secure real/possible conditional
practice to contrast the hypothetical exception against. TA-3 (third
conditional) extends the past-for-unreality convention one step further
into counterfactual past, building on the now-secure second-conditional
insight. TA-4 (selecting the correct conditional type for a given
scenario) is placed next, directly targeting
MC-ALL-CONDITIONALS-ARE-THE-SAME-STRUCTURE, addressing a SECOND,
independent dimension (active selection among four types, not
production of one at a time) once all four types are individually
secure. TA-5 (mixed conditionals and error correction) is placed last as
the concept's integration capstone, requiring the learner to detect and
repair tense-pairing mismatches — the most diagnostic, error-focused
application of the full four-way system. This entry does not restate the
Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see
Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously explains a
  second-conditional sentence's meaning in terms of present unreality
  ("I don't have it now") rather than past time ("I used to have it"),
  and whether the learner spontaneously selects the correct conditional
  type for a fresh scenario based on its actual reality-degree — these
  are the primary diagnostic signals for whether TA-2 and TA-4's
  disciplines have genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice
  items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before selecting between
  second and third conditional for an ambiguous-sounding scenario,
  followed by a correct, reality-degree-consistent choice, suggests the
  learner is consciously applying the ladder judgment rather than
  guessing — a good sign. Instant, confident use of the first-
  conditional pattern for a scenario that is clearly hypothetical or
  already-resolved-past (without self-correction) is the concerning
  pattern for MC-ALL-CONDITIONALS-ARE-THE-SAME-STRUCTURE.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Protocol Routing — for S9 learners whose L1 has a
  simpler or unified conditional system, reassure that this is
  genuinely one of the most advanced grammar topics in English,
  frequently among the last structures even long-term L2 learners fully
  master, and that partial mastery after one session is expected, not a
  gap.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does that mean it happened, or is this
  imaginary?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for
  MC-SECOND-CONDITIONAL-PAST-TENSE-MEANS-PAST-TIME, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh second- or third-conditional sentence is introduced,
  making the reality-check a routinely-invited response rather than an
  afterthought.

- **"I don't know"** on a conditional-type identification or production

  simplest, most literal zero- or first-conditional example available,
  with the tutor modeling the ladder-placement process before the
  learner attempts a fresh second- or third-conditional sentence
  independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for
  MC-SECOND-CONDITIONAL-PAST-TENSE-MEANS-PAST-TIME, misreading a
  second-conditional sentence as describing genuine past time is more
  likely than "I don't know" — route directly to the Blueprint's own

  distance discipline has generalized — this should be named and
  praised explicitly, per the Blueprint's own regulation-tail guidance
  (praising matching tense pairing to the correct reality-degree over
  defaulting to one pattern).

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedural/framework — a four-way reality-graded
tense-pairing system applied to genuinely novel scenarios; mastery is
demonstrated by correctly selecting and producing the appropriate
conditional type for fresh, previously-unseen scenarios and by
correctly repairing mismatched tense pairings (MP-1 through MP-4, and
