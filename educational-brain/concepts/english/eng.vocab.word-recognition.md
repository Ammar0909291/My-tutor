# Word Recognition — `eng.vocab.word-recognition`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.word-recognition` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Word Recognition" — the
  domain's zero-prerequisite entry node, teaching that recognizing a
  printed word means connecting it to a meaning already known from
  spoken vocabulary, a genuinely separate step from merely decoding
  (sounding it out) correctly.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.phonics.decoding-fluency` — load-bearing
  because this concept's entire distinction (decoding vs. recognition)
  is meaningless without a learner who can already decode accurately
  and at a reasonable pace; a learner still struggling with the
  mechanics of decoding itself has no reliable pronunciation output to
  check against their spoken vocabulary in the first place.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.vocab.context-clues`,
  `eng.vocab.synonyms-antonyms`, `eng.vocab.word-families`,
  `eng.grammar.word-classes-overview`. This concept is the entry point
  for the entire vocab domain — every downstream vocabulary skill
  (inferring meaning from context, comparing word meanings, recognizing
  morphological families) presupposes that the learner already treats a
  printed word as a unit connected to meaning, not merely a decodable
  sound sequence.
- **Cross-links** (from KG): `eng.phonics.sight-words`. Sight words are
  the practical training ground where instant, effortless recognition
  (TA-2's skill) is first built — this concept formalizes and names the
  distinction that sight-word practice has already been building
  informally.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: remember · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.80 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Explain that decoding (sounding out a printed word) and recognition
   (connecting that word to a meaning already known from spoken
   vocabulary) are two distinct steps, not the same accomplishment.
2. Instantly recognize common, high-frequency printed words without
   conscious decoding effort.
3. Decode an unfamiliar-looking printed word BEFORE judging it as
   unknown, correctly discovering that many such words are already
   known from speech once properly sounded out.
4. Distinguish a closed recognition gap (a word decoded and then
   recognized as already known) from a genuine vocabulary gap (a word
   decoded correctly but whose meaning remains unknown even after
   decoding).
5. Apply this decode-then-check distinction while reading connected
   text, flagging genuine vocabulary gaps for future vocabulary-building
   work.

## Core Understanding

Word recognition is the act of connecting a printed word's decoded
pronunciation to a meaning the learner already possesses in their
spoken vocabulary — it is a distinct cognitive step from decoding
itself, which only produces a pronunciation. A learner can decode a
word perfectly (sound it out with complete phonetic accuracy) and still
not recognize it, if that word is not yet part of their spoken
vocabulary at all; conversely, a learner can fail to recognize a word
from its printed appearance alone, yet instantly recognize it the moment
it is correctly decoded, because the word was already known from speech
all along — the printed FORM, not the word itself, was unfamiliar. This
second fact reframes an unfamiliar-looking printed word as a puzzle to
be decoded first, not a verdict to be judged from appearance alone: many
printed words that look tricky are simply words the learner has not yet
seen written down often, not words genuinely absent from their
vocabulary. Genuine word recognition, over repeated successful decode-
then-recognize experiences, becomes automatic — the learner stops
needing to consciously decode common words at all, connecting printed
form directly to meaning.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "if I can sound a word out correctly, I've done
  the job — the word is now 'read'"**: the arriving, pre-instructional
  model this concept exists to correct. This model is a natural
  extension of the intense, effortful focus decoding fluency itself
  required — once decoding becomes the hard-won achievement, it is easy
  to treat successful decoding as the full and complete task.
- **Intermediate model — "decoding gets me to a pronunciation;
  recognition is a separate check of whether I already know that
  pronunciation's meaning from speech"**: installed by TA-1's explicit
  decode-then-check routine, using the Component 3 anchor's "aha, I DO
  know this!" moment to make the two-step process concrete and felt.
  Upgrade trigger: spontaneously stating both steps ("I sounded it out,
  and yes, I know that word — it means...") without being prompted to
  check separately.
- **Advanced model — "an unfamiliar-looking printed word isn't
  necessarily an unknown word — I should decode it before judging"**:
  installed by TA-3's unfamiliar-looking-but-known words, resolving
  MC-UNFAMILIAR-PRINTED-WORD-MEANS-UNKNOWN-WORD through the felt
  surprise of recognizing a word immediately once properly decoded.
  Upgrade trigger: attempting to decode a visually unfamiliar word
  before declaring it unknown, without being reminded to do so.
- **Expert model — "I can distinguish a closed recognition gap (now
  known) from a genuine vocabulary gap (still unknown even after
  correct decoding), and I use this distinction productively during
  real reading"**: installed by TA-4's explicit sorting task and TA-5's
  connected-text application, resolving MC-DECODING-EQUALS-RECOGNIZING-
  MEANING at its most demanding level — treating a residual "I can say
  it but don't know it" case not as a failure but as useful diagnostic
  information about where future vocabulary work should focus.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 (English-learner) students whose spoken vocabulary is still
  developing, a higher proportion of TA-4 sorting outcomes will
  genuinely be true vocabulary gaps rather than closed recognition gaps —
  this should never be reframed as a decoding or recognition-skill
  deficiency; the tutor should not push toward "closing" gaps that
  reflect genuine, expected vocabulary growth rather than a recognition-
  skill failure.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both stemming from
reasonable extrapolations of the decoding-fluency skill the learner has
just secured. The decoding-equals-recognition failure exists because
decoding itself was hard-won and effortful — a learner who has just
achieved fluent decoding has strong, recent evidence that "getting the
pronunciation right" is the difficult, meaningful accomplishment, with
nothing in the decoding process itself signaling that a further,
separate meaning-connection step exists and can independently succeed
or fail. The unfamiliar-appearance-means-unknown failure exists because
judging a word's familiarity from its VISUAL appearance is a fast,
low-effort heuristic that works correctly most of the time for words
already seen often in print — a learner reasonably relies on this
heuristic without realizing that an unusual spelling or simply a
lower print-frequency word can make an already-known spoken word look
unfamiliar in its printed form, with the visual judgment alone giving no
signal that decoding first would resolve the apparent unfamiliarity.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.vocab.word-recognition.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-DECODING-EQUALS-RECOGNIZING-MEANING (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — decoding
  accuracy genuinely correlates with word knowledge for the large
  majority of words a beginning reader meets (most decoded words ARE
  already known from speech), so the learner extends "decoding
  succeeded" into "the word is now known" as an unconditional
  equivalence, with nothing in ordinary decoding practice marking that
  the two can come apart for a genuinely unfamiliar word.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, immediately after
  correctly decoding a genuinely unfamiliar word, says something like
  "I can say it, but I don't actually know what it means" without being
  prompted to check is showing the distinction has been genuinely
  internalized — a stronger signal than passively agreeing when asked.

**MC-UNFAMILIAR-PRINTED-WORD-MEANS-UNKNOWN-WORD (Blueprint Component
1) — Type 2, perceptual intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a genuine perceptual default rather than
  a wrongly-taught rule — visual unfamiliarity is a fast, largely
  reliable proxy for actual unfamiliarity across the many words a
  learner has genuinely never encountered, so judging by appearance
  alone is a reasonable low-effort strategy that simply has no
  built-in signal distinguishing "this word's FORM is unfamiliar" from
  "this WORD itself is unfamiliar" until decoding is actually attempted.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, after decoding a
  visually tricky word, reacts with visible surprise ("oh, THAT'S what
  that word was!") is showing the conflict-evidence probe working
  exactly as intended — this genuine surprise response is a stronger
  signal of real learning than a flat, expected confirmation.
- *English-learner note*: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, S9
  (English-learner) students should expect a genuinely higher rate of
  true vocabulary gaps (not recognition gaps) when this misconception is
  tested — this is a normal reflection of a still-developing spoken
  vocabulary, not evidence the misconception itself is unresolved.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the decode-then-recognize check itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): decoding a
  printed word that looks unfamiliar but is actually already known from
  speech, and capturing the "aha, I DO know this!" moment, directly
  demonstrates that decoding UNLOCKS recognition of an already-possessed
  word rather than creating new knowledge from nothing. Breaking point:
  this anchor works cleanly for words already in the learner's spoken
  vocabulary; it should not be over-extended to imply every decoded word
  will produce an "aha" moment — some genuinely will remain unknown
  (true vocabulary gaps), and the anchor is deliberately paired with
  TA-4's distinct sorting task to teach that contrast explicitly.
- **Alternative — a locked door and a key**: the printed word's letters
  are a lock; decoding is turning the key; recognition is what happens
  when the door opens onto a room (a meaning) you've already furnished
  — if the room behind the door is empty (a genuinely new word), the
  key still turned correctly, but there's nothing there yet. Breaking
  point: useful for framing decoding as necessary-but-not-sufficient,
  but should not be extended to suggest recognition is instantaneous or
  effortless for every already-known word — TA-2's automaticity is
  itself a separate, practice-built skill.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe a decoded-but-unrecognized word as a
  "failed" reading attempt**: framing a genuine vocabulary gap as a
  reading failure risks discouraging the very act (attempting to
  decode unfamiliar-looking words) this concept is built to encourage,
  and directly contradicts the Blueprint's own explicit framing of
  vocabulary gaps as valuable diagnostic data, not failures.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/auditory (decode-then-recognize check)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated. Ideally
  chosen individually per learner (a word already in their spoken
  vocabulary but not yet seen much in print) for maximum "aha" impact.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any printed material (a book, a sign, a food
  label) supplies real decode-then-check opportunities; no special
  materials are required beyond something to read.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-1's decode-then-check routine and
  TA-4's true-gap-vs-recognition-gap sorting task are both hands-on,
  requiring the learner to actively classify each word, not merely
  listen to an explanation.
- **Teacher-demo (instant recognition contrast)**: the tutor
  demonstrates the visible difference between effortful decoding (sound-
  by-sound) and instant recognition (immediate, unhesitating) on two
  different words, making TA-2's automaticity target concretely visible.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming whether
  a decoded word is recognized (TA-1) or sorting a word as a closed gap
  vs. a true gap (TA-4), ask the learner to state their own judgment
  before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
decoding/recognition separation and the unfamiliar-appearance trap are
both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having just achieved decoding fluency, is asked
whether decoding alone always means "I know this word"); **playground/
invention** (TA-1's decode-then-check routine across several words lets
the learner notice recognition sometimes happens instantly and
sometimes doesn't); **collision** (the Component 3 anchor's "aha, I DO
know this!" moment breaks the unfamiliar-appearance assumption directly;
TA-4's sorting task breaks the decoding-equals-recognition
assumption by forcing an explicit true-gap vs. closed-gap judgment);
**formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of the decoding/
recognition distinction); **compression** (TA-2's instant, effortless
recognition of high-frequency words, requiring no conscious decode-then-
check step at all). There is no specific convention-bound terminology
here requiring direct instruction beyond the labels "decoding" and
"recognition" themselves, which are best introduced once the learner has
already felt the distinction through the anchor and TA-1.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (decode-then-check-for-meaning routine) is placed first because it
establishes the foundational two-step process (decode, then separately
check for recognition) that every subsequent TA depends on — attempting
any later TA without this explicit routine already practiced would risk
the learner collapsing the two steps back into one. TA-2 (instant
recognition of high-frequency words) follows immediately, showing the
learner what the SAME distinction looks like once it becomes automatic
— demonstrating the goal state (effortless recognition) right after the
effortful, conscious version (TA-1) makes the automaticity target
concrete rather than abstract. TA-3 (unfamiliar-looking-but-known
words) is placed next, directly targeting MC-UNFAMILIAR-PRINTED-WORD-
MEANS-UNKNOWN-WORD once the learner already has the decode-then-check
routine (TA-1) as an available tool to apply to visually tricky words.
TA-4 (true vocabulary gaps vs. recognition gaps) is placed after TA-3
specifically because it requires the learner to have already
experienced BOTH outcomes — words that resolve into recognition (TA-1,
TA-3) and, by implication, words that might not — before productively
sorting a mixed set into the two categories. TA-5 (word recognition in
connected text) is placed last as the concept's integration and
real-world-application capstone, applying the full decode-then-check
distinction during genuine, continuous reading rather than isolated
word-by-word practice. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own
turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References
below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner explicitly voices BOTH
  steps unprompted ("I sounded it out, and yes, I know that word") when
  reading an unfamiliar-looking word, rather than only the decoded
  pronunciation — this is the primary diagnostic signal for whether the
  two-step process has been genuinely internalized versus performed
  only when directly asked.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause after decoding an
  unfamiliar-looking word, followed by a correct recognition statement,
  is a good sign — it suggests the learner is consciously checking for
  recognition (TA-3's target skill) rather than assuming familiarity
  status from appearance alone. Instant, confident recognition on a
  high-frequency word with no decoding effort at all is the expected,
  correct pattern for TA-2, not a concerning shortcut.
- **Honesty stakes**: real and important for MP-4 specifically — a
  learner who falsely claims to "know" a genuinely unfamiliar word's
  meaning (rather than honestly reporting a true vocabulary gap) should
  be gently redirected toward honest self-assessment, since the whole
  value of this concept's distinction depends on accurately
  distinguishing decoding success from genuine meaning-knowledge,
  per the Blueprint's own regulation-tail guidance (praising honest
  distinction between "I can say it" and "I know it").
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "do you know this word now that you've
  heard it?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  immediately after every decoding attempt on an unfamiliar-looking
  word, making the recognition check an explicit, spoken step rather
  than an implicit assumption.

## Assessment Signals

- **Diagnostic (golden probe)**: the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence
  probe (Component 1) — decoding a genuinely unfamiliar-looking word
  and immediately asking whether the learner now recognizes it — cleanly
  separates a learner still conflating decoding with recognition from
  one who correctly checks for the meaning connection separately.
- **Guided practice → independent practice**: the Blueprint's WE-1 →
  WE-2 → WE-3 → MP-1 through MP-5 fading ladder is reused directly
  (Components 5–6) — not re-authored here.
- **Mastery gate set** (`../../assessment/05-mastery-verification.md
  §3`): decode-then-check (MP-1, a fresh word decoded and correctly
  recognized with its meaning stated); instant recognition (MP-2, a
  fresh high-frequency word recognized without effortful decoding);
  unfamiliar-looking-but-known (MP-3, a strong diagnostic item since it
  directly tests whether visual unfamiliarity is correctly overridden
  by decode-first practice); true vocabulary gap honesty (MP-4, the
  concept's most demanding item, requiring the learner to correctly
  report NOT knowing a word's meaning despite decoding it correctly);
  explanation (MP-5, requiring articulation of the decoding/recognition
  distinction in the learner's own words).
- **Interpretation of response pattern**: fast-and-correct decode-then-
  recognize responses on genuinely novel words, including honest
  "I don't know what that means" responses on true vocabulary gaps
  (MP-4), confirm the distinction is internalized. A learner who claims
  to "know" every word they successfully decode, with no true
  vocabulary gaps ever reported, is the dangerous quadrant for
  MC-DECODING-EQUALS-RECOGNIZING-MEANING, warranting a return to TA-4's
  explicit sorting task with a genuinely unfamiliar word chosen
  specifically to test honest gap-reporting. A learner who refuses to
  attempt decoding a visually unfamiliar word at all, declaring it
  "unknown" from appearance alone, is showing MC-UNFAMILIAR-PRINTED-
  WORD-MEANS-UNKNOWN-WORD still active, warranting a return to TA-3.
- **Transfer items**: near (a fresh word decoded and correctly checked
  for recognition); far (flagging genuine vocabulary gaps while reading
  a genuinely novel connected passage, per TA-5); real-world (applying
  the decode-then-check habit to any unfamiliar-looking printed word
  encountered outside structured teaching — signs, labels, books).

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a decode-then-check task shrinks to the
  Component 3 anchor using a word individually chosen to be maximally
  familiar in speech but unfamiliar in print, maximizing the chance of a
  genuine "aha, I DO know this!" recovery moment.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: declaring a visually
  unfamiliar word "unknown" without attempting to decode it first is
  more likely than a generic "I don't know" — route directly to
  MC-UNFAMILIAR-PRINTED-WORD-MEANS-UNKNOWN-WORD's recovery (decode it
  together, then check for recognition) rather than treating it as a
  vocabulary gap before decoding has even been attempted.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who shows
  visible surprise (a widened expression, an audible "oh!") upon
  recognizing a word immediately after decoding it is showing the
  conflict-evidence probe working exactly as intended — this should be
  named and praised explicitly, since it is a stronger signal of
  genuine learning than a flat, expected confirmation.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedure — a metacognitive checking habit (decode,
then explicitly verify recognition) applied across novel words, rather
than a fixed fact set, following a skill-fluency pattern: correctness
first (reliably performing the explicit two-step check, even slowly),
then automaticity for high-frequency words specifically (TA-2), while
the explicit check itself remains a standing habit for genuinely
unfamiliar-looking words rather than something to eventually skip
entirely. Interleaving partners: mix already-familiar high-frequency
words (testing TA-2's automaticity) with unfamiliar-looking-but-known
words and genuine vocabulary-gap words (testing TA-3/TA-4's sorting
judgment) within ongoing review, so the learner continues exercising
both the automatic and the deliberate-checking modes of this skill.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: decoding a fresh word and correctly checking it for
  recognition, including honest reporting of a true vocabulary gap when
  one exists.
- **Far**: this concept's decode-then-check habit is the direct
  foundation for `eng.vocab.context-clues` (using surrounding text to
  resolve a genuine vocabulary gap once one is correctly identified),
  `eng.vocab.synonyms-antonyms` and `eng.vocab.word-families` (both
  presupposing that a printed word is already treated as a meaning-
  bearing unit), and `eng.grammar.word-classes-overview` (which likewise
  depends on words being recognized as meaningful units, not merely
  decodable strings).
- **Real-world**: every piece of printed text the learner encounters —
  books, signs, labels, screens — is an immediate, ungated opportunity
  to apply the decode-then-check habit and to flag genuine vocabulary
  gaps for future learning.
- **Expert-transfer**: fluent, silent reading comprehension at any
  advanced level depends on word recognition having become fully
  automatic for the vast majority of encountered words, freeing
  cognitive resources for meaning-construction at the sentence and
  passage level rather than word-by-word decoding.
