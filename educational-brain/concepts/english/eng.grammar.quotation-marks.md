# Quotation Marks — `eng.grammar.quotation-marks`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.quotation-marks` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Quotation Marks" —
  punctuation conventions for marking direct quotations, titles of
  short works, and dialogue.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.direct-and-indirect-speech` AND
  `eng.grammar.comma-usage` — load-bearing on two fronts: direct/
  indirect speech because the entire first rule this concept teaches
  (quote direct speech, never indirect speech) depends on a learner
  already being able to reliably tell the two apart; comma usage
  because the second rule (comma/period placement relative to the
  closing quotation mark) is a punctuation-placement skill that
  presupposes the learner already treats punctuation placement as a
  checkable, rule-governed decision rather than a matter of feel — the
  identical structural-not-pause-based discipline `eng.grammar.comma-
  usage` itself exists to install.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none — this is a terminal node in the current
  curriculum sequence, per both the KG and the Blueprint's own explicit
  framing ("a foundational mechanical skill for narrative and academic
  writing" with "no further downstream unlocks").
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 1

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Correctly place quotation marks around a direct quotation (a
   speaker's exact words) while NEVER placing them around indirect/
   reported speech (a paraphrase).
2. Correctly place commas and end punctuation relative to the closing
   quotation mark in dialogue, following the standard American
   convention (inside the closing mark).
3. Correctly use quotation marks to mark the title of a short work
   (poem, article, short story), distinct from the italics convention
   used for longer, standalone works.
4. Apply all three rules together, fluently and correctly, within a
   single mixed passage containing dialogue and a title reference.

## Core Understanding

Quotation marks make a specific, narrow promise to the reader: "what is
inside these marks is someone's exact, word-for-word statement" — never
a paraphrase, however close. This is why they attach exclusively to
direct speech and never to indirect/reported speech (which is, by
definition, a paraphrase with pronouns and tenses already shifted away
from the speaker's literal words). A second, entirely independent
convention governs WHERE commas and periods sit relative to the closing
quotation mark: in standard American English, they go INSIDE it, every
time, as a fixed, memorized, arbitrary-but-consistent typographic rule —
not something derivable from logic about what the punctuation "belongs
to." A third, unrelated use extends the same mark to a different job
entirely: flagging the title of a SHORT, standalone-but-not-full-length
work (a poem, a magazine article, a short story, a song), where longer
works (novels, plays, albums, full-length films) instead take italics.
All three rules are genuinely independent conventions that happen to
share one punctuation mark — mastering one does not automatically
transfer to the others, which is exactly why the Blueprint treats them
as three separate teaching acts (TA-1 through TA-3) before a final
synthesis task (TA-4).

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "quotation marks go around anything someone said,
  including reports of what they said"**: the arriving, pre-
  instructional model this concept exists to correct for MC-A — a
  natural default because both direct and indirect speech describe
  "what someone said," with no obvious surface signal distinguishing
  them without deliberate attention to whether the exact original
  wording (with its original pronouns and tense) survived.
- **Intermediate model — "quotation marks are reserved exclusively for
  a speaker's exact, verbatim words"**: installed by TA-1 using the
  Museum-Authenticity-Label anchor, resolving MC-A-QUOTATION-MARKS-
  SHOULD-BE-USED-AROUND-INDIRECT-REPORTED-SPEECH-TOO. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly leaving indirect speech unquoted even when it closely
  paraphrases a dramatic or emotionally charged original statement.
- **Advanced model — "commas and periods go inside the closing
  quotation mark, as a fixed convention, regardless of what feels
  logical"**: installed by TA-2 using the Drive-on-One-Side anchor,
  resolving MC-B-COMMAS-AND-END-PUNCTUATION-GO-OUTSIDE-THE-CLOSING-
  QUOTATION-MARK-IN-AMERICAN-ENGLISH-DIALOGUE.
- **Expert model — "I apply the direct-speech-only rule, the inside-
  placement convention, and the short-work-title rule together, fluently,
  in one mixed passage"**: installed by TA-3 (the title-marking
  extension) and TA-4 (full synthesis), the concept's genuine mastery
  bar — three independent conventions applied simultaneously without
  cross-contamination.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing
  (S9), MP-1 (direct/indirect discrimination) and MP-2 (comma/period
  placement) test the two misconceptions independently; MP-5 (full
  passage) specifically catches a learner who has mastered each rule in
  isolation but reverts under mixed load — do not certify mastery on
  MP-1 through MP-3 passing alone if MP-5 still shows integration
  failures (Component 8's own first Adaptive Flag).

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and they do not share
a root cause, which is exactly why the Blueprint treats them as
separate misconceptions with separate anchors rather than one combined
correction. The over-quoting-indirect-speech failure (MC-A) exists
because both direct and indirect speech report the same underlying
content ("what she said"), and a learner who has not yet internalized
the direct/indirect grammatical distinction taught in the prerequisite
concept has no principled reason to treat them differently for
quoting purposes — the surface similarity (both are about someone's
words) masks the structural difference (exact wording preserved vs.
paraphrased). The comma/period-outside-the-mark failure (MC-B) exists
because the convention is genuinely arbitrary from a logical standpoint
— there is no inherent reason punctuation that isn't "part of" the
quotation should nonetheless sit inside it — so a learner reasoning from
first principles (or one who has encountered British-convention texts,
where placement can differ) has no logical anchor to fall back on
except direct, deliberate memorization of the specific convention this
curriculum targets.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.quotation-marks.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-A-QUOTATION-MARKS-SHOULD-BE-USED-AROUND-INDIRECT-REPORTED-SPEECH-
TOO (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 3, language contamination**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: Type 3, specifically the everyday-word-
  meaning-carried-into-technical-context variant rather than L1
  transfer — in ordinary, non-grammatical usage, "quoting" someone
  loosely covers any report of what they said, direct or paraphrased
  ("she quoted him as saying he was tired," said conversationally about
  a clear paraphrase); the learner imports this loose, everyday sense
  of "quote" into the technical, narrow grammatical convention that
  quotation marks exclusively flag verbatim wording, producing the
  over-application to indirect speech.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, given a fresh
  indirect-speech sentence with dramatic or quotable-sounding content,
  spontaneously leaves it unquoted while correctly quoting only the
  genuine direct-speech sentence nearby is showing the exact-words-only
  promise has been internalized past the surface "this sounds like
  something someone said" pull.

**MC-B-COMMAS-AND-END-PUNCTUATION-GO-OUTSIDE-THE-CLOSING-QUOTATION-
MARK-IN-AMERICAN-ENGLISH-DIALOGUE (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 3,
language contamination**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: Type 3, specifically cross-convention
  contamination rather than a purely invented guess — British and some
  other English-variety conventions genuinely DO place certain
  punctuation outside the closing quotation mark under specific rules,
  so a learner who has read texts in that convention (or half-recalls
  a "logical" version of the rule) is importing a real, valid-elsewhere
  convention into a context requiring the different standard American
  one, rather than reasoning from a false premise about English in
  general.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a
  British-convention example, can explicitly name it as "a different
  but valid convention" while still correctly applying the standard
  American inside-placement rule to their own assigned writing context
  is showing genuine convention-awareness rather than either blind
  memorization or unresolved confusion between the two systems.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the Museum-Authenticity-Label and Drive-on-One-Side
  combined anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a museum's "authentic
  artifact" label makes a specific promise (this is the genuine
  original) that a modern replica cannot honestly carry, directly
  seeding MC-A's fix — quotation marks are that same specific promise
  of exact, verbatim words, which indirect speech by definition cannot
  carry. A fixed traffic convention (which side of the road to drive
  on) has no universal "correct by nature" answer, but is applied
  consistently within one system, directly seeding MC-B's fix — comma/
  period placement relative to quotation marks is a fixed, memorized
  convention specific to standard American English, not a logically
  derivable fact. Breaking point: both halves of the anchor are
  deliberately narrow, each targeting exactly one misconception; TA-3's
  title-marking extension needs its own, separate framing (the
  short-vs-long-work distinction), which the anchor does not cover.
- **Alternative for the title rule — a name tag vs. a formal
  certificate**: a short work's title gets quotation marks (a name tag
  — informal, attached, easily worn by something shorter/less
  standalone), while a long, complete, standalone work gets italics (a
  formal certificate — its own complete, independently-standing
  document). Breaking point: useful for building initial intuition
  about "short, part-of-a-collection" vs. "long, standalone," but the
  actual boundary cases (is a novella short or long? is an epic poem
  published alone still short-work-quoted?) require the Blueprint's own
  quick-reference categorization rather than the analogy alone.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT teach comma/period placement relative to
  quotation marks as "whatever feels logical based on what the
  punctuation belongs to"**: this framing directly reinforces MC-B by
  inviting the learner to reason their way to an answer on a convention
  that is, by design, not derivable from logic; instead, per the
  Blueprint's own framing, the inside-placement rule is presented from
  the start as a fixed, memorized convention specific to standard
  American English, exactly parallel to a driving-side rule.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the Museum-Authenticity-Label and Drive-on-One-Side
  anchor)**: the Blueprint's Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced
  not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any recent real conversation the learner had
  supplies genuine TA-1 material — have them write one sentence
  reporting it as direct speech (their conversation partner's exact
  words, quoted) and one as indirect speech (paraphrased, unquoted),
  using their own real memory rather than a supplied example.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's full-passage synthesis task
  is inherently hands-on, requiring the learner to apply all three
  independent rules within one continuous piece of writing rather than
  isolated single-sentence items.
- **Teacher-demo (the comma-placement contrast)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration side by side — "I am
  tired", she said. versus "I am tired," she said. — making the single,
  narrow visual difference (comma position relative to the closing
  mark) the explicit object of attention rather than a detail easily
  glossed over.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  quoting or punctuation-placement attempt (TA-1 through TA-4), ask the
  learner to state their own attempt before the tutor confirms —
  Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits MC-A; MC-B is better taught by
direct instruction.** The direct/indirect distinction (MC-A) is a
genuinely discoverable, logically-motivated insight well-suited to
guided discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured the
prerequisite direct/indirect distinction, is shown "She said, 'I am
tired.'" beside "She said that she was tired." and asked whether the
second sentence's words are literally, verbatim what she said out loud,
discovering that a paraphrase cannot honestly carry the "exact words"
promise a quotation mark makes); **playground/invention** (the learner
converts a few of their own direct-speech sentences into indirect
speech, observing the pronoun/tense shift that removes the "exact
words" claim each time); **collision** (the Blueprint's own "He said
'that he would be late.'" trigger sentence directly breaks the
over-quoting assumption); **formalization** (the museum-label framing —
"quotation marks are a promise of authenticity, and a paraphrase cannot
honestly make that promise"); **compression** (TA-1's six-sentence
direct/indirect sort, applied without re-deriving the promise-logic
each time). MC-B, by contrast, is a genuinely arbitrary, convention-
bound rule with no discoverable logical structure — per the Blueprint's
own framing ("no universal 'correct by nature' answer"), it is best
taught directly: state the standard American convention, model it, and
move immediately to practice (TA-2), reserving discovery-style
questioning for conventions that actually reward it.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (never quoting indirect speech) is placed first, directly
targeting MC-A, per the Blueprint's own session architecture — securing
the narrower, more logically-motivated rule (exact-words-only) before
introducing the purely conventional comma-placement rule, so the
learner is not asked to absorb two independently-arbitrary-feeling
rules simultaneously. TA-2 (comma and end-punctuation placement)
directly targets MC-B once TA-1's quoting judgment is reasonably
secure, since a learner who is still uncertain WHETHER to quote a given
sentence cannot productively practice WHERE the punctuation goes within
a quotation they haven't yet correctly identified as one. TA-3
(quoting titles of short works) introduces the second, genuinely
unrelated use of the same mark, deliberately placed after TA-1/TA-2 so
the learner does not conflate "exact-words" quoting with "title-
marking" quoting — they are independent conventions sharing one symbol.
TA-4 (full application) is placed last as the concept's integration
capstone, requiring the learner to apply all three rules simultaneously
within one continuous passage — directly addressing the Blueprint's own
first Adaptive Flag concern (isolated-rule mastery not transferring to
mixed application). This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own
turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References
below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously names WHY a
  given sentence should or shouldn't be quoted (citing the exact-words
  test, not just a guess), and whether the learner spontaneously checks
  comma/period placement against the fixed convention rather than
  guessing — these are the primary diagnostic signals for whether TA-1
  and TA-2's disciplines have genuinely generalized beyond the specific
  practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before quoting a fresh
  sentence that closely paraphrases dramatic content, followed by a
  correct decision to leave it unquoted, suggests the learner is
  consciously checking the exact-words test rather than reacting to
  surface "this sounds quotable" content — a good sign. Instant,
  confident quoting of any sentence describing speech (without checking
  direct vs. indirect) is the concerning pattern for MC-A.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: MC-B is explicitly flagged in the
  Blueprint as genuinely cross-convention (British and other English
  varieties can differ) rather than purely invented — when a learner
  has been exposed to non-American texts, acknowledge the alternative
  convention as real and valid (per Component 8's second Adaptive Flag)
  while confirming which specific convention (standard American) this
  concept and their assigned writing context requires; do not treat the
  learner's alternative-convention exposure as an error to be corrected
  away, only as a context-specific convention to be set aside for this
  task.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "are these the speaker's exact,
  word-for-word words, or a paraphrase?" is this concept's load-bearing
  sentence for MC-A, per `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-
  model.md §3` — asked whenever a fresh sentence reporting speech is
  introduced, making the exact-words check a routinely-invited response
  rather than an afterthought.

- **Mastery gate set** (`../../assessment/05-mastery-verification.md
  §3`): direct/indirect discrimination (MP-1, the strongest diagnostic
  item for residual MC-A); comma/period placement (MP-2, the strongest
  diagnostic item for residual MC-B); title-quoting identification

  title-quoting extension specifically needs review, distinct from

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a quoting-or-punctuating task shrinks to the
  Component 3 Museum-Authenticity-Label half of the anchor using the
  simplest, most familiar direct-speech example available (a single
  short sentence someone obviously said out loud), with the tutor
  modeling the exact-words check before the learner attempts a fresh
  indirect-speech, comma-placement, or title item independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for MC-A, quoting an
  indirect-speech sentence without hesitation is more likely than "I
  don't know" — route directly to the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence
  probe rather than treating it as a generic quoting gap. For MC-B,
  placing punctuation outside the closing mark without hesitation is
  more likely than confusion — route directly to TA-2's drive-on-one-
  side framing rather than re-explaining quoting from scratch.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously self-corrects a comma placement upon checking the
  convention ("tired", she said — wait, the comma goes inside the
  quotation mark") before being told is showing the fixed-convention
  discipline has generalized — this should be named and praised
  explicitly, consistent with the Blueprint's own protocol routing
  emphasis on redirecting both misconceptions to their respective
  anchor halves rather than correcting item-by-item.
