## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Sort a story summary's events into the causally-connected plot chain versus incidental, non-causal details.
2. Analyze how the same basic scenario in two contrasting settings shapes atmosphere and character possibility differently.
3. Identify a story's protagonist, antagonist, and conflict type with textual evidence.
4. Arrange a jumbled list of story events into the correct causal plot chain.
5. Independently identify all four narrative elements (plot chain, characters, conflict type, setting's function) in a novel short story.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **plot is not everything that happens in a story — it is specifically the causally-connected chain of events driving the central conflict toward resolution**; a story's incidental details (what a character had for breakfast, background weather on an unrelated day) are texture, not plot, even though they technically "happen" in the story. Second, **setting is not a neutral factual label of where and when a story occurs — it actively shapes atmosphere, constrains what characters can plausibly do, and often reinforces the story's themes symbolically**; the identical plot event (a character making a difficult decision alone at night) feels entirely different in a warm, familiar kitchen versus a storm-battered ruin, because setting is doing real narrative work, not providing passive scaffolding. Both corrections train the same underlying discipline as close reading — distinguishing what's functionally significant from what merely appears in the text — applied here specifically to the structure of narrative.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "the plot is just a list of everything that happens; the setting is just where and when the story takes place."**
The simulation: recount every detail indiscriminately when asked for "the plot," and describe setting as a bare factual label with no further function.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a story summary mixing plot-critical events with incidental details, asked to identify "the plot"; and the same plot scenario in two contrasting settings, asked whether setting is "just background" in both.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Plot is the causal chain toward the conflict's resolution, not every detail. Setting actively shapes mood and possibility, not just labels where/when."

**Intermediate — "I can now separate plot-critical events from incidental ones, but I still describe setting mostly factually without analyzing its effect."**
The simulation: correctly sorts plot-chain events but continues to under-analyze setting's active narrative function.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-4's direct application of the discrimination pairs, presenting the same scenario in two settings and requiring an explicit atmosphere/possibility analysis for each.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Don't just name the setting — ask what mood it creates and what it makes possible or believable."

**Advanced — "I identify the plot chain, characters, conflict type, and setting's active function together in a novel, unscaffolded story."**
The simulation: integrates all four narrative-element analyses into one coherent reading. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the five direct downstream cross-links, each zooming into one of this concept's four elements in greater depth.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the four foundational elements together. Each one gets its own deeper study next — plot structure, character development, setting and atmosphere, point of view, and drama."

**Expert — "at fluency, narrative-element identification happens as an integrated part of ordinary literary reading, without a separate deliberate four-part checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit plot-chain-sort and setting-function-analysis routine is a scaffold for a reading habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of literary comprehension.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **a plain retelling ("everything that happens") is a genuinely easier, more accessible task than causal-chain analysis**, and for simple stories the two often produce similar-looking summaries, so the shortcut goes uncorrected until a story includes enough incidental texture to expose the difference.

The second mechanism is that **setting is frequently introduced in early instruction primarily as a factual category** ("identify the time and place"), reinforcing a where/when-only model with no exposure to setting's atmospheric or thematic function until a concept like this one deliberately confronts it.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-PLOT-MEANS-EVERYTHING-THAT-HAPPENS-IN-THE-STORY
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry — includes the plot-critical/incidental discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of "recount what happens" as a genuinely useful basic comprehension task, extended into treating it as equivalent to identifying the plot, which requires the stricter causal-connection filter.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **add the causal-chain-to-conflict test as a further filter, not discard event-recall entirely** — a learner's ability to recall events is a prerequisite the plot-chain test then narrows, not a skill to be dismissed.
- **Verification of death**: given a story summary with mixed plot-critical and incidental events, the learner correctly identifies only the causally-connected chain.

### MC-SETTING-IS-JUST-WHERE-AND-WHEN-A-STORY-HAPPENS-WITH-NO-OTHER-EFFECT
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — includes the kitchen/storm-ruin discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from setting typically being introduced first as a factual identification task (name the time and place), with no early exposure to its atmospheric or thematic function.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct side-by-side comparison of the identical scenario in two settings**, since the atmospheric effect is far more convincing demonstrated than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given the same basic scenario in two contrasting settings, the learner explains how each setting actively shapes atmosphere or character possibility.

## Analogies

**Best — the Story Stage Set** (Blueprint Component 3): a miniature stage with character figures, a swappable backdrop, a problem card, and a chain of event cards, only some of which are causally connected to the problem card; physically swapping the backdrop between a cozy cottage and a dark storm directly seeds the setting fix, while removing the non-causally-connected event cards directly seeds the plot fix.
*Breaking point*: a physical stage set has a fixed, visible boundary between what's "on stage" (relevant) and off, while a real story's plot-relevance and setting-function require interpretive judgment about causal connection and thematic resonance that a stage set's clean separation doesn't fully capture.

**Alternative — a movie's script versus its cinematography.** The script (plot chain) could technically be filmed in any location, but the director's choice of location (setting) changes how the audience feels about identical dialogue and events — the same scene reads as cozy or menacing depending entirely on where it's shot.
*Breaking point*: a film's cinematography is a separate creative layer added after the script, while in written narrative, setting and plot are conceived and revealed together within the same text, without literally separable "layers."

**Visual anchor — the Story Stage Set**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"The plot is just what happens in the story, in order."** Directly installs the everything-counts misconception as a stated definition.
- **"Setting is just the time and place — where and when it happens."** Directly installs the neutral-background misconception as a stated definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Story Stage Set (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Which of these six event cards actually belong in the plot chain?" Then physically remove the non-causal ones and swap the backdrop.
2. **The plot-critical/incidental collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Does this detail belong in 'the plot,' or is it just texture?"
3. **The kitchen/storm-ruin collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Is the setting doing anything besides telling us where we are?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both structural facts are directly checkable by testing candidate events and settings against the story's own causal structure and atmosphere.

1. **Need**: "If I list every single thing that happens in a story, including the weather on a random morning, have I described 'the plot'?"
2. **Playground**: the learner sorts several event cards into plot-chain versus incidental freely before being told the official test.
3. **Invention**: "Can you change only the setting of this scene and describe how the feeling changes, even though nothing else changed?"
4. **Collision**: present the same scenario in two settings and ask whether setting is "just background" in both.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — plot is the causal chain to conflict resolution, not everything that happens; setting actively shapes mood and possibility — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Follow the causal chain for plot; ask what mood and possibility a setting creates."

## Teaching Sequence

- **The plot-chain test (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the first misconception before character or conflict content is introduced, since isolating the causal chain is the foundational analytical move the other elements build alongside.
- **Character identification (TA-2) and conflict types (TA-3) come second and third**, introducing the two remaining, less misconception-laden elements once the plot-chain discipline from TA-1 is established.
- **Setting as an active narrative tool (TA-4) comes fourth**, directly confronting the second misconception once the other three elements are secure, since setting's effect is easier to isolate once plot, character, and conflict are already correctly identified.
- **Identifying all four elements in a novel story (TA-5) comes last**, the generative capstone integrating both misconception fixes with the two more straightforward elements — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for including or excluding an event from the plot chain, and for what a setting is "doing" beyond labeling**, since a correct-looking sort or setting description with no stated causal or functional reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated causal-connection check** ("does this event cause or lead to the next plot event, or is it just texture?") applied to a new event — the direct behavioral signature of the plot-chain discipline taking hold.
- **Voluntary description of what a setting makes possible or believable**, beyond naming it — direct evidence the setting-as-active-tool fix is internalized.
- **A story summary that recounts every detail indiscriminately, with no filtering by causal relevance** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-PLOT-MEANS-EVERYTHING-THAT-HAPPENS-IN-THE-STORY still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Follow the causal chain for plot, and ask what mood or possibility the setting is creating."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But this detail is in the story too, so isn't it part of the plot?"** — take this as an accurate observation about presence, not a mistake; respond by adding the stricter test: "It's in the story, that's true — but does it CAUSE the next event, or lead into the conflict? If not, it's texture, not plot."
- **"I thought setting was just where and when it happens"** — a genuine, reasonable expectation given how setting is often first introduced; respond by naming the additional layer: "That's the basic fact, yes — but setting also creates a feeling and shapes what characters can plausibly do. What mood does THIS setting create?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full novel-story analysis down to **sorting a single short list of 4 events into plot versus incidental, with no character/conflict/setting analysis demanded.** Isolates the most basic plot-chain test before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just tell me what happens in the story."** This directly licenses the everything-counts pattern, undermining the causal-chain discipline this concept specifically builds.
