## Learning Objective
The learner will identify the specific diction, sentence-structure, and figurative choices that make up a piece of writing's style, explain how they combine into one consistent voice, and evaluate/adjust style deliberately to fit a given purpose and audience.

## Core Understanding
Style is not a single ingredient (vocabulary flashiness) but the CONSISTENT COMBINATION of diction (word choice level/precision), sentence structure (length, complexity, rhythm), figurative choices, and attitude, working together to create a recognizable "fingerprint." A skilled writer's style has both a CORE (habits that stay recognizable across pieces — a preference for concrete imagery, a characteristic rhythm) and a FLEXIBLE surface (formality, sentence complexity, humor) that should adjust deliberately by purpose and audience. Precision, not complexity, is what makes style strong: simple, well-chosen words used consistently often create a more confident voice than elaborate vocabulary reached for to sound impressive.

## Mental Models
- **Musician's-Range Anchor**: the same musician plays a gentle ballad and an energetic rock song — same core technical habits (a chord-voicing preference, characteristic phrasing) recognizable underneath deliberately adjusted surface choices (volume, tempo, instrumentation). Style works the same way: adjusting the flexible parts by purpose is skilled range, not inauthenticity.
- **Chef's-signature-dish**: style isn't defined by the most exotic ingredient in every recipe, but by a recognizable, consistent approach to flavor and technique — precision and consistency, not complexity for its own sake.

## Why Students Fail
Learners equate "strong style" with elaborate vocabulary because sophistication feels safer to demonstrate than precision. Separately, learners who've just found "their voice" often treat any adjustment for audience/purpose as inauthentic, not realizing skilled writers flex surface choices while keeping core habits recognizable.

## Misconceptions
- **MC-A (Type 1, overgeneralization from "sophisticated = impressive")**: "Style is just using fancy or complicated vocabulary." Diagnostic: present a simple, precise sentence ("The dog trotted across the green field, nose down, tail up") against a needlessly elaborate one on the same content ("The perambulating canine traversed the verdant expanse") and ask which has the stronger style. Repair: chef's-signature-dish framing — ask "is this the most PRECISE and EFFECTIVE word for the effect I want, and is it consistent with the voice I'm building?" not "is this a fancy enough word?"
- **MC-B (Type 1, overgeneralization from "authenticity = sameness")**: "My true style should stay exactly the same across every piece I write." Diagnostic: show two pieces by the same accomplished writer — one casual, one formal — both sharing recognizable habits; ask "is this the same writer? What stays, what changes?" A learner who insists adjusting voice by audience is "fake" signals MC-B. Repair: musician's-range anchor — distinguish core habits (stay) from flexible surface choices (should flex by purpose).

## Analogies
- Musician's-range anchor (above) for core-vs-flexible.
- Chef's-signature-dish for precision-over-complexity.
- Anti-analogy: this is not "anything goes as long as it's consistent" — a consistently bad stylistic choice (e.g., always using jargon a target audience won't understand) is still a style-purpose mismatch, not exempted by consistency alone.

## Demonstrations
- Side-by-side: elaborate-vocabulary sentence vs. simple-precise sentence describing the same scene; ask which has the stronger voice.
- Two passages by one constructed "writer" for two different purposes (casual blog post vs. formal museum placard on the same topic), with a shared core habit (vivid sensory detail) identified in both.
- A passage with a stated purpose/audience mismatch (overly casual piece for a formal audience) for the learner to evaluate and partially revise.

## Discovery Questions
- "Is this word the most precise choice for the effect you want, or does it just sound impressive?"
- "What stays the same about this writer's habits in both pieces? What changes?"
- "Is this piece's style well-suited to who's supposed to read it?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Diagnostic: figurative-language check (prerequisite) and basic diction/tone awareness.
2. Anchor: musician's-range framing, extended to precision-over-complexity.
3. TA-1: identify diction, sentence structure, and tone in contrasting passages.
4. TA-2: revise elaborate sentences into precise, simple ones (targets MC-A).
5. TA-3: distinguish core habits from flexible choices across two pieces by one writer (targets MC-B).
6. TA-4: evaluate and revise a style-purpose mismatch, preserving a recognizable core habit.
7. Independent: full style analysis + deliberate adjustment on a new passage.

## Tutor Actions
- SHOW: elaborate-vs-precise sentence pair; two-purposes-same-writer pair.
- DO: learner rewrites their own overly elaborate sentence into simple, precise language.
- TEST-THINKING: predict which core habits would survive a purpose change.
- ORGANIZE: a "core habit / flexible choice" two-column sort for a given writer's two pieces.

## Voice Teaching Notes
If a learner defends an elaborate-vocabulary passage as "sounding smart," don't argue the point directly — have them read both versions aloud; the simpler version's clarity and confidence is often more audible than arguable in text alone.

## Assessment Signals
- Probe: "Revise this overly elaborate sentence into simpler, more precise language and explain why the revision creates a stronger voice." Correct answer names precision, not vocabulary simplicity itself, as the goal.
- Probe: given two purpose-adjusted pieces by the same writer, "identify one core habit and one flexible choice." MC-B response denies any legitimate adjustment exists.
- Confident-wrong signature: a fluent revision that swaps simple words for obscure synonyms uniformly, defended as "improving the style" — flag MC-A regardless of grammatical correctness.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If a learner overcorrects after MC-A into flat, plain writing (fearing any vivid word choice), recalibrate explicitly: precision, not blandness, is the goal — a well-chosen vivid word is not the same as an unnecessarily obscure one.

## Memory Hooks
"Precise, not fancy" as the retrievable check against MC-A; "same musician, different song" as the retrievable check against MC-B.
