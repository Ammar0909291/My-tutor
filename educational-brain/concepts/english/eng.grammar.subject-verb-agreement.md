# Subject-Verb Agreement — `eng.grammar.subject-verb-agreement`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.subject-verb-agreement` (canonical
  English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Subject-Verb Agreement"
  — the grammatical rule that a verb must agree in number and person
  with its subject.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.nouns`, `eng.grammar.verbs`, AND
  `eng.grammar.present-tenses` — load-bearing on three fronts: nouns
  because identifying the TRUE subject (not the nearest noun) requires
  already reliably identifying nouns and their number; verbs because
  agreement is applied to the verb itself; present tenses because the
  third-person singular "-s" pattern (already established there) is
  the concrete mechanism agreement operates through in present-tense
  sentences.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.pronoun-antecedent-agreement`.
  That downstream concept formally extends the identical number/person-
  matching principle this concept establishes for subject-verb pairs to
  pronoun-antecedent pairs, reusing the same "find the TRUE governing
  element, not the nearest noun" discipline.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.80 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Apply basic singular/plural agreement between a subject and verb in
   simple sentences with no interrupting phrases.
2. Identify the TRUE subject when a prepositional phrase or other
   modifier sits between the subject and verb, using the cross-out
   technique, and apply correct agreement to that true subject.
3. Apply standard singular agreement to collective nouns (team, family,
   group, committee) when the group acts as one unit.
4. Apply the correct compound-subject agreement rule based on
   conjunction type — "and" typically takes a plural verb; "or/nor"
   agrees with the nearer subject.
5. Apply correct agreement for indefinite pronoun subjects (everyone,
   someone, each — typically singular; several, many, few — typically
   plural).

## Core Understanding

Subject-verb agreement requires the verb to match its subject in
number (singular/plural) and person — but the most load-bearing
insight this concept teaches is that the verb must agree with the TRUE
subject, never simply the nearest noun. When a prepositional phrase or
other modifier interrupts the subject and verb ("The box OF TOYS ___
on the floor"), that interrupting phrase does NOT change what the real
subject is — mentally "crossing out" the interrupting phrase reveals
the true subject ("The box ___ on the floor" — clearly singular "is,"
despite the nearby plural "toys"). A second, independent, equally
load-bearing insight concerns collective nouns: words like "team,"
"family," "group," and "committee" name a group of individuals treated
as a SINGLE unit, and in standard American English usage they typically
take a SINGULAR verb when the group acts together ("The team IS playing
well") — not a plural verb, despite genuinely referring to multiple
people. This is a specific, learnable convention (with British English
differing in some contexts), not a logical derivation from the
plurality of the group's members. Two further, more localized rules
complete the system: compound subjects joined by "and" typically take a
plural verb, while those joined by "or/nor" agree with whichever
subject sits nearer to the verb; and indefinite pronouns like
"everyone" or "someone" are typically singular, while others like
"several" or "many" are typically plural.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "the verb agrees with whichever noun is closest to
  it, and collective nouns like 'team' always take a plural verb since
  they refer to many people"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model
  this concept exists to correct — a natural default because the
  nearest noun genuinely IS a strong, immediately-available cue
  requiring no deeper sentence analysis, and because a group word
  logically DOES refer to multiple individuals, making a plural verb
  feel like the intuitively consistent choice.
- **Intermediate model — "I identify the true subject by crossing out
  interrupting phrases, and I apply agreement to that true subject"**:
  installed by TA-1 and TA-2, using the Component 3 cross-out-the-
  interrupter anchor, resolving MC-AGREE-WITH-THE-NEAREST-NOUN. Upgrade
  trigger: correctly agreeing with a fresh true subject despite a
  nearby, number-mismatched interrupting noun, without hesitation.
- **Advanced model — "I apply standard singular agreement to collective
  nouns acting as one unit"**: installed by TA-3, resolving
  MC-COLLECTIVE-NOUNS-ARE-ALWAYS-PLURAL-VERBS by directly establishing
  the singular-by-convention rule.
- **Expert model — "I apply the correct compound-subject rule based on
  conjunction type, and I apply correct agreement for indefinite
  pronoun subjects"**: installed by TA-4 and TA-5, the concept's
  genuine mastery bar — extending the core true-subject-identification
  discipline to two additional, more localized rule systems.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 doesn't mark subject-verb agreement the same
  way, or marks agreement differently for collective/group nouns,
  expect this concept — especially the interrupting-phrase and
  collective-noun cases — to require substantial dedicated practice,
  since these are well-documented persistent error areas even among
  advanced L2 English learners and writers, and even many native
  speakers in complex writing.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and the Blueprint
itself explicitly names both as persistent challenge areas beyond
early instruction. The nearest-noun failure exists because the closest
noun is genuinely the most immediately available, lowest-effort cue a
learner can reach for — proximity-based agreement is a reasonable
shortcut absent explicit instruction on identifying the TRUE subject,
with nothing marking the error until a learner directly examines "the
box of toys" and asks whether "of toys" genuinely changes what the
sentence's real subject is. The collective-nouns-always-plural failure
exists because a group word logically DOES refer to multiple
individuals, making a plural verb feel like the intuitively consistent,
even more "correct" choice — nothing marks the singular-by-convention
rule until a learner is directly asked, for a team acting together as
one unit, whether standard American usage prefers "is" or "are."

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.subject-verb-agreement.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-AGREE-WITH-THE-NEAREST-NOUN (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 2,
perceptual intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: Type 2, not Type 1 — this is a genuine
  perceptual/processing default rather than an overextension of a
  partially-correct grammatical rule: the nearest noun is physically,
  perceptually closest to the verb being produced, creating a strong
  proximity-based pull independent of any taught rule, similar to how
  adjective-order intuitions are felt rather than derived; the
  misconception survives because in the majority of simple sentences
  without interrupting phrases, the nearest noun genuinely IS the
  subject, offering no counter-evidence until an interrupting phrase
  directly separates the two.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a fresh
  interrupted-subject sentence, spontaneously mentally crosses out the
  interrupting phrase before selecting a verb form is showing the
  true-subject discipline has been genuinely internalized.

**MC-COLLECTIVE-NOUNS-ARE-ALWAYS-PLURAL-VERBS (Blueprint Component 1)
— Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — a group word
  genuinely does refer to multiple individuals, so extending "plural
  meaning requires plural verb" to collective nouns is a reasonable,
  logically-motivated generalization from the standard plural-agreement
  rule, until a learner is directly shown that standard American usage
  specifically treats these words as singular units by convention.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when discussing a
  fresh collective noun acting as one unit, spontaneously reaches for
  the singular verb form without hesitation is showing the singular-
  by-convention rule has generalized beyond the specific TA-3 practice
  items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the cross-out-the-interrupter check itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically
  covering or crossing out an interrupting prepositional phrase on a
  sentence card, revealing the true subject-verb relationship
  underneath, directly embodies the true-subject-identification
  discipline as a concrete, repeatable, visually-checkable process
  rather than an abstract mental rule. Breaking point: the cross-out
  technique demonstrates the interrupting-phrase case well but doesn't
  on its own address collective-noun agreement (TA-3), which needs its
  own separate, convention-focused demonstration.
- **Alternative — a team huddle vs. individual players scattering**: a
  sports team huddling together, moving and acting as one coordinated
  unit, is naturally described with singular language ("the team IS
  huddling"), while the same players scattering to their individual
  positions would more naturally invite plural, individual-focused
  language — parallel to collective nouns defaulting to singular when
  the group acts as one, with genuine plural exceptions reserved for
  emphasizing individual members acting separately. Breaking point:
  useful for the acting-as-one-unit insight, but doesn't address the
  interrupting-phrase true-subject discipline, which the cross-out
  anchor's own TA-2 practice handles directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe the nearest-noun error as "just
  carelessness" or "not paying attention"**: this framing misdiagnoses
  a genuine, systematic perceptual pull (proximity-based agreement) as
  a simple attention lapse, discouraging the learner from developing
  the specific, reusable cross-out STRATEGY this concept teaches;
  instead, frame it as a real, well-documented pattern with a real,
  learnable fix, not a character flaw or lapse in focus.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the cross-out-the-interrupter check)**: the
  Blueprint's Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any real object described with a modifying
  phrase in the learner's environment ("the pile of books," "the group
  of friends") supplies real TA-1/TA-2 material; any real team, family,
  or group the learner belongs to supplies real TA-3 collective-noun
  material.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's compound-subject-agreement
  task (applying different rules for "and" vs. "or/nor") is inherently
  hands-on, requiring the learner to actively distinguish two genuinely
  different rule systems, not merely recognize a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the true-subject test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether "of
  toys" changes what the true subject of "the box of toys" actually is,
  making the true-subject-identification discipline directly audible
  and testable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming an
  agreement attempt (TA-1 through TA-5), ask the learner to state their
  own attempt before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
true-subject-identification discipline and the collective-noun
singular-by-convention rule are both genuine, directly testable
phenomena well-suited to guided discovery: **need** (the learner,
having secured TA-1's basic agreement practice, is shown "the box of
toys ___ on the floor" and asked which noun is the true subject,
discovering their proximity-based instinct feels wrong once the
interrupting phrase is examined); **playground/invention** (TA-1's
basic-agreement practice builds initial confidence with the core rule
before the interrupting-phrase and collective-noun complications are
introduced); **collision** (the Blueprint's own "box of toys" probe
breaks the nearest-noun assumption; the "the team ___ playing well"
probe breaks the collective-nouns-always-plural assumption);
**formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of both the true-subject
discipline and the collective-noun convention); **compression** (TA-5's
fluent indefinite-pronoun-agreement production, applying the true-
subject discipline directly without each instance separately re-
derived). The specific terms "true subject," "collective noun," and
"indefinite pronoun" are convention-bound grammatical terminology best
introduced by direct instruction once the learner has already felt the
underlying distinctions through the cross-out anchor and TA-1–2.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (basic singular/plural agreement) is placed first as the concept's
most stable entry point, per the Blueprint's own session architecture —
establishing the core agreement rule in simple, uninterrupted sentences
before any complication is introduced. TA-2 (agreement with
interrupting phrases) follows immediately, directly targeting
MC-AGREE-WITH-THE-NEAREST-NOUN using the cross-out technique. TA-3
(collective noun agreement) introduces a SECOND, independent dimension
(a specific lexical-category convention, not proximity-based error),
directly targeting MC-COLLECTIVE-NOUNS-ARE-ALWAYS-PLURAL-VERBS, once
the base true-subject discipline from TA-1/TA-2 is secure. TA-4
(compound subject agreement) extends the concept to two genuinely
different conjunction-based rules (and vs. or/nor), building on the
now-secure singular/plural foundation. TA-5 (indefinite pronoun
agreement) is placed last as the concept's integration capstone,
applying the true-subject and number-determination disciplines to a
final, lexically-varied subject category. This entry does not restate
the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see
Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously mentally
  crosses out an interrupting phrase before selecting a verb form for a
  fresh sentence, and whether the learner spontaneously reaches for the
  singular verb form for a fresh collective noun acting as one unit —
  these are the primary diagnostic signals for whether TA-2 and TA-3's
  disciplines have genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice
  items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before selecting a verb
  form for a fresh interrupted-subject sentence, followed by a correct,
  true-subject-consistent choice, suggests the learner is consciously
  applying the cross-out technique rather than defaulting to the
  nearest noun — a good sign. Instant, confident agreement with the
  nearest noun (without checking the true subject) is the concerning
  pattern for MC-AGREE-WITH-THE-NEAREST-NOUN.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Protocol Routing — for S9 learners whose L1 doesn't
  mark subject-verb agreement the same way, or marks agreement
  differently for collective/group nouns, reassure that interrupting-
  phrase and collective-noun agreement remain well-documented,
  persistent error areas even for advanced L2 English learners and
  many native speakers, warranting sustained, dedicated practice.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does that phrase in the middle change
  what the TRUE subject is?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence
  for MC-AGREE-WITH-THE-NEAREST-NOUN, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh interrupted-subject sentence is introduced, making
  the true-subject check a routinely-invited response rather than an
  afterthought.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedural/framework — a true-subject-identification
discipline (extending across interrupting phrases) combined with a
lexical-category convention (collective nouns) and two conjunction-
based sub-rules (compound subjects with and/or/nor), applied to
genuinely novel sentences; mastery is demonstrated by correctly
applying agreement across fresh sentences of all five types (MP-1
through MP-4) rather than by recognizing a fixed, previously-taught
example list. Interleaving partners: mix basic-agreement items (testing
TA-1) with interrupting-phrase items (testing TA-2), collective-noun
items (testing TA-3), and compound-subject items (testing TA-4) in
ongoing review, so the learner continues exercising all dimensions of
the agreement system, since interrupting-phrase and collective-noun
cases remain persistent error sources warranting sustained, distributed
practice.

- **Real-world**: every sentence the learner produces — in speech or

  grammatical correctness, with interrupting-phrase and collective-
  noun cases remaining genuinely challenging even in the learner's own

- **Expert-transfer**: polished, professional-quality writing at any
  advanced level depends on reliable subject-verb agreement across
  complex sentence structures, since agreement errors — especially
  with interrupting phrases and collective nouns — remain among the
  most immediately noticeable markers of unpolished writing, even at
  otherwise advanced proficiency and even for native speakers.
