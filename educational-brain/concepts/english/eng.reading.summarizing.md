# Summarizing — `eng.reading.summarizing`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.reading.summarizing` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / reading / "Summarizing" —
  condensing a text's essential content into a concise restatement in
  one's own words.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.reading.main-idea-and-details` — load-bearing
  because summarizing IS main-idea/detail-weighting judgment applied
  under a compression constraint, per the Blueprint's own Protocol
  Routing (S0): a learner who cannot already distinguish a main idea
  from supporting details, and essential details from minor ones, has
  no basis for deciding what to keep and what to compress away when
  producing a summary.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.reading.compare-and-contrast-texts`. Per
  the Blueprint's own Component 8 Adaptive Flags, that concept requires
  summarizing each text first before the comparison itself can happen,
  and directly reuses this concept's "suitcase, not a slightly smaller
  closet" compression framing.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: analyze · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Restate a text's content in their own words and sentence structure,
   without copying or lightly rephrasing the original's exact wording.
2. Sort a passage's details into essential (load-bearing for the main
   idea) and minor (illustrative flavor, safely droppable) categories.
3. Produce a summary that achieves genuine COMPRESSION — significantly
   shorter than the original — by deliberately omitting minor details
   rather than attempting to include everything in slightly fewer
   words.
4. Combine own-words restatement and appropriate compression
   simultaneously in a single summary of a multi-paragraph passage.
5. Independently summarize a novel, unscaffolded multi-paragraph text
   under a specified length constraint, self-checking it against an
   own-words/essential-only/correct-main-idea checklist.

## Core Understanding

A summary is not a selection of the original text's "important"
sentences strung together — it is a restatement of the text's essential
content built entirely in the reader's OWN words and sentence
structure. Copying sentences, even genuinely important ones, only
proves the reader can locate text; it does not prove they understood
it. A real summary requires processing the meaning and rebuilding it
independently — if a reader cannot restate content without looking back
at the original's exact phrasing, that signals the content hasn't been
fully digested yet, not that summarizing means finding the right
sentences to quote. A second, equally load-bearing insight concerns
LENGTH: a summary's entire purpose is compression — capturing essential
content in significantly less space than the original. A summary that
is nearly as long as the source has not done its job, regardless of how
accurately worded it is. Achieving real compression requires actively
judging which details are truly load-bearing for the main idea (details
that would change a reader's understanding if removed) versus which are
minor illustrative flavor that can be safely dropped entirely — not
attempting to preserve as much of the original as possible in a
slightly trimmed form. Genuine summarizing therefore requires BOTH
disciplines working together: own-words restatement (not copying) and
deliberate, judgment-based omission (not comprehensive inclusion) — a
summary that only achieves one is not yet a complete summary.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "summarizing means finding and copying the
  important sentences, and a good summary should include as much of
  the original as possible"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model
  this concept exists to correct — two related defaults (copy-instead-
  of-restate, and include-everything-instead-of-compress) that both
  avoid the harder cognitive work of processing and genuinely
  compressing meaning, per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing (S1).
- **Intermediate model — "I restate content in my own words without
  looking back at the original's exact phrasing"**: installed by TA-1
  (cover-and-restate practice) using the Packing-the-Weekend-Suitcase
  anchor's own-words-description half, resolving MC-A-SUMMARY-MEANS-
  COPYING-THE-IMPORTANT-SENTENCES. Upgrade trigger: spontaneously
  restating a passage in fresh wording without needing to reference the
  original.
- **Advanced model — "I sort details into essential (load-bearing) and
  minor (droppable), and I deliberately achieve real compression by
  cutting minor details entirely rather than trimming everything
  slightly"**: installed by TA-2 (essential vs. minor sorting) and TA-3
  (the suitcase compression test) using the anchor's packing-means-
  leaving-things-behind half, resolving MC-A-GOOD-SUMMARY-INCLUDES-
  EVERY-DETAIL-FROM-THE-ORIGINAL.
- **Expert model — "I combine own-words restatement and genuine
  compression simultaneously, and I independently summarize novel,
  longer, unscaffolded passages under a length constraint"**: installed
  by TA-4 (combining own-words and compression) and TA-5 (summarizing a
  novel multi-paragraph text), the concept's genuine mastery bar —
  demonstrating both disciplines operating together, not in isolation.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing
  (S9), mastery must NOT be certified unless MP-1 (rejecting a copied-
  sentence "summary") and MP-2 (achieving real compression) both pass —
  a learner who correctly sorts essential/minor details in isolation
  (MP-3) but still copies phrasing or over-includes details in an
  actual summary has not yet integrated both required skills.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and the Blueprint
itself explicitly frames both as ways of avoiding the harder cognitive
work genuine summarizing requires. The copy-the-important-sentences
failure (MC-A-SUMMARY-MEANS-COPYING-THE-IMPORTANT-SENTENCES) exists
because locating and reproducing "important-sounding" sentences is
genuinely easier than processing meaning and rebuilding it
independently — it requires only recognition and selection, not
synthesis, making it an attractive shortcut that superficially resembles
a summary without demonstrating the understanding a summary is meant to
prove. The include-every-detail failure (MC-A-GOOD-SUMMARY-INCLUDES-
EVERY-DETAIL-FROM-THE-ORIGINAL) exists because omitting content feels
risky — a learner uncertain about which details are truly load-bearing
reasonably defaults toward including more rather than less, since
leaving out a detail that turns out to matter feels like a bigger
failure than a summary that's merely too long; this caution avoids the
harder, riskier judgment call of deciding what can be safely dropped.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.reading.summarizing.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions
this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-A-SUMMARY-MEANS-COPYING-THE-IMPORTANT-SENTENCES (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 5, instruction-induced**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: Type 5, traceable to a specific
  external pattern rather than a self-generated overgeneralization —
  many prior reading tasks (this concept's own prerequisite, literal
  comprehension, and "find the sentence that says X" style exercises)
  explicitly reward locating and quoting text; a learner who has
  succeeded repeatedly with find-and-quote strategies reasonably
  extends that specific, previously-rewarded pattern to summarizing,
  where it is no longer the correct standard.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, after covering the
  original text, produces a restatement in visibly different sentence
  structure and wording — without needing to peek back — is showing
  the own-words discipline has been internalized.

**MC-A-GOOD-SUMMARY-INCLUDES-EVERY-DETAIL-FROM-THE-ORIGINAL (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: Type 1 — thoroughness and inclusion
  genuinely ARE valued in many other reading and writing contexts (not
  missing important information is usually praised), so extending
  "don't leave things out" to summarizing is a reasonable but
  overextended application of a generally correct instinct; the
  overgeneralization surfaces specifically because summarizing's whole
  purpose — unlike most other reading tasks — is deliberate,
  judgment-based omission, which directly inverts the usual
  don't-leave-anything-out instinct.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, given a target
  length constraint, confidently and deliberately omits a specific,
  named minor detail ("I'm leaving out the mayor's jacket color because
  it doesn't affect the main idea") is showing the essential-vs-minor
  judgment has generalized past comprehensive-inclusion instinct.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — Packing the Weekend Suitcase itself is the analogy,
  not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): choosing 5 truly
  essential items from a full closet to physically pack into a small
  suitcase, genuinely leaving the rest behind, directly seeds MC-A-
  GOOD-SUMMARY-INCLUDES-EVERY-DETAIL-FROM-THE-ORIGINAL's fix — a
  summary is a suitcase, not a slightly-trimmed closet. Describing what
  was packed to a friend in the learner's own words, rather than
  reciting the closet's inventory list, directly seeds MC-A-SUMMARY-
  MEANS-COPYING-THE-IMPORTANT-SENTENCES's fix — a summary is a fresh
  description, not a recited list. Breaking point: the packing metaphor
  works cleanly for the two core insights on a single passage, but
  needs explicit extension (TA-4/TA-5) to demonstrate both disciplines
  operating simultaneously on a longer, multi-paragraph text — one
  packing exercise doesn't on its own build that combined, extended
  practice.
- **Alternative — a movie trailer vs. the full film**: a good trailer
  captures the essential feel and key moments of a film in a fraction
  of its length, using its own edited footage and framing — it doesn't
  simply play consecutive scenes verbatim, nor does it try to include
  every scene. Breaking point: useful for the compression insight
  broadly, but the "own edited footage" parallel to "own words" is
  looser than the suitcase anchor's direct describe-in-your-own-words
  framing.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT frame summarizing as "pick out the most
  important sentences"**: this framing directly reinforces MC-A-
  SUMMARY-MEANS-COPYING-THE-IMPORTANT-SENTENCES by treating selection
  (choosing which original sentences matter) as the task itself, rather
  than restatement; instead, per the Blueprint's own replacement text,
  the correct framing is: identify the main idea and key details, THEN
  close or cover the original and rebuild the content in fresh
  wording — selection and restatement are two distinct steps, not one.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (Packing the Weekend Suitcase)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any short article, recipe, or story the
  learner has access to supplies genuine TA-1/TA-2 material — read it,
  cover it, and restate the content aloud in fresh words; sort its
  details into essential and minor categories without needing special
  materials.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-5's independent multi-paragraph
  summarization under a length constraint, followed by self-checking
  against a checklist, is inherently hands-on, requiring the learner to
  produce and verify a complete summary rather than evaluate a given
  one.
- **Teacher-demo (the copied-vs-restated contrast)**: the tutor models
  the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration directly —
  presenting a "summary" made of copied sentences beside one restated
  in fresh words at similar length, asking which one demonstrates
  genuine understanding, making the copy-vs-restate distinction
  directly visible.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  restatement, essential/minor sort, compression, or combined-summary
  attempt (TA-1 through TA-5), ask the learner to state their own
  attempt before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** Both the
own-words-restatement insight and the deliberate-compression insight
are genuinely felt, directly testable phenomena well-suited to guided
discovery: **need** (the learner is shown a copied-sentence "summary"
and an own-words one of similar length, and asked which one shows the
reader actually understood the passage, discovering that copying proves
only location, not comprehension); **playground/invention** (TA-1's
cover-and-restate practice and TA-2's essential/minor sorting build
initial restatement and judgment skills before the combined-compression
complication is introduced); **collision** (the Blueprint's own
target-length probe — asking the learner to write a summary roughly
one-quarter the original's length and observing whether minor details
can be cut — directly breaks the include-everything assumption when the
learner struggles to reach the target length without cutting); **for-
malization** (the restate-not-copy framing for MC-A-SUMMARY-MEANS-
COPYING-THE-IMPORTANT-SENTENCES, and the load-bearing-vs-flavor framing
for MC-A-GOOD-SUMMARY-INCLUDES-EVERY-DETAIL-FROM-THE-ORIGINAL);
**compression** (TA-5's fluent independent summarization of a novel,
longer passage, combining both disciplines without each separately
re-derived).

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (cover-and-restate practice) is placed first, directly targeting
MC-A-SUMMARY-MEANS-COPYING-THE-IMPORTANT-SENTENCES, per the Blueprint's
own session architecture — establishing own-words restatement as the
standard practice from the very first activity, before compression
judgment is introduced. TA-2 (essential vs. minor detail sorting)
builds the judgment skill needed for real compression, directly reusing
the main-idea/detail-weighting discipline from the prerequisite
concept. TA-3 (the suitcase compression test) directly targets MC-A-
GOOD-SUMMARY-INCLUDES-EVERY-DETAIL-FROM-THE-ORIGINAL, applying TA-2's
sorting judgment under an explicit length constraint to force genuine
omission rather than comprehensive-but-shortened inclusion. TA-4
(combining own-words and compression) requires both disciplines
(TA-1's restatement and TA-2/TA-3's compression) to operate together
on a longer passage, since a learner who has practiced each skill in
isolation has not yet demonstrated they can apply both simultaneously.
TA-5 (summarizing a novel multi-paragraph text) is placed last as the
concept's genuine generative capstone, requiring fully independent,
unscaffolded application of both disciplines under a specified length
constraint, with self-verification against an explicit checklist. This
entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script
(Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner's restatement uses
  visibly different sentence structure and wording from the original
  (not merely swapped synonyms), and whether the learner spontaneously
  names a specific detail as safely droppable with justification —
  these are the primary diagnostic signals for whether TA-1 and TA-2/
  TA-3's disciplines have genuinely generalized.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause to mentally reprocess
  content before restating it (rather than glancing back at the
  original), followed by a genuinely fresh restatement, suggests the
  learner is consciously rebuilding meaning rather than lightly
  rephrasing — a good sign. Instant, near-verbatim restatement with
  only minor word swaps is the concerning pattern for MC-A-SUMMARY-
  MEANS-COPYING-THE-IMPORTANT-SENTENCES.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: not specifically flagged for L1-
  transfer in this Blueprint — both misconceptions here stem from
  genuine reading-task-transfer patterns (prior find-and-quote success,
  general don't-omit-anything instinct) rather than L1-specific
  structural transfer, so no S9-specific routing beyond standard
  practice depth is called for.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does the main idea actually depend on
  this detail, or is it just extra flavor?" is this concept's load-
  bearing sentence for MC-A-GOOD-SUMMARY-INCLUDES-EVERY-DETAIL-FROM-
  THE-ORIGINAL, per `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-
  model.md §3` — asked whenever a fresh detail is being considered for
  inclusion, making the essential-vs-minor check a routinely-invited
  response rather than an afterthought.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedural/framework — a dual-discipline compression
practice (own-words restatement plus deliberate, judgment-based
omission) applied to genuinely novel passages of increasing length;
mastery is demonstrated by independently producing a complete,
appropriately compressed, own-words summary of a fresh, previously-
unseen multi-paragraph passage under a length constraint (MP-5) rather
than by recognizing a previously-practiced example. Interleaving
partners: mix cover-and-restate, essential/minor sorting, and
compression-under-constraint tasks together in ongoing review sets
(rather than blocking practice by skill type), converging toward TA-4/
TA-5's combined, independent application only once each individual
discipline is secure.
