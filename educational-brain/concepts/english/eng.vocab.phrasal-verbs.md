# Phrasal Verbs — `eng.vocab.phrasal-verbs`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.phrasal-verbs` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Phrasal Verbs" — verb-
  plus-particle combinations ("give up," "look after") that function as
  a single semantic unit, often with a meaning not derivable from the
  individual verb and particle, and with specific separability rules
  governing object placement.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.collocations` — load-bearing because
  phrasal verbs are a specialized, very common case of the fixed-
  pairing principle that concept established: a verb plus one or more
  small particles functions as ONE unit of meaning, extending the
  collocations concept's own puzzle-piece, shape-not-meaning framing
  directly (per the Blueprint's own explicit Adaptive Flag naming this
  reuse).
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none — this concept is terminal in the current
  curriculum sequence, though it is a foundational, high-frequency
  vocabulary category in everyday spoken and written English.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Recognize a phrasal verb as a single semantic unit and explain its
   meaning, distinguishing it from a literal reading of the verb plus a
   simple preposition.
2. Correctly determine whether a specific phrasal verb is separable
   (the object can go between the verb and particle) or inseparable
   (the object must follow the whole unit).
3. Correctly use a pronoun object with a separable phrasal verb,
   recognizing that a pronoun object MUST go between the verb and
   particle.
4. Test a phrasal verb's separability status by attempting both object
   placements and checking against fluent-speaker judgment.
5. Fully analyze a passage containing phrasal verbs with various object
   types — identifying each unit's meaning, determining separability,
   and correctly placing any pronoun object.

## Core Understanding

A phrasal verb is a verb combined with one or more small particles (up,
off, in, after) that together function as a SINGLE semantic unit, with
a meaning that is often completely different from either word's literal
sense — "give up" (meaning "to quit or surrender") has no derivable
connection to physically giving something in an upward direction, the
same way modern speakers learn "breakfast" as one fused word rather than
deriving it fresh each time from "break" plus "fast." A second, equally
load-bearing fact is that a phrasal verb's two parts can sometimes be
SEPARATED by an object, and this behavior varies by specific verb, not
by any general rule: SEPARABLE phrasal verbs allow the object either
between the verb and particle or after the whole unit with a noun
object ("give up the fight" or "give the fight up," both correct),
while INSEPARABLE phrasal verbs require the object to always follow the
whole unit together ("look after her," never "look her after"). A
further, specific rule sharpens this: for separable phrasal verbs
specifically, a PRONOUN object MUST go between the verb and particle,
never after the whole unit ("give it up," never "give up it") — a noun
object has flexibility a pronoun object does not.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "I can figure out a phrasal verb's meaning by
  combining the literal meanings of the verb and the particle
  separately"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept
  exists to correct — a natural extension of ordinary compositional
  reading strategy, where combining word meanings normally does
  construct sentence meaning, giving no built-in signal that phrasal
  verbs are a specific category where this strategy typically fails.
- **Intermediate model — "I can recognize a phrasal verb as a single
  semantic unit and state its whole-unit meaning"**: installed by TA-1's
  recognition practice, using the Component 3 breakfast-fusion anchor to
  make the whole-unit-learning principle concrete. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly stating a genuinely novel phrasal verb's meaning as a whole
  unit, without attempting to derive it from the individual verb and
  particle first.
- **Advanced model — "phrasal verbs vary in separability — some allow
  the object between the parts or after the whole unit, others require
  the object to always follow the whole unit"**: installed by TA-2's
  separability-testing practice, resolving MC-B-YOU-CAN-ALWAYS-PLACE-
  THE-OBJECT-EITHER-BETWEEN-THE-VERB-AND-PARTICLE-OR-AFTER-BOTH-
  REGARDLESS-OF-WHICH-PHRASAL-VERB-IT-IS through the felt contrast
  between "give up the fight"/"give the fight up" (both work) and "look
  after her"/"look her after" (only one works). Upgrade trigger:
  spontaneously testing BOTH object placements for a fresh phrasal verb
  before assuming its separability status.
- **Expert model — "I know the pronoun-specific rule — a pronoun object
  MUST split a separable phrasal verb — and I can fully analyze phrasal
  verbs in context, applying meaning, separability, and pronoun
  placement together"**: installed by TA-3's pronoun-placement
  extension and TA-4's full-synthesis task. Per the Blueprint's own
  explicit protocol routing, MC-A generally needs resolving before
  MC-B, since a learner who hasn't accepted the whole-unit-meaning
  principle has less motivation to carefully learn each phrasal verb's
  individual separability behavior.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, a
  learner who masters separability testing (MP-2/MP-4) but consistently
  forgets the pronoun-specific rule (MP-3) needs additional DEDICATED
  practice on this specific sub-rule — it is a common, persistent error
  even among otherwise proficient speakers, and should not be treated as
  a sign the broader separability concept is unlearned.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of otherwise-reliable language patterns. The literal-
derivation failure exists because combining a verb's and a particle's
individual meanings genuinely IS how most verb-plus-preposition
combinations in English work outside the phrasal-verb category ("walk
up the stairs" is genuinely about walking in an upward direction), so a
learner reasonably applies this general compositional strategy to
phrasal verbs too, with nothing in ordinary exposure marking that this
SPECIFIC category of fixed verb-particle combinations resists that
strategy until a context clearly fails to support the literal reading.
The uniform-separability failure exists because a learner who has just
correctly learned that ONE phrasal verb (e.g., "give up") allows both
object placements reasonably generalizes this flexibility as a property
of phrasal verbs in general, with nothing in that single correct
example marking that separability is a per-verb, individually-learned
property rather than a general rule, until a genuinely inseparable
verb (like "look after") is directly tested and found to reject the
split-object placement.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the breakfast-word-fusion and fixed-vs-openable-
  zipper anchor itself is the analogy, not a supplement to it**
  (Blueprint Component 3): learning "breakfast" as one fused whole-word
  unit rather than deriving it from "break" plus "fast" each time
  directly embodies the whole-unit-learning insight (MC-A's fix); a
  zipper that can be opened from either end versus one fixed to work
  only one specific way directly embodies the per-verb separability
  insight (MC-B's fix). Breaking point: a zipper's separability is a
  fixed, permanent physical property of that specific zipper, which
  usefully parallels a phrasal verb's fixed separability status, but
  the zipper metaphor doesn't itself capture the FURTHER pronoun-
  specific sub-rule (TA-3) — this entry treats the zipper anchor as the
  introductory frame for the basic separable/inseparable distinction,
  with TA-3 explicitly extending it to the pronoun case as a further,
  separately-taught refinement.
- **Alternative — a two-part name that must stay together, versus one
  that can be reordered (like "Mary Smith" vs. certain formal titles)**:
  some name-and-title combinations tolerate reordering without losing
  their reference, while others are conventionally fixed in one order
  — parallels how some phrasal verbs tolerate object-position
  flexibility while others are fixed. Breaking point: useful for
  reinforcing that fixedness varies case-by-case, but less vivid and
  concrete than the zipper anchor for conveying the physical,
  testable, binary open/fixed distinction.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe inseparable phrasal verbs as
  "stricter" or "more formal" than separable ones**: framing
  inseparability as a matter of formality or strictness risks implying
  a learner could deduce which category a verb falls into from its
  register or tone; separability is an arbitrary, lexically-specific
  property that must be individually learned per verb, with no reliable
  shortcut from formality level or any other general property.

- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
whole-unit-meaning principle and the per-verb separability property are
both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured the collocation fixed-pairing
principle, is asked whether "gave up" in "she finally gave up" involves
physically giving anything upward); **playground/invention** (TA-1's
whole-unit-meaning practice across five sentences lets the learner
notice the non-derivable pattern); **collision** (the Blueprint's own
gave-up literal-reading probe breaks the literal-derivation assumption;
the look-after/give-up separability contrast breaks the uniform-
separability assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's full contextual
application, requiring explicit statement of meaning, separability, and
pronoun placement together); **compression** (TA-4's fluent full-
synthesis application without each step separately re-derived). The
specific terms "separable" and "inseparable" are convention-bound
terminology best introduced by direct instruction once the learner has
already felt the underlying per-verb distinction through the anchor and
TA-2.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (recognizing phrasal verbs as single semantic units) is placed
first, directly targeting MC-A, because accepting the whole-unit-
meaning principle is itself a prerequisite to everything that follows —
per the Blueprint's own explicit protocol routing, MC-A generally needs
resolving before MC-B, since a learner who hasn't accepted the whole-
unit-meaning principle has less motivation to carefully learn each
phrasal verb's individual separability behavior. TA-2 (separable vs.
inseparable phrasal verbs) follows, directly targeting MC-B, sequenced
after the learner already has secure whole-unit recognition (TA-1) to
apply separability testing to genuinely understood units rather than
still-being-derived ones. TA-3 (pronoun objects must split separable
phrasal verbs) extends TA-2's separability distinction to the further,
specific pronoun-placement sub-rule — placed after basic separability
is secure because the pronoun rule only applies meaningfully to
phrasal verbs already correctly classified as separable. TA-4 (full
application) is placed last as the concept's complete synthesis task,
requiring whole-unit meaning recognition (TA-1), separability testing
(TA-2), and pronoun placement (TA-3) all applied together to a
genuinely novel passage. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's
own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint
References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously treats a
  fresh phrasal verb as a whole unit to be learned (rather than
  attempting literal derivation) and spontaneously tests both object
  placements before assuming separability — these are the primary
  diagnostic signals for whether TA-1 and TA-2's disciplines have
  genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before placing a
  pronoun object with a separable phrasal verb, followed by a correct
  split placement ("give it up," not "give up it"), suggests the
  learner is consciously applying TA-3's specific sub-rule rather than
  defaulting to the noun-object pattern — a good sign. Instant,
  confident placement of a pronoun object AFTER the whole unit (a
  common, persistent error even among otherwise proficient speakers,
  per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags) is the concerning pattern
  warranting dedicated TA-3 practice, distinct from a broader
  separability failure.
- **Implicit-vs-explicit stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags — a learner whose own speech or
  writing already uses phrasal verbs fluently and correctly (common for
  native or highly proficient speakers) should have mastery probes
  focused on EXPLICIT articulation of the separability rules rather
  than production alone, since implicit correct use doesn't guarantee
  explicit metalinguistic awareness.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does the object have to go between the
  parts, or does it have to follow the whole unit — test both" is this
  concept's load-bearing sentence for MC-B, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh phrasal verb's separability is in question, making
  per-verb testing a routinely-invited response rather than an
  assumption.
