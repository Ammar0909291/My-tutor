# Word Formation Processes — `eng.vocab.word-formation-processes`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.word-formation-processes` (canonical
  English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Word Formation Processes"
  — the systematic processes (derivation, compounding, conversion,
  blending, clipping) by which English creates new words, taught as
  five structurally distinct mechanisms rather than one generic
  "combining" process.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.roots-and-origins`,
  `eng.vocab.compound-words` — both load-bearing because this concept
  explicitly builds on and contrasts with the two mechanisms those
  concepts already installed (derivation-adjacent root/affix analysis,
  and compounding), using them as the secure baseline against which
  three genuinely new processes (conversion, blending, clipping) are
  introduced and distinguished.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.vocab.academic-vocabulary`,
  `eng.linguistics.morphology-intro`. Academic vocabulary is
  disproportionately built through derivation and compounding, so
  recognizing these processes productively is directly useful there;
  the linguistics concept formalizes these same five processes with
  proper terminology (free/bound morphemes, zero-derivation), building
  directly on this concept's intuitive "factory" model.
- **Difficulty**: advanced · **Bloom**: analyze · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 3

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Sort new words into their correct formation process — derivation,
   compounding, conversion, blending, or clipping — explaining what
   specifically happened to each word's form.
2. Review and extend derivation (affix-based word-class change) and
   compounding (joining two whole words) as already-familiar processes.
3. Verify that conversion involves genuinely ZERO change to spelling or
   pronunciation — only a shift in grammatical role — without searching
   for a hidden change that isn't there.
4. Identify blending (material fused and lost from both source words)
   and clipping (simple shortening with no meaning change) and
   correctly distinguish the material actually lost or joined in each.
5. Identify the primary word-formation process behind a genuinely
   unfamiliar or recently-coined word, justifying the classification
   with specific form-change evidence.

## Core Understanding

English creates new words through several structurally distinct
processes, not one single generic mechanism: DERIVATION adds an affix to
change a word's class (happy + -ness = happiness); COMPOUNDING joins two
whole existing words (tooth + brush = toothbrush); CONVERSION shifts a
word's grammatical role with genuinely zero change to its spelling or
pronunciation (the noun "email" becoming the verb "to email," entirely
identical in form); BLENDING fuses parts of two words together, with
material actually dropped from both (breakfast + lunch = brunch); and
CLIPPING simply shortens a longer word with no meaning change
(advertisement → ad). The single most counter-intuitive of these is
conversion, precisely because it is defined by the ABSENCE of any
visible change — unlike every other process, which leaves some trace on
the word's form (an added affix, joined words, fused-and-shortened
material, or simple truncation), conversion's entire "action" is
invisible: the word looks and sounds completely identical in both uses,
and only its grammatical job in the sentence differs. Genuine mastery
requires recognizing which of these five distinct tools produced a
given new word by examining what actually happened to its form, not by
defaulting to a single assumed mechanism.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "new words are made by combining word parts,"
  applied as a single, generic explanation for every new word**: the
  arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct — a
  natural extrapolation from the prerequisite concepts' own combining-
  based processes (derivation's affix-attachment, compounding's whole-
  word-joining), which together cover a genuine and common pattern but
  give no signal that conversion, blending, and clipping work by
  entirely different mechanisms.
- **Intermediate model — "I can sort new words into five distinct
  formation processes and explain what happened to each word's form"**:
  installed by TA-1's five-process sort, using the Component 3 Five
  Word Factories anchor to make each mechanism concrete and visually
  distinct. Upgrade trigger: correctly sorting a genuinely mixed set of
  ten words (two per process) without defaulting to a single explanation
  for all of them.
- **Advanced model — "derivation and compounding are secure processes I
  already know, and conversion is a genuinely zero-change process — I
  don't need to find a hidden change to trust it"**: installed by TA-2's
  review and TA-3's explicit zero-change verification, resolving
  MC-CONVERSION-MUST-INVOLVE-SOME-HIDDEN-SUFFIX-OR-CHANGE through the
  felt confirmation that "email" (noun) and "email" (verb) are letter-
  for-letter, sound-for-sound identical. Upgrade trigger: confidently
  confirming a genuine conversion pair as identical in form without
  searching for a spelling difference first.
- **Expert model — "I can identify blending's genuine material loss,
  clipping's simple shortening, and apply the full five-process
  discrimination to genuinely unfamiliar, recently-coined words"**:
  installed by TA-4's blending/clipping practice and TA-5's novel-word-
  analysis capstone, resolving MC-ALL-NEW-WORDS-ARE-FORMED-THE-SAME-WAY
  at its most demanding level. Per the Blueprint's own explicit mastery
  gate, certification requires BOTH MP-1 (five-process discrimination)
  and MP-2 (true conversion verification) to pass — a learner who sorts
  familiar examples correctly (MP-3) but cannot verify a true conversion
  or analyze a truly novel word has not yet learned the analytical skill
  this concept requires.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own explicit mastery-
  gate rule (Component 7's S9 protocol), do not certify mastery on
  familiar-example sorting alone — both MP-1 and MP-2 must
  independently pass, since recognizing already-taught examples is a
  fundamentally easier task than the analytical discrimination (five
  distinct processes, true zero-change verification) this concept is
  actually built to install.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of the prerequisite concepts' own genuinely reliable
patterns. The single-process-default failure exists because derivation
and compounding — both "combining" processes in a real sense (adding an
affix, joining two whole words) — are the two mechanisms a learner has
just secured through the prerequisite concepts, so the learner
reasonably extends "new words are formed by combining parts" as a
complete, general explanation, with nothing in that prior instruction
marking that conversion, blending, and clipping work by entirely
different, non-combining mechanisms until directly contrasted. The
hidden-change-search failure exists specifically because every OTHER
process the learner has encountered (derivation, compounding, blending,
clipping) leaves some visible trace on the word's form — a learner
reasonably expects this pattern to hold universally, so when conversion
presents a case with genuinely no visible change at all, the learner's
well-founded expectation that "word formation always changes the word
somehow" leads them to search for a change that simply does not exist,
rather than accepting the absence of change as the defining feature of
this specific process.

  knows genuinely do share a family resemblance (both involve adding or
  joining material), so the learner extends this real, shared pattern
  into a claim about ALL word formation, with nothing in prior
  instruction — which only covered these two processes — marking that
  conversion, blending, and clipping work by structurally different
  mechanisms.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, upon meeting a new
  word, spontaneously asks "wait, was this one combined, or did
  something else happen to it?" before assuming a combining process is
  showing the five-process discrimination has generalized beyond the
  specific TA-1 practice set.

**MC-CONVERSION-MUST-INVOLVE-SOME-HIDDEN-SUFFIX-OR-CHANGE (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: also a Type 1 signature, distinct
  mechanism — every other word-formation process the learner has
  encountered (derivation, compounding, and later blending/clipping)
  DOES leave a visible trace on the word's form, so the learner
  reasonably extends "word formation always changes the word somehow"
  into an expectation that conversion must too, with nothing marking
  that conversion's entire defining feature is the ABSENCE of such a
  change until directly, explicitly verified.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when asked to spell
  both forms of a suspected conversion pair, confidently confirms "yep,
  they're exactly the same" without searching further for a hidden
  difference is showing the zero-change model has been genuinely
  internalized, rather than reluctantly accepted only after repeated
  reassurance.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the Five Word Factories themselves are the analogy,
  not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): five distinct
  physical/drawn factory stations (Affix, Assembly, Mirror, Blender,
  Scissors), each producing a new word through a visibly different
  mechanism, directly embodies the five-process distinction — with the
  Mirror Factory's literal mirror demonstration (showing zero visible
  change, only a different "job badge") directly embodying conversion's
  genuinely non-combining nature. Breaking point: five separate,
  clean-cut factory stations can imply every word-formation case sorts
  unambiguously into exactly one station, which can understate that
  some words genuinely combine more than one process historically
  (e.g., clipping followed by derivation, as in "selfie" per TA-5) —
  this entry treats the factory framing as the introductory frame for
  clearly distinct individual processes, with TA-5 explicitly
  addressing multi-process cases as a more advanced pattern.
- **Alternative — kitchen preparation methods, not one generic "cooking"
  category**: just as "cooking" isn't one single technique but includes
  distinct methods (baking, frying, blending ingredients, trimming
  excess) that produce different results through different physical
  actions, word formation includes distinct techniques producing new
  words through different structural actions. Breaking point: useful
  for the general "not all one process" insight, but should not be
  extended to suggest the five word-formation processes have a natural,
  intuitive one-to-one mapping onto specific cooking methods — the
  Mirror Factory's zero-change case in particular has no clean cooking
  parallel.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe conversion as "the lazy way" of
  making a new word, implying it does less "real" work than derivation
  or compounding**: framing conversion as a lesser or incomplete process
  risks reinforcing the search for a "real," hidden change underneath
  it; conversion should be presented as a fully legitimate, equally
  systematic process, distinguished only by WHERE the change occurs
  (in grammatical function, not in form), not by how much "work" it
  does.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
five-process distinction and conversion's true zero-change nature are
both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured derivation/compounding, is asked
whether all four presented example words — happiness, toothbrush, to
email, brunch — were "made the same way"); **playground/invention**
(TA-1's ten-word sort across all five processes lets the learner notice
multiple distinct mechanisms); **collision** (the Blueprint's own
four-word comparison breaks the single-process assumption; the
google/to-google spelling check breaks the hidden-change assumption);
**formalization** (MP-5's original-word-construction task, requiring
explicit statement of which process was used and how); **compression**
(TA-5's fluent process-identification for genuinely unfamiliar words
without each process separately re-derived). The specific PROCESS NAMES
(derivation, compounding, conversion, blending, clipping) are
convention-bound terminology best introduced by direct instruction
alongside the Five Word Factories anchor, since there is no regularity
to discover in the labels themselves, only in the form-change patterns
they name.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (the five-process sort) is placed first as the concept's essential
orienting task, directly targeting MC-ALL-NEW-WORDS-ARE-FORMED-THE-
SAME-WAY by presenting all five processes together from the start,
before any single process is developed in depth — this prevents the
learner from anchoring on whichever process happens to be taught first
as "the" process. TA-2 (derivation and compounding deep dive) follows,
reviewing and extending the two already-familiar processes to build
confidence before the three genuinely new processes are introduced.
TA-3 (conversion, the zero-change process) is placed next, directly
targeting MC-CONVERSION-MUST-INVOLVE-SOME-HIDDEN-SUFFIX-OR-CHANGE — per
the Blueprint's own session architecture, this is the first genuinely
novel process introduced, specifically because it is the one most
likely to trigger the hidden-change-search failure, and addressing it
early (before blending/clipping) means the learner's "every process
changes the word somehow" expectation is challenged while still fresh
from TA-2's confirming examples. TA-4 (blending and clipping) follows,
introducing the remaining two processes together since both genuinely
involve material loss (blending fuses and drops material from two
words; clipping simply shortens one word) and are naturally
contrasted against each other. TA-5 (identifying the process in novel
words) is placed last as the concept's full analytical capstone,
requiring the learner to apply all five-process discrimination to
genuinely unfamiliar or recently-coined words, including multi-process
cases (like "selfie," combining clipping and derivation). This entry
does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script
(Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously names the
  SPECIFIC process (not merely "combining" or "some kind of change")
  when meeting a fresh new word — this is the primary diagnostic signal
  for whether the five-process discrimination has genuinely replaced
  the single-process default as the learner's operative model.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before confirming a
  suspected conversion pair, followed by an explicit side-by-side
  spelling/pronunciation check, suggests the learner is consciously
  verifying rather than assuming — a good sign, distinct from the
  concerning pattern of continued searching for a hidden difference
  even after the check confirms identity. Instant, confident acceptance
  of genuine zero-change after a quick verification is the target
  pattern, not a red flag.
- **Novel-word stakes**: real and central to TA-5/MP-4 — this concept's
  practical payoff is specifically the ability to analyze recently-
  coined vocabulary (tech terms, slang, brand-derived verbs) the learner
  has never been explicitly taught, so the tutor should draw genuinely
  current examples where possible to build the transfer this concept is
  designed to enable.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "what actually happened to this word's
  form?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for MC-ALL-NEW-WORDS-
  ARE-FORMED-THE-SAME-WAY, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh new word is introduced, making explicit form-change
  analysis a routinely-invited response rather than a default
  combining-process assumption.
