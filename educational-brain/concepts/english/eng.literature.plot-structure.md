## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify a story's five plot stages (exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, resolution) by narrative function, not length or intensity.
2. Apply the turning-point test to correctly identify the true climax even when a different scene is more visually exciting.
3. Confirm that a story with dramatically uneven stage proportions is still a valid, well-structured plot.
4. Map a new story's events onto the five-stage structure with justified boundaries.
5. Construct an original five-stage plot outline with deliberately varied stage proportions.

## Core Understanding

This concept extends `eng.literature.narrative-elements`' basic plot-chain identification into a structured five-stage arc. Two corrections define it. First, **the climax is not defined by being the most exciting or action-packed moment — it is the specific structural turning point where the central conflict's outcome is decided**, which may or may not be the loudest scene; a quiet, tense decision can be the true climax while a louder chase scene earlier in the story is merely rising-action complication. Second, **not every story divides its length equally among the five plot stages** — real stories almost never do, with rising action very often the longest stage and resolution frequently quite brief; plot structure describes the ORDER and FUNCTION of the stages, not their proportional length, and uneven proportions are the norm, not an exception. Both corrections replace a surface heuristic (excitement level; expected equal length) with the functional analysis this program's literature-domain concepts consistently require.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "the climax is just the most exciting or action-packed moment; every story follows the five stages in roughly equal proportion."**
The simulation: locate the climax by scanning for the loudest, most dramatic scene, and expect (or force) an even division of story length across the five stages.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a loud chase scene in the rising action followed by a quiet, decisive conversation, asked which actually decides the central conflict's outcome; and a story with 80% rising action and a one-paragraph resolution, asked whether this violates plot structure.
*Shelf-life warning*: "The climax is the turning point, not the loudest scene. Stages are rarely equal in length — that's normal."

**Intermediate — "I look for the turning point now, but I still expect stages to be roughly balanced and get confused when one is much longer or shorter than another."**
The simulation: correctly applies the turning-point test for climax but hasn't yet fully accepted that uneven proportions are the normal case.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct comparison of two stories with dramatically different, but both valid, stage-length proportions.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Identify stages by what they DO, not how long they last. Uneven length is expected, not a flaw."

**Advanced — "I identify all five stages by function, correctly locate the true climax even when misdirected by excitement, confirm uneven proportions as valid, and construct an original plot outline with deliberately varied stage lengths."**
The simulation: integrates functional stage identification, turning-point analysis, and deliberate proportion choice into one coherent original composition. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the four direct downstream cross-links, each building on this concept's stage-function map for a more specialized literary analysis.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the structural map. Theme, foreshadowing, dramatic structure, and prose fiction each build on this map in a different direction."

**Expert — "at fluency, functional stage identification and turning-point recognition happen as an integrated part of ordinary literary reading."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit function-labeling and turning-point-test routine is a scaffold for a reading habit that, at true fluency, operates as an integrated part of literary comprehension.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **excitement and intensity are the most perceptually salient features of a scene**, while identifying a "turning point" requires tracking the central conflict's actual trajectory across the whole story, a subtler and more effortful analytical task that a loud, dramatic scene can distract from.

The second mechanism is that **the five-stage model is frequently taught with a simplified, symmetrical diagram** (evenly-spaced labels along a rising-then-falling line), and without explicit correction, learners assume the diagram's visual symmetry implies proportional equality in the actual story's length, when the diagram is meant to show only order and function.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-THE-CLIMAX-IS-JUST-THE-MOST-EXCITING-OR-ACTION-PACKED-MOMENT
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry — the loud-chase-scene/quiet-decisive-conversation discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from excitement and action being the most perceptually salient scene features, mistaken for the actual structural criterion (deciding the conflict's outcome).
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **use a story where the misleadingly exciting scene and the true climax are DIFFERENT scenes**, since the fix requires the learner to directly experience the turning-point test overriding their salience-based first impression.
- **Verification of death**: given such a story, the learner correctly identifies the true climax using the turning-point test.

### MC-EVERY-STORY-FOLLOWS-THE-FIVE-STAGES-IN-EQUAL-PROPORTION
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — the 80%-rising-action/brief-resolution discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 4 (notation-induced), from the five-stage diagram's typical visually symmetrical or evenly-spaced representation being mistaken for a claim about the story's actual proportional length, when the diagram only represents order and function.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **explicitly stretching the visual model itself** (the Mountain Path anchor's unevenly-stretched segments), since the misconception originates in a visual representation and is most directly corrected by revising that same visual representation.
- **Verification of death**: given a story with dramatically uneven stage-length proportions, the learner confirms it is a valid, well-structured plot and identifies each stage by function.

## Analogies

**Best — the Mountain Path** (Blueprint Component 3): five labeled segments (gentle trail, steepening climb, sharp peak, descending path, flat resting area), physically stretched so the climb is much longer than the peak or resting area, directly seeding the uneven-proportion fix; the peak marked as "the turning point where direction changes," sometimes a quiet foggy moment rather than a storm, directly seeding the climax-as-turning-point fix.
*Breaking point*: a mountain path's physical length is a fixed, measurable geographic fact, while a story's "stage length" is a more interpretive matter of how much narrative attention or word count is devoted to each phase — useful for the uneven-segments intuition, less precise for implying stage boundaries are as objectively measurable as trail distance.

**Alternative — a chess game's opening, middlegame, and endgame.** A chess game's middlegame (rising action) is often by far the longest phase, while the decisive move (climax) might be a single quiet, quick decision rather than a flashy combination — and the game's true turning point isn't necessarily its most visually complex-looking position.
*Breaking point*: chess has an objectively determinable "decisive move" verifiable through analysis engines, while a story's climax often remains a matter of reasonable literary interpretation.

**Visual anchor — the Mountain Path**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"The climax is the biggest, most exciting scene in the story."** Directly installs the excitement-as-climax misconception as a stated definition.
- **"A well-structured story divides its length evenly among the five stages."** Directly installs the equal-proportion misconception as a stated rule.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Mountain Path (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Should the climbing segment be the same length as the peak?" Then stretch the model unevenly and mark the peak as the turning point.
2. **The loud-chase/quiet-decision collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Which scene actually decides the central conflict's outcome?"
3. **The 80%-rising-action/brief-resolution collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Does this violate plot structure since the stages aren't equal length?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both structural facts are directly checkable by testing candidate scenes and stage-length proportions against the story's own conflict trajectory.

1. **Need**: "If a story has one really loud, exciting scene, is that automatically the climax?"
2. **Playground**: the learner labels several story events by function freely before being told the official five-stage names.
3. **Invention**: "Can you find the exact moment where this story's central conflict gets decided, even if it's not the loudest moment?"
4. **Collision**: present a story with 80% rising action and a one-paragraph resolution; ask whether this is still well-structured.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — climax is the turning point, not the excitement peak; stage proportions vary widely and normally — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Find the turning point, not the loudest scene — and expect uneven stages."

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for why a given moment is or isn't the climax, and why an uneven-length story is still well-structured**, since a correct-looking stage label with no stated functional or turning-point reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated turning-point check** ("does this scene decide the conflict's outcome, or does it just add tension?") applied to a new, potentially misleading scene — the direct behavioral signature of the climax fix taking hold.
- **Confident acceptance of a story with dramatically uneven stage lengths as well-structured**, without expecting or forcing balance — direct evidence the proportion fix is internalized.
- **A stage label assigned purely by scene length or visual "size" in the story**, rather than by function — the direct behavioral signature of MC-EVERY-STORY-FOLLOWS-THE-FIVE-STAGES-IN-EQUAL-PROPORTION still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Find the turning point, not the loudest scene — and expect the stages to be uneven, not equal."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But this scene has all the action, isn't that the climax?"** — take this as a reasonable inference from perceptual salience; respond by applying the turning-point test: "Let's check — does this scene actually decide how the central conflict turns out, or does something else settle it?"
- **"This story doesn't seem balanced, is something wrong with it?"** — a genuine, reasonable expectation given how the five-stage diagram is often presented; respond by naming the corrected expectation: "Real stories are almost never evenly divided — a long rising action and a short resolution is completely normal. What matters is that each stage does its job."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full original plot outline down to **applying the turning-point test to a single pair of candidate scenes (one exciting, one quiet-but-decisive), with no proportion or full-mapping task demanded.** Isolates the most basic climax test before returning to fuller structural analysis.
- **Never shrink to "just point to the most dramatic part of the story."** This directly licenses the excitement-as-climax pattern, undermining the turning-point discipline this concept specifically builds.
