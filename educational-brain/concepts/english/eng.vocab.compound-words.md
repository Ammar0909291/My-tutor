# Compound Words — `eng.vocab.compound-words`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.compound-words` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Compound Words" — words
  formed by combining two or more independent words into a single new
  word, taught with explicit attention to the transparent/opaque meaning
  spectrum and the three genuine written forms (closed, hyphenated,
  open).
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.word-families` — load-bearing because
  that concept's core distinction (shared root MEANING as the real test
  of relatedness, not surface appearance) is the exact skill this
  concept applies in reverse: recognizing when two whole, independently-
  meaningful words combine into a single new meaning, sometimes
  predictable from the parts and sometimes not, mirrors the word-family
  concept's meaning-over-appearance discipline.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.vocab.word-formation-processes`. Compound
  words are one specific word-formation mechanism (combining two whole
  words); a broader treatment of how English forms new words (including
  affixation, blending, clipping, and others) depends on the learner
  already having a clear, concrete example of one such mechanism in
  hand.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 1

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify closed compound words (notebook, sunflower, doghouse) and
   their two component words.
2. Distinguish TRANSPARENT compound meanings (predictable from the
   parts) from OPAQUE compound meanings (drifted, non-literal), rather
   than assuming every compound's meaning is a literal sum of its parts.
3. Recognize hyphenated (well-known) and open (ice cream) compounds as
   genuine compound words, despite not being written as a single
   unbroken word.
4. Generate and classify compound words by both written form (closed/
   hyphenated/open) and meaning type (transparent/opaque).
5. Apply a unified-meaning test — not spelling convention — as the true
   defining criterion for whether a word combination counts as a
   compound.

## Core Understanding

A compound word forms when two or more independent words combine into a
single new word or unified concept — but this concept's genuine
teaching challenge lies in two dimensions where the surface form
misleads. First, a compound's meaning is not always the literal,
predictable sum of its parts: some compounds are TRANSPARENT (doghouse =
a house for a dog; sunflower = a flower associated with the sun), where
the meaning follows logically from combining the parts, while others
are OPAQUE (butterfly is not literally about butter or bird-like flying;
understand is not literally about standing under something) — their
meaning has drifted over the history of the language into its own
distinct sense that must be learned, not derived. Second, compounds are
not always written as a single unbroken word: English genuinely uses
THREE written conventions — closed (notebook), hyphenated (well-known,
mother-in-law), and open (ice cream, high school) — and all three are
equally legitimate compounds, with the specific convention for a given
compound often simply a matter of established, sometimes shifting,
orthographic habit (as "e-mail" became "email" over time). The real,
reliable test for whether a word combination counts as a compound is
whether it expresses ONE unified meaning or concept, regardless of
spacing or hyphenation, and regardless of whether that unified meaning
happens to be transparently derivable or opaquely drifted.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "a compound word's meaning is always the literal
  sum of its two parts, and it's always written as one unbroken word"**:
  the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct —
  a natural extrapolation from the most commonly taught, transparent,
  closed compounds (doghouse, sunflower) that early vocabulary
  instruction typically favors, giving no signal that opaque meanings
  or alternative written forms exist until directly presented.
- **Intermediate model — "I can identify closed compounds and confirm
  their two component words"**: installed by TA-1's identification
  practice, using the Component 3 puzzle-pieces anchor to make the
  combination process visually and physically concrete. Upgrade
  trigger: correctly naming both component words of a fresh closed
  compound without hesitation.
- **Advanced model — "some compounds are transparent (predictable
  meaning) and some are opaque (drifted, non-literal meaning), and I
  check which type I'm dealing with before assuming"**: installed by
  TA-2's transparent-vs-opaque sorting task, resolving MC-COMPOUND-
  MEANING-IS-ALWAYS-LITERAL-SUM through the felt surprise of
  "butterfly" not being about butter at all. Upgrade trigger:
  spontaneously questioning whether a fresh compound's meaning is
  actually predictable before assuming it is.
- **Expert model — "compounds can be written closed, hyphenated, or
  open, and the real test is unified meaning, not spelling — I can
  generate and classify compounds along both dimensions"**: installed
  by TA-3's hyphenated/open recognition task (resolving MC-COMPOUND-
  WORDS-ARE-ALWAYS-WRITTEN-AS-ONE-WORD) and TA-4's generation-and-
  classification task, requiring both dimensions (form and meaning
  type) to be applied together productively. This is the concept's
  genuine mastery bar — treating spelling convention as incidental,
  never as the defining test of "compound."
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags,
  for S9 learners whose L1 either lacks compounding entirely or uses a
  different open/closed/hyphenated convention system, English's
  specific written-form variability (and its documented inconsistency,
  e.g. "email" evolving from "e-mail") is a genuinely English-specific
  orthographic feature warranting dedicated practice — the tutor should
  not assume this dimension transfers readily from the learner's
  existing language intuitions the way the underlying combination
  concept itself might.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of how compound words are typically first encountered. The
literal-sum-assumption failure exists because the compounds most
commonly used in early vocabulary teaching (doghouse, sunflower,
toothbrush) genuinely ARE transparent, so a learner reasonably
generalizes "compound meaning = combine the parts logically" from this
consistently transparent early exposure, with nothing marking that
opaque compounds — common, everyday words like "butterfly,"
"understand," and "breakfast" — exist until specifically contrasted.
The one-word-spelling-assumption failure exists because the very term
"compound WORD" implies a single word, and the most salient, easily
noticed compounds are indeed written as one unbroken word (closed
compounds are visually the most obviously "compound-looking"), so a
learner reasonably infers that spacing itself is the defining test,
with nothing in ordinary exposure signaling that "ice cream" or
"well-known" are functioning identically as unified concepts despite
their different written form.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.vocab.compound-words.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-COMPOUND-MEANING-IS-ALWAYS-LITERAL-SUM (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — literal-sum
  meaning genuinely holds for the transparent compounds most commonly
  used in early instruction (doghouse, sunflower), so the learner
  extends this reliable pattern into an unconditional rule, with
  nothing in that instruction marking the many common opaque exceptions
  (butterfly, understand, breakfast) until directly tested.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, upon meeting a new
  compound, spontaneously asks "wait, does that actually mean what the
  two parts suggest, or has it drifted?" before being prompted to check
  is showing the transparent/opaque discipline has generalized beyond
  the specific TA-2 practice pairs.

**MC-COMPOUND-WORDS-ARE-ALWAYS-WRITTEN-AS-ONE-WORD (Blueprint Component
1) — Type 3, language contamination**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a Type 3 signature — the ordinary,
  correctly-functioning convention that separate spacing signals
  separate words (true for the vast majority of English text) intrudes
  into this specific domain, where "ice cream" and "high school"
  genuinely function as single conceptual units despite following the
  ordinary spacing convention of two separate words; the learner is not
  misapplying a wrong rule about compounds specifically, but rather
  correctly applying a different, unrelated convention (spacing signals
  word boundaries) into a domain where it doesn't determine compound
  status.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown "ice
  cream," says something like "it looks like two words, but it really
  means just one thing" is showing the unified-meaning test has
  replaced the spacing test as the operative criterion.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the compound-word puzzle pieces themselves are the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): two word-
  part puzzle pieces physically snapping together into "sunflower"
  directly embodies transparent combination; the same snapping action
  producing "butterfly" — with its meaning revealed as unrelated to
  butter — directly embodies the opaque contrast; laying three written
  forms side by side embodies the closed/hyphenated/open equivalence.
  Breaking point: a physical snap-together implies the combination
  process itself is uniform across all compounds, which can understate
  that opaque compounds' meaning drift happened historically, over time,
  not through any special "wrong" combination — this entry treats the
  puzzle-pieces anchor as the introductory frame for both key contrasts,
  not a claim about historical word-formation mechanics.
- **Alternative — a nickname that outgrew its literal origin**: some
  nicknames make perfect literal sense (a tall person nicknamed
  "Stretch"), while others have drifted so far from their literal
  origin that the connection is no longer obvious (a nickname whose
  origin story is long forgotten, yet the name still functions
  perfectly as that person's identity). Breaking point: useful for the
  transparent/opaque contrast specifically, but should not be extended
  to the written-form dimension — nicknames don't have a parallel
  closed/hyphenated/open spelling-convention question.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe open compounds ("ice cream," "high
  school") as "not real" compound words or as merely "two words that
  happen to go together"**: this framing directly reinforces
  MC-COMPOUND-WORDS-ARE-ALWAYS-WRITTEN-AS-ONE-WORD by implying only
  closed forms genuinely count; open compounds should always be
  presented as fully legitimate compounds from the first time they are
  introduced, never as a lesser or borderline category.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (compound-word puzzle pieces)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: word cards, or simply spoken word-pairs, can
  substitute for physical puzzle pieces; any grocery list or menu
  supplies real open-compound examples (ice cream, French fries).
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's generation-and-classification
  task is hands-on, requiring the learner to actively produce compounds
  from given word pairs and sort each along both dimensions (form and
  meaning type).
- **Teacher-demo (transparent/opaque contrast)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether
  "butterfly" has anything to do with butter — making the opaque
  category directly and memorably visible.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  transparent/opaque classification (TA-2) or a hyphenated/open
  compound's legitimacy (TA-3), ask the learner to predict or judge
  before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
transparent/opaque contrast and the three-written-form equivalence are
both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured word-family knowledge, is asked
whether every compound word's meaning is exactly what its parts
suggest); **playground/invention** (TA-1's closed-compound
identification across several transparent examples lets the learner
build initial confidence in the literal-sum pattern); **collision** (the
Blueprint's own "does butterfly relate to butter?" probe breaks the
literal-sum assumption directly; the "is ice cream a real compound?"
probe breaks the one-word-spelling assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's
own-words statement of both the transparent/opaque distinction and the
three written forms); **compression** (TA-4's fluent generation and
dual classification without needing each dimension separately
re-derived). The specific LABELS "transparent," "opaque," "closed,"
"hyphenated," and "open" are convention-bound terminology best
introduced by direct instruction once the learner has already felt the
underlying distinctions through the anchor and TA-1–2.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (identifying closed compound words) is placed first as the
concept's most basic, familiar entry point — closed, transparent
compounds are the most visually obvious and intuitively satisfying
category, giving the learner a secure foundation before either
complication is introduced. TA-2 (transparent vs. opaque compound
meaning) follows immediately, directly targeting MC-COMPOUND-MEANING-
IS-ALWAYS-LITERAL-SUM — per the Blueprint's own session architecture,
this conflict-evidence probe comes right after TA-1, once the learner
already has confident transparent examples to contrast an opaque one
against. TA-3 (hyphenated and open compounds) is placed next, addressing
the SECOND, independent dimension (written form) only after the meaning
dimension (TA-1/TA-2) is already secure — introducing spelling-form
variability simultaneously with meaning-type variability would risk
conflating two genuinely separate axes of variation. TA-4 (generating
and classifying compound words) is placed last as the concept's
integration and productive-application capstone, requiring the learner
to apply BOTH dimensions (form and meaning type) together on
self-generated compounds, demonstrating that the two axes are
independent and can be freely combined (an opaque hyphenated compound is
just as possible as a transparent closed one). This entry does not
restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) —
see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously checks a
  fresh compound's meaning against its literal parts before assuming
  it's transparent — this is the primary diagnostic signal for whether
  TA-2's transparent/opaque discipline has genuinely generalized beyond
  the specific practice examples.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before classifying an
  unfamiliar opaque-sounding compound, followed by a correct "this one
  seems to have drifted" judgment, suggests the learner is consciously
  checking rather than reflexively assuming transparency — a good sign.
  Instant, confident literal-sum explanations for a genuinely opaque
  compound (with no hesitation or self-correction) is the concerning
  pattern for MC-COMPOUND-MEANING-IS-ALWAYS-LITERAL-SUM.
- **Written-form stakes**: lower emotional stakes than in more
  affectively loaded concepts, but real practical stakes — a learner who
  confidently declares "ice cream" is "not really a compound word"
  because it's two words is showing MC-COMPOUND-WORDS-ARE-ALWAYS-
  WRITTEN-AS-ONE-WORD actively, and this should be gently but directly
  corrected using the unified-meaning test.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does this actually mean what the parts
  suggest, or has it drifted?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence
  for MC-COMPOUND-MEANING-IS-ALWAYS-LITERAL-SUM, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh compound is introduced, making the transparent/opaque
  check a routinely-invited response rather than an afterthought.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework — a two-dimensional classification
framework (meaning type: transparent/opaque; written form: closed/
hyphenated/open) applied to genuinely novel compound words, combined
with a productive generation skill (TA-4); mastery is demonstrated by
correctly classifying unfamiliar compounds along both independent
dimensions (MP-2/MP-3/MP-4) rather than by increasing fluency at a fixed
task. Interleaving partners: mix meaning-type classification items
(testing TA-1/TA-2) with written-form classification items (testing
TA-3) in ongoing review, so the learner continues treating the two
dimensions as genuinely independent rather than collapsing them into a
single judgment.
