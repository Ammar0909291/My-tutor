# Verbs — `eng.grammar.verbs`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.verbs` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Verbs" — words
  expressing action or state of being, including main verbs and
  auxiliary (helping) verbs.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.word-classes-overview` — load-bearing
  because identifying verbs as one of the eight word classes, and
  reliably using function-based (not fixed-label) reasoning, is exactly
  the skill this concept extends into verb-specific subcategorization.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.adverbs`,
  `eng.grammar.subject-and-predicate`,
  `eng.grammar.present-tenses`, `eng.grammar.subject-verb-agreement`.
  A secure understanding of verbs — especially the full verb-phrase
  concept including auxiliaries — is the direct foundation for adverb
  modification targets, predicate identification, tense formation, and
  subject-verb agreement.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.80 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify physical-action verbs in sentences.
2. Identify linking/state verbs (is, seem, become, feel) as legitimate
   verbs despite the absence of physical movement.
3. Identify mental/sensory-process verbs (think, know, believe, want)
   as legitimate verbs.
4. Identify complete verb phrases — helping (auxiliary) verbs combined
   with a main verb — rather than only the main action word.
5. Test whether a word group is a complete sentence by checking for a
   genuine, complete verb (including its helping-verb partner where
   needed), and correct incomplete "sentences" missing a necessary verb
   component.

## Core Understanding

Verbs express action OR state of being — "is," "am," "are," "seem,"
"feel," "become" are all verbs even though they don't show physical
movement; they connect a subject to a description or state (linking
verbs), or express mental/emotional states (think, want, believe,
love) rather than physical action, and every one of these does the
grammatical JOB of a verb — essential to forming a complete sentence.
Checking whether a word expresses action OR a state/condition/mental
process — not just visible physical movement — is the reliable test. A
second, equally load-bearing fact is that helping (auxiliary) verbs —
is, am, are, was, were, has, have, had, will, would, can, could, must,
do, does, did — combine with a main verb to form the complete VERB
PHRASE, carrying essential information about tense, ongoing action,
ability, obligation, or question formation. They are genuine verbs, not
optional extras: "She is running" needs "is" as much as it needs
"running" — removing the helping verb ("She running") breaks the
sentence's grammar entirely. Always checking for helping verbs
alongside the main verb, and treating the full verb phrase as the
complete verb, is the reliable practice.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "verbs are words that show physical movement"**:
  the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct
  — a natural default since physical-action verbs (run, jump, throw)
  are the most vivid, easily demonstrated examples, giving no built-in
  signal that state-of-being and mental-process verbs also function as
  verbs until directly tested.
- **Intermediate model — "I can identify physical-action verbs, and I
  recognize linking/state verbs as legitimate verbs too"**: installed
  by TA-1's physical-action-verb practice and TA-2's linking/state-verb
  practice, using the Component 3 verb-charades anchor (a "no
  movement" round following the action round) to make the expansion
  concrete, resolving MC-VERBS-ARE-ONLY-PHYSICAL-ACTIONS. Upgrade
  trigger: correctly identifying a fresh linking verb (like "seems")
  without hesitation, citing its grammatical job (connecting subject
  to description) rather than needing reassurance.
- **Advanced model — "I can also identify mental/sensory-process
  verbs"**: installed by TA-3's practice, completing the expansion
  beyond physical action into a third verb subcategory (think, know,
  believe, want).
- **Expert model — "I identify the COMPLETE verb phrase, including
  helping verbs, and I use the verb to test sentence completeness"**:
  installed by TA-4's helping-verb-and-verb-phrase practice (resolving
  MC-HELPING-VERBS-ARE-NOT-REAL-VERBS) and TA-5's sentence-completeness
  testing. This is the concept's genuine mastery bar — treating the
  full verb phrase, not just the main action word, as the complete
  grammatical unit.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 learners whose L1 doesn't use a copula/linking verb the way
  English does (some languages omit "be" in present-tense statements),
  or has a different auxiliary-verb system, TA-2/TA-4 should receive
  dedicated, explicit contrastive teaching — omitting "is/are" or
  misusing helping verbs is a very common, persistent transfer error
  for learners from copula-drop languages, warranting sustained
  practice rather than assumed quick mastery.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of how verbs are typically first encountered. The physical-
action-only failure exists because physical-action verbs genuinely ARE
the most vivid, most easily demonstrated (and often first-taught)
examples — a learner reasonably extends "verbs show physical movement"
from this consistently reinforced early pattern, with nothing marking
the state/mental-verb exception until directly tested against a
sentence like "She IS happy." The helping-verbs-don't-count failure
exists because the main verb genuinely carries the most salient,
content-rich meaning in a sentence (running, finished, going) — a
learner's attention naturally focuses there, with the smaller,
grammatically-functional helping verb receiving less perceptual
weight, so nothing in ordinary sentence processing marks the helping
verb as equally essential until the sentence is tested for completeness
without it.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.verbs.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions this
concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-VERBS-ARE-ONLY-PHYSICAL-ACTIONS (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — physical-
  action verbs genuinely ARE the most common, vivid, and typically
  first-taught verb examples a learner meets, so the learner extends
  "verbs show movement" from this consistently reinforced pattern,
  with nothing in that early exposure marking the state/mental-verb
  exception until directly tested against a sentence with no physical
  movement at all.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, upon meeting a
  state or mental verb, spontaneously says something like "it's still
  doing a verb's job, even though nothing's moving" before being told
  is showing the job-not-movement model has generalized beyond the
  specific TA-2/TA-3 practice sentences.

**MC-HELPING-VERBS-ARE-NOT-REAL-VERBS (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 2,
perceptual intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a genuine perceptual default rather than
  a wrongly-taught rule — the main verb genuinely carries the most
  salient, content-rich meaning in ordinary sentence processing, so a
  learner's attention naturally settles there, with the smaller,
  grammatically-functional helping verb receiving less perceptual
  weight; the learner isn't applying a false belief, just following the
  natural attentional focus toward content over function words.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when asked to
  identify a verb, spontaneously scans for BOTH a helping verb and a
  main verb before answering, rather than stopping at the first
  content-rich word found, is showing the complete-verb-phrase
  discipline has generalized beyond the specific TA-4 practice
  sentences.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the verb charades anchor itself is the analogy, not
  a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): an action round (acting
  out physical verbs) followed explicitly by a "no movement" round
  (identifying state/mental verbs' grammatical job without any
  physical acting-out possible) directly embodies the expansion from
  physical-action to the broader verb category. Breaking point: a
  charades game inherently favors physically demonstrable verbs, which
  can make the "no movement" round feel like a lesser, add-on activity
  rather than an equally central part of the category — this entry
  treats the two-round structure as the introductory frame for the
  expansion, with explicit framing (per the Blueprint's own Adaptive
  Flags) that state/mental verbs are equally central, not exceptions.
- **Alternative — a car's engine (main verb) and its ignition system
  (helping verb)**: the engine does the visible, powerful work, but the
  car cannot run at all without the ignition system enabling it — both
  are essential, working together as one functional unit. Breaking
  point: useful for the essential-partnership insight (MC-HELPING-
  VERBS-ARE-NOT-REAL-VERBS's fix), but doesn't address the physical-
  action-only misconception the way the charades anchor's two-round
  structure does.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe helping verbs as "small" or "minor"
  words even while explaining their function**: framing helping verbs
  with diminishing language, even while correcting the misconception,
  risks reinforcing the very perceptual bias (main verb = important,
  helping verb = extra) this concept is built to overcome; helping
  verbs should be described as carrying essential grammatical weight,
  not as smaller or less significant than the main verb.

- **Teacher-demo (state-verb and helping-verb contrasts)**: the tutor

- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
job-not-movement principle and the complete-verb-phrase principle are
both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured physical-action verbs, is asked
whether anything moves in "She is happy"); **playground/invention**
(TA-1's charades round builds initial confidence with the action-verb
category); **collision** (the Blueprint's own "is" probe breaks the
physical-action-only assumption; the "She running" incompleteness probe
breaks the helping-verbs-don't-count assumption); **formalization**
(MP-5's own-words statement of both the action-or-state principle and
the complete-verb-phrase principle); **compression** (TA-5's fluent
sentence-completeness testing without each verb-phrase component
separately re-derived). The specific terms "linking verb," "auxiliary
verb," and "verb phrase" are convention-bound terminology best
introduced by direct instruction once the learner has already felt the
underlying functional distinctions through the anchor and TA-1–2.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (physical action verbs) is placed first as the concept's most
intuitive, easily demonstrated entry point, per the Blueprint's own
charades-based anchor. TA-2 (linking/state verbs) follows immediately,
directly targeting MC-VERBS-ARE-ONLY-PHYSICAL-ACTIONS — per the
Blueprint's own session architecture, this conflict-evidence probe is
placed right after TA-2, once the learner has secure action-verb
practice to contrast against. TA-3 (mental/sensory verbs) extends the
non-physical-verb category further, completing the three-part
expansion (physical action → state/being → mental process). TA-4
(helping verbs and verb phrases) is placed next, directly targeting
MC-HELPING-VERBS-ARE-NOT-REAL-VERBS, addressing the SECOND, independent
dimension (verb-phrase completeness, not verb-type category) once the
learner already has a secure sense of what a verb is across its three
functional categories. TA-5 (testing sentence completeness via the
verb) is placed last as the concept's practical-payoff capstone,
applying the full verb-phrase concept to the genuinely useful task of
checking whether a word group is grammatically complete. This entry
does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script
(Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously scans for
  BOTH a helping verb and a main verb when identifying a sentence's
  verb (rather than stopping at the first content-rich word found) —
  this is the primary diagnostic signal for whether TA-4's complete-
  verb-phrase discipline has genuinely generalized beyond the specific
  practice sentences.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before confirming a
  state or mental verb, followed by a correct "yes, it's still doing a
  verb's job" judgment, suggests the learner is consciously applying
  the job-not-movement test rather than defaulting to a physical-
  action check — a good sign. Instant denial that "is" or "seems" could
  be "real" verbs is the concerning pattern for MC-VERBS-ARE-ONLY-
  PHYSICAL-ACTIONS.
- **Copula-drop stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags — for S9 learners from L1s that omit
  "be" in certain constructions or handle auxiliaries differently,
  budget explicit, repeated practice on the specific English auxiliary
  patterns that are commonly dropped or misapplied, since this is a
  well-documented, persistent transfer-error source.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "would that be a complete sentence
  without the helping verb?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence
  for MC-HELPING-VERBS-ARE-NOT-REAL-VERBS, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh helping-verb-plus-main-verb combination is
  introduced, making the completeness check a routinely-invited
  response rather than an afterthought.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework — a multi-category verb classification
system (physical action / linking-state / mental-sensory) combined
with a complete-verb-phrase identification procedure (checking for
helping verbs alongside the main verb), applied to genuinely novel
sentences, with a productive sentence-completeness-testing application
(TA-5); mastery is demonstrated by correctly classifying unfamiliar
verbs across all three categories and identifying complete verb
phrases (MP-2/MP-3/MP-4) rather than by recognizing a fixed,
previously-taught example set. Interleaving partners: mix single-
category identification items (testing TA-1/TA-2/TA-3 individually)
with complete-verb-phrase and sentence-completeness items (testing
TA-4/TA-5 together) in ongoing review, so the learner continues
exercising both categorical recognition and the harder verb-phrase-
completeness judgment as distinct, separately-verified competencies.

  verbs.

  `eng.grammar.present-tenses` (tense formation building directly on
