# Literal Comprehension — `eng.reading.literal-comprehension`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.reading.literal-comprehension` (canonical
  English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / reading / "Literal Comprehension"
  — understanding information that is explicitly stated in a text.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.reading.reading-fluency` — load-bearing
  because fluent reading (accuracy, appropriate rate, expression
  reflecting meaning) frees the attentional capacity this concept
  requires for the deeper analytical work of extracting, paraphrasing,
  and precisely integrating explicit facts — a learner still expending
  effort on basic fluency has no capacity left to check qualifying
  words or resolve pronoun references with the precision this concept
  demands.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.reading.main-idea-and-details`.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Accurately restate explicitly stated facts (who, what, when, where)
   in their own words, without requiring or attempting verbatim
   memorization of the original text.
2. Paraphrase sentences while deliberately preserving all key explicit
   facts (names, numbers, outcomes) rather than losing or altering them
   during restatement.
3. Correctly integrate qualifying words ("only," "most," "all,"
   "never") into their understanding of a statement's precise scope,
   rather than assuming word-level recognition guarantees correct
   sentence-level meaning.
4. Correctly resolve pronoun references for literal accuracy,
   identifying who or what a pronoun refers to based on the text.
5. Answer direct, explicit "who/what/when/where" questions accurately
   from a text, demonstrating genuine literal comprehension of stated
   information.

## Core Understanding

Literal comprehension means accurately understanding information that
is EXPLICITLY STATED in a text — it does NOT mean memorizing or
reproducing the text's exact original wording. A learner who can
restate "The volcano erupted in 1883, killing thousands" as "A volcano
exploded in 1883 and many people died" has demonstrated genuine literal
comprehension, because the key explicit facts (what happened, when,
outcome) are accurately preserved, even though the wording has
changed entirely. A second, independent insight concerns what
"understanding" a sentence actually requires: knowing every individual
word's meaning is NECESSARY but not SUFFICIENT for literal
comprehension. A learner can know every word in "Only some of the
students passed the exam" and still misread it as "all students
passed" if they fail to INTEGRATE the qualifying word "only some" into
the sentence's actual scope. Literal comprehension specifically checks
this integration step — correctly combining word-level meanings into
an accurate understanding of WHO did WHAT, WHEN, and with what
qualifying scope (only some vs. most vs. all vs. none) — not merely
whether the individual words were recognized. Pronoun resolution (who
or what a "she," "it," or "they" refers to) is a further, specific
integration checkpoint of the same kind.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "understanding the text means remembering its
  exact words; if I can't recite it, I didn't really understand it"**:
  the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct
  for MC-A — often reinforced by school contexts that reward verbatim
  quoting, producing anxiety about needing perfect recall rather than
  confidence in accurate own-words restatement.
- **Intermediate model — "I demonstrate understanding by accurately
  restating explicit facts in my own words, preserving key details like
  names and numbers"**: installed by TA-1 (fact extraction) and TA-2
  (paraphrasing without losing details) using the Fact-Extraction
  Worksheet anchor, resolving MC-LITERAL-COMPREHENSION-MEANS-
  MEMORIZING-EXACT-WORDING. Upgrade trigger: spontaneously paraphrasing
  a stated fact in fresh wording without anxiety about exact recall.
- **Advanced model — "knowing every word doesn't automatically mean I
  correctly understood the whole sentence — I check qualifying words
  and pronoun references explicitly"**: installed by TA-3 (qualifying-
  word integration) and TA-4 (pronoun reference resolution), resolving
  MC-IF-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-WORDS-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-TEXT.
- **Expert model — "I accurately answer direct factual questions from a
  text by combining fact extraction, detail preservation, qualifier
  integration, and pronoun resolution together"**: installed by TA-5
  (answering direct who/what/when/where questions), the concept's
  genuine mastery bar — demonstrating that the full literal-
  comprehension pipeline operates together, fluently, on questions
  rather than isolated restatement tasks alone.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing
  (S1), a learner who already comprehends texts well overall but has
  not practiced EXPLICIT fact-restatement in their own words should
  still receive TA-1/TA-2's practice — general comprehension ability
  does not automatically transfer into the specific, checkable skill of
  producing accurate, own-words fact statements.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, each traceable to a
distinct, plausible root. The verbatim-memorization failure (MC-A)
exists because school contexts frequently do reward exact quoting
(direct-quote test answers, "find the sentence that says..." tasks),
giving the learner real, partial evidence that precise wording matters
— generalizing this into "understanding requires exact wording" is a
reasonable but overextended conclusion. The words-guarantee-
understanding failure exists because word-level recognition genuinely
IS a necessary precondition for comprehension (a learner who doesn't
know what "erupted" means cannot understand the sentence at all), so
treating word-level recognition as SUFFICIENT is an understandable
shortcut — nothing marks the gap until the learner is shown a sentence
where every word is familiar but a qualifying word ("only," "most") or
a pronoun reference changes the sentence's actual meaning in a way pure
word-recognition does not catch. Qualifying words and pronouns are
specifically dangerous because they do not introduce new vocabulary to
struggle with — they silently modify scope or referent using words the
learner already fully knows, which is precisely what makes the error
invisible from a word-recognition standpoint alone.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.reading.literal-comprehension.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-LITERAL-COMPREHENSION-MEANS-MEMORIZING-EXACT-WORDING (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 5, instruction-induced**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: Type 5, traceable to a specific external
  pattern rather than a self-generated overgeneralization — the
  Blueprint's own s6_path names school contexts that "sometimes reward
  verbatim quotes" as the direct origin; a learner who has been
  rewarded for exact-quote answers in prior instruction reasonably
  concludes precise wording is the actual target, taking that specific
  instructional pattern as an absolute rule rather than a context-
  specific technique.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when asked to
  restate a fact, confidently produces an accurate paraphrase in fresh
  wording without first checking "did I get the exact words right?" is
  showing the verbatim-memorization anxiety has been resolved.

**MC-IF-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-WORDS-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-TEXT (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: Type 1 — word-level recognition genuinely
  IS necessary for comprehension, and for most ordinary sentences
  (without qualifying words or ambiguous pronouns), knowing every word
  reliably DOES produce correct sentence-level understanding — the
  misconception surfaces specifically on the subset of sentences
  containing qualifiers or pronoun references, where the true-most-of-
  the-time correlation between word-knowledge and sentence-understanding
  breaks down.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, given a sentence
  with a qualifying word or ambiguous pronoun, spontaneously pauses to
  check "wait, does this mean ALL of them, or just SOME?" or "who does
  'she' refer to here?" before answering is showing the integration
  check has generalized past confident but unchecked word recognition.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the Fact-Extraction Worksheet itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): extracting
  and listing explicit facts (who, what, when, where) in the learner's
  OWN words, rather than copying sentences verbatim, directly seeds
  MC-A's fix by making own-words restatement the concrete, practiced
  task from the start. The same anchor's qualifying-detail check ("Only
  some of the plants survived the frost" — does the extracted fact
  correctly preserve "only some," not "all" or "none"?) directly seeds
  MC-IF-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-WORDS-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-TEXT's fix. Breaking
  point: the worksheet anchor demonstrates both insights well on
  isolated sentences, but does not on its own build pronoun-resolution
  practice (TA-4), which needs its own dedicated sentence set.
- **Alternative — a detective's case notes vs. a court transcript**:
  literal comprehension is like a detective writing case notes in their
  own words that accurately capture the facts (who, what, when),
  rather than transcribing a witness's testimony word for word — the
  facts must be right, but the wording is the detective's own.
  Breaking point: useful for the own-words-restatement insight
  broadly, but doesn't address the qualifying-word integration insight
  at all, which needs the worksheet anchor's own explicit qualifier
  check.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT frame literal comprehension checks as "find
  the sentence in the text that says this"**: this framing directly
  reinforces MC-LITERAL-COMPREHENSION-MEANS-MEMORIZING-EXACT-WORDING by
  rewarding location-and-quotation of original wording rather than
  restatement in the learner's own words; instead, per the Blueprint's
  own Adaptive Flags, every fact-extraction task should explicitly
  require paraphrasing, never verbatim location-and-copy.

  rather than open-ended restatement alone.
- **Teacher-demo (the qualifier-flip contrast)**: the tutor models the

- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a fact-
  extraction, paraphrase, qualifier-integration, or pronoun-resolution
  attempt (TA-1 through TA-5), ask the learner to state their own
  attempt before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** Both the
own-words-restatement insight and the word-knowledge-is-not-sufficient
insight are genuinely felt, directly testable phenomena well-suited to
guided discovery: **need** (the learner is asked to restate "The
volcano erupted in 1883, killing thousands" and, upon producing an
accurate paraphrase, is asked whether they needed the exact words to
show understanding, discovering the answer is no); **playground/
invention** (TA-1's fact extraction and TA-2's detail-preserving
paraphrase build initial restatement confidence before the qualifier
complication is introduced); **collision** (the Blueprint's own "you
know every word in this sentence — but can you tell me exactly WHO did
WHAT to WHOM?" probe breaks the words-are-sufficient assumption, and the
"only some of the students passed" trigger breaks it specifically for
qualifying words); **formalization** (the necessary-but-not-sufficient
framing for word-level vs. sentence-level understanding, and the own-
words-restatement framing for what literal comprehension actually
requires); **compression** (TA-5's fluent direct-question answering,
combining all four skills without each separately re-derived).

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (extracting explicit facts) is placed first, directly targeting
MC-LITERAL-COMPREHENSION-MEANS-MEMORIZING-EXACT-WORDING, per the
Blueprint's own session architecture — establishing own-words
restatement as the standard practice from the very first activity,
before any more nuanced integration work begins. TA-2 (paraphrasing
without losing key details) deepens the restatement skill by adding a
precision requirement — accurate paraphrase is not enough if names,
numbers, or outcomes are lost or altered along the way. TA-3
(integrating qualifying words) directly targets MC-IF-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-
WORDS-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-TEXT, introduced once the learner has secured
basic fact-restatement, since qualifier integration is a refinement of
that same restatement accuracy rather than a separate skill. TA-4
(resolving pronoun references) extends the same integration discipline
to a second, distinct source of silent meaning-change (referent
ambiguity rather than scope-narrowing). TA-5 (answering direct
who/what/when/where questions) is placed last as the concept's genuine
capstone, requiring fact extraction, detail preservation, qualifier
integration, and pronoun resolution to operate together against
specific, targeted questions rather than open-ended restatement alone.
This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session
script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner's paraphrase attempts
  spontaneously use fresh wording rather than hedging with "I think it
  says..." followed by near-verbatim recall, and whether the learner
  spontaneously flags a qualifying word or pronoun as needing a second
  check before answering — these are the primary diagnostic signals for
  whether TA-1 and TA-3/TA-4's disciplines have genuinely generalized.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before answering a
  qualifier-containing or pronoun-containing question, followed by a
  correct, carefully-scoped answer, suggests the learner is consciously
  checking sentence-level integration rather than reacting to
  recognized words alone — a good sign. Instant, confident answers that
  ignore a qualifying word's scope (treating "only some" as "all") is
  the concerning pattern for MC-IF-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-WORDS-I-UNDERSTAND-
  THE-TEXT.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: per the Blueprint's own S9 routing
  note, English learners may face a COMPOUNDED difficulty — literal-
  comprehension errors can stem from either missed individual
  vocabulary OR failed sentence-level integration (qualifiers, L1
  sentence-structure differences affecting pronoun resolution), and the
  tutor should diagnose which specific source is actually operating
  before assuming either one, per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags —
  do not default to reteaching vocabulary if the actual gap is
  integration, or vice versa.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does that mean ALL of them, or just
  SOME?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for MC-IF-I-
  UNDERSTAND-THE-WORDS-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-TEXT, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh sentence containing a qualifying word is
  encountered, making the scope check a routinely-invited response
  rather than an afterthought.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a fact-extraction or paraphrase task shrinks to
  the Component 3 Fact-Extraction Worksheet anchor using the shortest,
  simplest sentence available, with the tutor modeling an own-words
  restatement before the learner attempts a fresh extraction,
  qualifier-check, or pronoun-resolution task independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for MC-A, an attempt
  to recall or quote the exact original wording (rather than
  paraphrasing freely) is more likely than "I don't know" — route
  directly to the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence probe rather than
  treating it as a generic restatement gap. For MC-IF-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-
  WORDS-I-UNDERSTAND-THE-TEXT, a confident but scope-incorrect answer
  (treating "only some" as "all") is more likely than confusion — route
  directly to TA-3's qualifying-word integration practice.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously catches their own scope error mid-answer ("only some of
  them — wait, that's not all of them, let me re-answer") before being
  told is showing the integration-check discipline has generalized —
  this should be named and praised explicitly, consistent with the
  Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags emphasis on qualifying words as a
  distinct, high-value practice category.

  is.
