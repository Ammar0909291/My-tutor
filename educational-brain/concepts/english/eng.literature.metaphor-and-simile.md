## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Distinguish a genuine metaphor or simile (a figurative comparison highlighting one specific shared quality) from mere word-spotting on "like" or "as."
2. Explain why the presence of "like" or "as" alone does not determine whether a comparison is a simile.
3. Identify what specific quality a given metaphor or simile is highlighting, rather than treating the comparison as literal or arbitrary.
4. Distinguish a bold, direct metaphor's claim from a softer, more suggestive simile's signal.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **identifying a simile is not simply spotting the words "like" or "as" anywhere in a sentence** — many uses of "like" or "as" are literal comparisons or unrelated grammatical structures, and a genuine simile requires an actual figurative comparison highlighting a specific shared quality between two otherwise different things. Second, **not every comparison using "like" or "as" is automatically a simile just because it fits that surface pattern** — the deeper test is whether the sentence is making a genuine, purposeful figurative claim about a shared quality, distinguishing a bold, direct metaphorical assertion ("time is a thief") from a softer, more suggestive simile's signal ("time slips by like sand"), each doing different rhetorical work.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "any sentence containing the word 'like' or 'as' is a simile, and any comparison is basically the same thing."**
The simulation: flag every occurrence of "like" or "as" as a simile regardless of whether a genuine figurative comparison is present, and treat all comparisons as interchangeable in strength and function.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a Word-Job Sorting Table separating literal from figurative uses of "like"/"as," and a Bold-Claim vs. Soft-Signal Dial distinguishing metaphor's directness from simile's suggestiveness.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Not every 'like' or 'as' signals a simile — and a bold metaphor claim differs from a soft simile signal."

**Intermediate — "I can now sort literal from figurative 'like'/'as' uses, but I still treat all genuine similes and metaphors as equally strong."**
The simulation: correctly filters out literal comparisons but doesn't yet distinguish a metaphor's directness from a simile's softness.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct dial exercise, rating a set of genuine figurative comparisons along the bold-claim-to-soft-signal spectrum.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A metaphor asserts directly; a simile signals more gently. Notice which one a given line is doing."
Notes:

**Advanced — "I identify genuine figurative comparisons, name the specific shared quality, and place them correctly on the bold-to-soft spectrum in a novel passage."**
The simulation: integrates word-spotting filtering, shared-quality identification, and directness placement into one coherent reading. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: the two direct downstream cross-links, each extending into a related but distinct figurative device.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you metaphor and simile specifically. Symbolism and imagery work by different principles, worth their own study next."

**Expert — "at fluency, recognizing genuine figurative comparison happens as an integrated part of ordinary literary reading, without a separate deliberate word-spotting check."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit sorting-and-dial routine is a scaffold for a reading habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of literary comprehension.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **"like" and "as" are genuinely reliable surface cues for many similes**, so pattern-matching on those words alone works often enough in simple examples that the shortcut goes uncorrected until a literal comparison ("she runs like her sister does, every morning") exposes the gap.

The second mechanism is that **early instruction commonly introduces simile and metaphor as interchangeable "comparison" devices** without distinguishing their different rhetorical strength, reinforcing a flattened, single-category view until a concept like this one deliberately confronts the directness distinction.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-ANY-SENTENCE-WITH-LIKE-OR-AS-IS-AUTOMATICALLY-A-SIMILE
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 4 (notation-induced), from "like" and "as" functioning as a genuinely reliable surface marker often enough that the marker itself gets treated as the defining test, rather than the underlying figurative-comparison judgment it's meant to signal.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **add the figurative-versus-literal filter as a further test, not discard the "like"/"as" cue entirely** — the cue is a useful hint that still requires confirming a genuine figurative comparison is present.
- **Verification of death**: given sentences with literal and figurative uses of "like"/"as," the learner correctly sorts only the genuine similes.

### MC-B-A-BOLD-METAPHOR-AND-A-SOFT-SIMILE-ARE-RHETORICALLY-INTERCHANGEABLE
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of "these are both comparison devices" extended into treating them as equally strong and interchangeable in effect.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct side-by-side rating of paired metaphor/simile examples on the same idea**, since the strength difference is far more convincing demonstrated than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given a metaphor and a simile on the same idea, the learner explains the difference in directness or rhetorical effect.

## Analogies

**Best — the Word-Job Sorting Table and the Bold-Claim vs. Soft-Signal Dial** (Blueprint Component 3): a sorting table separating literal from figurative uses of "like"/"as" directly seeds the fix for MC-A; a dial ranging from bold direct claim to soft suggestive signal directly seeds the fix for MC-B.
*Breaking point*: a physical sorting table implies a clean binary (literal or figurative), while some real comparisons sit in a genuinely ambiguous zone requiring interpretive judgment a table's clean categories don't fully capture.

**Alternative — a photograph versus a painterly sketch of the same scene.** A metaphor is like the photograph's direct, bold claim ("this IS the scene"); a simile is like the sketch's softer, more suggestive rendering ("this is LIKE the scene, evoking it").
*Breaking point*: a photograph and a sketch are both genuinely representational images of the same subject, while a metaphor's "is" claim is literally false (time is not literally a thief) in a way a photograph's direct representation is not.

**Visual anchor — the Word-Job Sorting Table and Bold-Claim vs. Soft-Signal Dial**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"If you see 'like' or 'as,' it's a simile."** Directly installs the word-spotting misconception as a stated definition.
- **"Metaphor and simile are basically the same thing, just with different words."** Directly installs the interchangeability misconception as a stated definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Word-Job Sorting Table (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Is every sentence with 'like' in it a simile?"
2. **The literal-versus-figurative collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Is this comparison figurative, or is it just describing a literal fact?"
3. **The Bold-Claim vs. Soft-Signal Dial (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Does this metaphor and this simile feel equally forceful, or does one assert more directly than the other?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by testing candidate sentences against the figurative/literal and bold/soft filters.

1. **Need**: "If I say 'she runs like her sister does every morning,' is that a simile, even though it has the word 'like'?"
2. **Playground**: the learner sorts several "like"/"as" sentences into literal versus figurative freely before being told the official test.
3. **Invention**: "Can you rewrite this simile as a metaphor, and describe how the feeling changes even though the comparison is the same?"
4. **Collision**: present a matched metaphor/simile pair on the same idea and ask whether they're equally forceful.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — "like"/"as" alone doesn't make a simile, and metaphor asserts more directly than simile — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Check for genuine figurative comparison, then judge how boldly it claims."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Sorting literal from figurative "like"/"as" (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the first misconception before the directness dial is introduced, since confirming genuine figurative comparison is the foundational filter the directness judgment builds on.
- **Naming the specific shared quality (TA-2) comes second**, extending TA-1 into identifying what a confirmed simile or metaphor is actually highlighting.
- **The Bold-Claim vs. Soft-Signal Dial (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting the second misconception once genuine figurative comparisons are reliably identified.
- **Full application to a novel passage (TA-4) comes last**, the generative capstone integrating both misconception fixes — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for classifying a comparison as figurative or literal, and for rating its directness**, since a correct-looking classification with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated figurative-versus-literal check** ("is this actually comparing two different things for a shared quality, or just describing a real similarity?") applied to a new sentence — the direct behavioral signature of the word-spotting fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary rating of a comparison's directness**, beyond just identifying it as figurative — direct evidence the bold/soft distinction is internalized.
- **Flagging every "like"/"as" occurrence as a simile with no figurative check** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Check whether it's a real figurative comparison first, then judge how boldly it claims."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But it has the word 'like' in it, so isn't it a simile?"** — take this as an accurate observation about a genuinely useful cue, not a mistake; respond by adding the stricter test: "That word is a hint, that's true — but is it actually comparing two different things for a shared quality, or just describing something literal?"
- **"I thought metaphor and simile were basically the same"** — a genuine, reasonable expectation given how they're often introduced together; respond by naming the additional layer: "They're both figurative comparisons, yes — but one claims directly and one signals more softly. Which is this one doing?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full novel-passage analysis down to **sorting a single list of 4 "like"/"as" sentences into literal versus figurative, with no directness rating demanded.** Isolates the most basic filtering test before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just find the word 'like' or 'as.'"** This directly licenses the word-spotting pattern, undermining the figurative-comparison discipline this concept specifically builds.
