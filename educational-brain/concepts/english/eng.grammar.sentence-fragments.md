# Sentence Fragments — `eng.grammar.sentence-fragments`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.sentence-fragments` (canonical English
  KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Sentence Fragments" —
  incomplete sentences lacking a subject, a verb, or a complete
  thought, and strategies for correcting them.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.simple-sentences` — load-bearing
  because this concept is defined entirely by contrast to that
  concept's own completeness requirement (a subject and a verb
  expressing a complete thought); a learner cannot recognize when that
  requirement is NOT met without first securing what it means for it to
  BE met.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none listed — this is a terminal node in the
  current KG's grammar domain, though it is a foundational error-
  recognition skill underlying clear, correct writing throughout.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: analyze · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 1

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify a sentence fragment missing a subject entirely, or missing
   a verb entirely.
2. Identify the subtler fragment type — a subordinate/dependent clause
   punctuated alone as if it were a complete sentence, even though it
   contains both a subject and a verb.
3. Determine whether the more natural fix for a given fragment is
   joining it to an adjacent sentence or supplying a missing element
   independently.
4. Correct a fragment by joining it to the adjacent sentence it
   naturally belongs with.
5. Identify and correct a mix of both fragment types within a full
   passage, choosing the most natural fix for each.

## Core Understanding

A sentence fragment is a group of words punctuated as a sentence but
failing the completeness requirement `eng.grammar.simple-sentences`
established. The most basic fragment type is straightforward: missing a
subject entirely, or missing a verb entirely. But the concept's single
most load-bearing insight is the SUBTLER fragment type: a subordinate/
dependent clause CAN contain both a subject and a verb and still be a
fragment, because words like "because," "although," "when," and
"since" create an expectation that MORE information is coming —
"Because the rain started." has a subject ("rain") and a verb
("started"), but still leaves the reader waiting for what happened as
a RESULT. Having a subject and verb somewhere in a group of words is
like having flour and water somewhere in a kitchen — necessary
ingredients, but not automatically a finished dish. A second,
independent, equally load-bearing insight concerns HOW to fix a
fragment: the instinct to always pad a fragment into its own artificial
standalone sentence is usually the WRONG fix — the more natural
correction is usually to JOIN the fragment to the adjacent sentence it
logically belongs with (often the one right before or after it), the
way a puzzle piece connects to its natural neighbor rather than having
random extra cardboard glued onto it to force it to stand alone. "The
team celebrated. Because they had finally won the championship." reads
far more naturally joined into "The team celebrated because they had
finally won the championship" than padded into an awkward, artificial
standalone sentence.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "any group of words with a subject and a verb
  somewhere in it is a complete sentence, and fixing a fragment always
  means padding it out to stand alone"**: the arriving, pre-
  instructional model this concept exists to correct — a natural
  default because subject-plus-verb IS a genuinely necessary condition
  for completeness (just not a sufficient one), and because the
  fragment is already standing alone as its own punctuated unit,
  making "add more words to it" feel like the obvious repair direction.
- **Intermediate model — "I identify fragments missing a subject or a
  verb entirely"**: installed by TA-1, establishing the most basic
  fragment type as the entry point before the subtler case is
  introduced.
- **Advanced model — "I identify subordinate-clause fragments that
  contain a subject and verb but still leave the reader expecting
  more"**: installed by TA-2, using the Component 3 flour-and-water
  anchor and the "does this feel finished?" test, resolving
  MC-A-A-GROUP-OF-WORDS-IS-A-COMPLETE-SENTENCE-AS-LONG-AS-IT-HAS-A-
  SUBJECT-AND-A-VERB-SOMEWHERE-IN-IT. Upgrade trigger: correctly
  identifying a fresh subordinate-clause fragment despite its containing
  a genuine subject-verb pair, without hesitation.
- **Expert model — "I check whether joining to an adjacent sentence
  produces a more natural fix before considering other corrections, and
  I identify and correct both fragment types across a full passage"**:
  installed by TA-3 and TA-4, the concept's genuine mastery bar,
  resolving
  MC-B-FIXING-A-FRAGMENT-ALWAYS-MEANS-ADDING-MORE-WORDS-TO-THE-SAME-
  STANDALONE-GROUP by requiring the learner to actively check the
  join-vs-pad decision rather than defaulting to one fix.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing
  (S1), MC-A generally needs resolving BEFORE MC-B, since a learner who
  cannot identify the subtler subordinate-clause fragment type has
  nothing yet to practice fixing correctly — the sequence matters, not
  just coverage of both misconceptions.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and the Blueprint
itself explicitly notes that resolving the first is a prerequisite for
meaningfully practicing the second. The subject-and-verb-somewhere-is-
enough failure exists because subject-plus-verb genuinely IS a real,
necessary part of the completeness test — narrowing "complete sentence"
to just this check is a reasonable, incomplete generalization from
correct partial evidence, with nothing marking the subordinate-clause
exception until a learner is directly shown "Because the rain started."
and asked whether it feels finished or leaves them expecting more. The
always-pad-into-standalone failure exists because the fragment is
already a standalone punctuated unit, making "add more words to THIS
group" feel like the obvious, most direct repair — nothing marks the
join-to-neighbor alternative as usually superior until a learner
directly compares an awkwardly-padded standalone fix against a
naturally-joined one and is asked which actually reads better.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.sentence-fragments.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-A-A-GROUP-OF-WORDS-IS-A-COMPLETE-SENTENCE-AS-LONG-AS-IT-HAS-A-
SUBJECT-AND-A-VERB-SOMEWHERE-IN-IT (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — subject-plus-
  verb genuinely IS a real, necessary component of sentence
  completeness, so treating it as SUFFICIENT (not just necessary) is a
  reasonable, incomplete generalization from correct partial evidence;
  the misconception survives because most simple fragments a learner
  first meets genuinely ARE missing a subject or verb entirely, giving
  no signal that a subject-and-verb-containing group can STILL be
  incomplete until a subordinate-clause example is directly examined.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a fresh
  subordinate clause standing alone, spontaneously says it "leaves me
  waiting for what happened" before being told is showing the feels-
  finished test has been genuinely internalized beyond the basic
  subject-and-verb check.

**MC-B-FIXING-A-FRAGMENT-ALWAYS-MEANS-ADDING-MORE-WORDS-TO-THE-SAME-
STANDALONE-GROUP (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — a fragment is
  already standing alone as its own punctuated unit, making "expand
  THIS unit" a reasonable, direct-feeling repair strategy; the
  misconception survives because padding a fragment into a grammatically
  valid standalone sentence genuinely IS a possible fix in the narrow
  technical sense, even when it produces awkward, unnatural prose
  compared to the join-to-neighbor alternative.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when correcting a
  fresh fragment, spontaneously checks the sentence immediately before
  or after it for a natural join opportunity before considering a
  standalone patch is showing the join-first discipline has generalized
  beyond the specific TA-3 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the combined Flour-and-Water-and-Puzzle-Piece anchor
  itself is the analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint
  Component 3): flour and water sitting separately in a kitchen —
  necessary ingredients present, but not yet a finished dish — directly
  seeds the fix for the subject-and-verb-isn't-enough insight; a puzzle
  piece that clearly belongs connected to its neighbor, rather than
  having random extra cardboard glued onto it, directly seeds the fix
  for the join-don't-pad insight. Breaking point: the combined anchor
  demonstrates both core insights clearly but doesn't on its own train
  the integrated, full-passage synthesis task (TA-4), which needs its
  own separate, mixed-fragment-type practice set.
- **Alternative — an unfinished text message ending mid-thought**:
  "Because I forgot my umbrella" sent alone as a text message would
  make the recipient reply "...and then what?" — the same genuine
  unfulfilled-expectation feeling a subordinate-clause fragment creates
  in formal writing. Breaking point: useful for the feels-incomplete
  insight, but doesn't address the join-vs-pad fixing decision, which
  the puzzle-piece anchor's own TA-3 practice handles directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe every fragment as "always wrong" or
  "never acceptable"**: per the Blueprint's own explicit Adaptive
  Flags, intentional, stylistically effective fragments used by
  professional writers for emphasis are a legitimate, advanced
  stylistic choice, genuinely distinct from the unintentional error
  this concept addresses; over-applying fragment detection to every
  short, deliberate construction risks diluting the core rule for
  standard academic writing and misidentifying a valid stylistic
  choice as an error.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the Flour-and-Water and Puzzle-Piece anchor)**:
  the Blueprint's Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not
  restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any short, unpunctuated string of thoughts the

- **Teacher-demo (the feels-finished test)**: the tutor models the

- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  fragment-identification or correction attempt (TA-1 through TA-4),
  ask the learner to state their own attempt before the tutor confirms

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions, appropriate
to this concept's `analyze`-level Bloom classification.** The feels-
finished test and the join-before-pad insight are both genuine, directly
testable phenomena well-suited to guided discovery: **need** (the
learner, having secured TA-1's missing-subject/verb detection, is shown
"Because the rain started." — which HAS both a subject and verb — and
asked whether it's complete, discovering their subject-and-verb-check
instinct feels wrong once the sentence is read aloud); **playground/
invention** (TA-1's basic-fragment practice builds initial confidence
with the core completeness test before the subtler subordinate-clause
case and the fixing-strategy complication are introduced); **collision**
(the Blueprint's own "Because the rain started." probe breaks the
subject-and-verb-is-enough assumption; the padded-vs-joined fix
comparison breaks the always-pad assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's
full identification-and-correction task, requiring articulation of both
insights across a mixed passage); **compression** (TA-4's fluent full-
passage synthesis, applying both disciplines directly without each
instance separately re-derived). The specific term "fragment" is
convention-bound grammatical terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying
distinctions through the anchor and TA-1–2.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (fragments missing a subject or a verb) is placed first as the
concept's most basic, unambiguous entry point, per the Blueprint's own
session architecture — establishing the simplest fragment type before
any subtler complication is introduced. TA-2 (subordinate clause
fragments) directly targets
MC-A-A-GROUP-OF-WORDS-IS-A-COMPLETE-SENTENCE-AS-LONG-AS-IT-HAS-A-
SUBJECT-AND-A-VERB-SOMEWHERE-IN-IT, using the flour-and-water anchor
and the feels-finished test to establish the subtler fragment type. TA-3
(fixing a fragment by joining to an adjacent sentence) directly targets
MC-B-FIXING-A-FRAGMENT-ALWAYS-MEANS-ADDING-MORE-WORDS-TO-THE-SAME-
STANDALONE-GROUP, addressing a SECOND, independent dimension (fixing
strategy, not fragment-type identification) — per the Blueprint's own
Protocol Routing, this sequencing is deliberate: MC-A generally needs
resolving before MC-B, since a learner who cannot even identify the
subtler fragment type has nothing yet to practice fixing correctly.
TA-4 (full application: identifying and correcting fragments) is
placed last as the concept's integration capstone, requiring the
learner to apply both fragment-type recognition and the fixing-strategy
decision across a realistic mixed passage. This entry does not restate
the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see
Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously applies the
  feels-finished test to a fresh subordinate clause (rather than
  stopping at the basic subject-and-verb check), and whether the
  learner spontaneously checks the adjacent sentences for a natural
  join opportunity before padding a fresh fragment into a standalone
  patch — these are the primary diagnostic signals for whether TA-2 and
  TA-3's disciplines have genuinely generalized beyond the specific
  practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before classifying a
  fresh subject-and-verb-containing clause as complete, followed by a
  correct application of the feels-finished test, suggests the learner
  is consciously checking beyond the basic subject/verb presence — a
  good sign. Instant, confident acceptance of any subject-and-verb-
  containing group as complete (without checking for an unfulfilled
  expectation) is the concerning pattern for
  MC-A-A-GROUP-OF-WORDS-IS-A-COMPLETE-SENTENCE-AS-LONG-AS-IT-HAS-A-
  SUBJECT-AND-A-VERB-SOMEWHERE-IN-IT.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: not specifically flagged for L1-
  transfer in this Blueprint — both misconceptions here stem from
  genuine overgeneralization of English-internal completeness patterns
  (Type 1) rather than L1-specific structural transfer, so no S9-
  specific routing beyond standard practice depth is called for. The
  Blueprint instead flags a different population worth noting: per its
  own Adaptive Flags, learners with a strong ear for spoken-language
  rhythm may find the "does this feel finished?" test more intuitive
  than the formal subject/verb identification — accept this as a valid
  entry point, then connect it to the formal grammatical explanation
  for precision.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does this feel finished, or am I
  still waiting for something?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence
  for
  MC-A-A-GROUP-OF-WORDS-IS-A-COMPLETE-SENTENCE-AS-LONG-AS-IT-HAS-A-
  SUBJECT-AND-A-VERB-SOMEWHERE-IN-IT, per
