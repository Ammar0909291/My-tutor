## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Summarize three short texts on a related theme individually.
2. Write one genuinely integrated sentence drawing on all three texts together.
3. Identify the shared underlying topic across texts in different genres and connect them.
4. Identify one point of agreement and one point of differing perspective across sources.
5. Fully synthesize three new texts in different genres on a shared topic.

## Core Understanding

This concept extends `eng.reading.evaluating-sources`' single-source assessment and `eng.reading.compare-and-contrast-texts`' two-text comparison into true multi-text, multi-genre synthesis. Two corrections define it. First, **synthesizing multiple texts is not the same as summarizing each one separately, placed back to back** — genuine synthesis integrates insights across texts into one unified understanding, drawing on multiple sources at once ("all three sources agree that X, though the poem frames it emotionally while the report frames it statistically"), not three isolated summaries that never actually combine. Second, **texts in different genres are not inherently unable to be meaningfully connected** — genre is the FORM a text takes, not a wall around its content; a poem and a scientific report can address the same underlying topic and illuminate different facets of it, and a reader must look past surface genre difference to find and connect the shared subject.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "synthesizing means summarizing each text separately, one after another; texts in different genres can't really be connected."**
The simulation: produce three independent summaries with no connecting statement, and assume a poem and a report simply have nothing to do with each other.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — three separate summaries versus one integrated synthesis sentence, asked which actually combines insights; and a poem on drought's emotional experience alongside a scientific report on drought's measurable effects, asked whether the two are genuinely related despite different genres.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Synthesis combines insights into one idea, not three separate summaries. Genre is form, not a content barrier."

**Intermediate — "I write integrated sentences within one genre now, but I still sometimes treat texts in very different genres as too different to connect."**
The simulation: correctly integrates same-genre or similar texts but hasn't yet reliably found shared topics across sharply different genres.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct practice identifying the shared underlying topic between a poem and a report.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Look past the different FORM — what shared subject do these texts both address?"

**Advanced — "I understand each text individually, genuinely integrate insights across all of them, connect across genre differences, and identify agreement and tension across sources."**
The simulation: integrates individual comprehension, genuine synthesis, cross-genre connection, and agreement/tension identification into one coherent analysis. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the cross-linked `eng.literature.comparative-literature-intro`, extending this concept's cross-genre synthesis into formal comparative literary study.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the full synthesis toolkit across any genres. Comparative literature applies this formally to literary works specifically."

**Expert — "at fluency, genuine cross-genre synthesis happens as an integrated part of ordinary research and reading."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit understand-integrate-connect routine is a scaffold for a reading habit that, at true fluency, operates as an integrated part of any multi-source research task.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **producing separate summaries is a genuinely easier, more mechanical task than genuine integration**, since each summary only requires processing one text at a time, while true synthesis requires holding multiple texts' content simultaneously and finding a connecting idea — a much higher cognitive demand that a simpler "one paragraph per text" structure can superficially mimic.

The second mechanism is that **genre is the single most immediately obvious feature distinguishing two texts** (a poem looks and reads completely differently from a scientific report), so this salient surface difference is mistaken for evidence of a deeper, content-level unrelatedness, when the two texts may in fact address the identical underlying subject.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-SYNTHESIZING-MULTIPLE-TEXTS-MEANS-SUMMARIZING-EACH-ONE-SEPARATELY-BACK-TO-BACK
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry — the three-separate-summaries/one-integrated-sentence discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the genuinely useful practice of summarizing individual texts (a real, necessary first step, per TA-1), extended into treating that step as sufficient for synthesis, when genuine integration is a distinct further task.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **have the learner take their own separate-summary draft and write one genuinely integrating sentence from it**, since the fix requires practicing the integration step itself, not merely being told separate summaries are insufficient.
- **Verification of death**: given three individually-summarized texts, the learner writes one integrated sentence drawing on all three together.

### MC-B-TEXTS-IN-DIFFERENT-GENRES-CANNOT-BE-MEANINGFULLY-CONNECTED-OR-SYNTHESIZED
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — the drought-poem/drought-report discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from genre's strong, immediately salient surface difference being mistaken for evidence of unrelated content, when the underlying subject matter may be identical.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct practice locating the shared topic explicitly**, since the fix requires developing a habit of looking past surface form for content, not merely being told genre isn't a barrier.
- **Verification of death**: given a poem and a report on a related topic, the learner identifies the shared underlying topic and writes one sentence integrating both.

## Analogies

**Best — the Three-Separate-Photographs-vs-Single-Portrait and Photograph-vs-Written-Description Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): three separate photographs never combined into one unified portrait directly seeds the genuine-synthesis fix; a photograph and a written description of the same event, different forms but genuinely connected, directly seeds the cross-genre fix.
*Breaking point*: a "single portrait" implies one final, fixed combined image, while genuine textual synthesis often produces a written argument or claim rather than a single visual artifact — useful for the combining-not-juxtaposing intuition, less precise for the actual output format of synthesis.
*Breaking point (photograph/description)*: a photograph and a written description of the identical event are trivially, obviously about the same subject, while a poem and a scientific report's shared topic often requires more interpretive work to identify — useful for the different-forms-same-subject intuition, less precise for the harder cross-genre identification task this concept actually requires.

**Alternative — a jury weighing testimony, forensic evidence, and expert analysis together.** Each type of evidence has a completely different "genre" (spoken testimony, physical evidence, technical report), yet a jury must synthesize all three into one coherent understanding of what happened, not treat each in isolation.
*Breaking point*: a jury's synthesis goal (determining a single factual truth) is more convergent than literary/topical synthesis, which often legitimately supports multiple valid integrated readings.

**Visual anchor — the Three-Separate-Photographs-vs-Single-Portrait and Photograph-vs-Written-Description Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Just summarize each source one at a time, and that's your synthesis."** Directly installs the separate-summaries misconception as a stated procedure.
- **"A poem and a scientific report are too different to really connect."** Directly installs the genre-barrier misconception as a stated rule.

Then connect to genuine synthesis.
2. **The separate-summaries/integrated-sentence collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-A. *Predict first*: "Does this version ever combine the three texts' insights into one idea?"
3. **The drought-poem/drought-report collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-B. *Predict first*: "Could understanding the emotional experience help you understand why the measurable effects matter?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both synthesis facts are directly checkable by comparing separate-summary drafts against genuine integration attempts, and by hunting for shared topics across genres.

1. **Need**: "If I read three texts and write three separate paragraphs, have I actually combined anything?"
2. **Playground**: the learner attempts several integration sentences from pre-summarized texts freely before being told the official genuine-synthesis standard.
3. **Invention**: "Can you find one shared topic connecting a poem and a report that look completely different on the surface?"
4. **Collision**: present a poem and a report on drought; ask whether genre difference prevents connection.
5. **Formalization**: name the two synthesis facts — genuine synthesis integrates, doesn't just juxtapose; genre is form, not a content barrier — from the learner's own attempts.
6. **Compression**: "Combine, don't stack — and look past form for shared content."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Understanding each text individually first (TA-1) comes first**, establishing the necessary but insufficient foundation for synthesis.
- **Genuine synthesis vs. separate summaries (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting MC-A once individual comprehension is secure.
- **Connecting texts across different genres (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting MC-B.
- **Identifying agreement and tension (TA-4) and full application (TA-5) come last**, extending synthesis to weighing differing perspectives and integrating everything into a generative capstone — matching this program's established sequencing principle.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for why a given sentence counts as genuine synthesis, or what shared topic connects two differently-formed texts**, since a plausible-looking synthesis with no stated integrating logic cannot be distinguished from a juxtaposed set of separate summaries.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A single sentence explicitly naming multiple sources together** ("all three sources suggest...") rather than one summary per source — the direct behavioral signature of the genuine-synthesis fix taking hold.
- **A stated search for shared topic before dismissing two differently-formed texts as unrelated** ("these look totally different, but what are they both actually about?") — direct evidence the cross-genre fix is internalized.
- **A "synthesis" that could be cleanly split into independent, unconnected sections**, one per text — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Combine what these texts say together — and look past their different forms for what they share."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads:

- **Summarizing individually (MP-1) and writing a genuinely integrated statement (MP-2).** Per the Blueprint's S9 routing, MP-1/MP-2 failures indicate residual MC-A.
- **Connecting across genre difference (MP-3) and identifying agreement/tension (MP-4).** MP-3/MP-4 failures indicate residual MC-B.
- **Full cross-genre synthesis (MP-5).** The synthesis item; per the Blueprint, failure here with MP-1 through MP-4 passing indicates an integration gap, not a residual misconception.

**Mastery certification trigger**: three correct individual summaries; one genuinely integrated sentence drawing on all three; a correctly identified shared topic across a poem and a report with an integrating sentence; one correctly identified point of agreement and one of differing perspective; a full synthesis of three new cross-genre texts.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"I summarized all three, isn't that the synthesis?"** — take this as a reasonable first step, not a mistake; respond by asking for the next step: "Great start — now what do all three say TOGETHER that none says alone?"
- **"A poem and a scientific report just don't go together"** — a reasonable-sounding surface reaction; respond by redirecting to content: "Let's ignore the form for a second — what subject is each one actually about?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full three-text synthesis down to **writing one integrated sentence from two already-summarized, same-genre texts, with no cross-genre task demanded.** Isolates the most basic integration skill before returning to fuller synthesis.
- **Never shrink to "just list what each text says."** This directly licenses the separate-summaries pattern, undermining the integration discipline this concept specifically builds.
