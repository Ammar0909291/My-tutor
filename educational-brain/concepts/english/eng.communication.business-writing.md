## Learning Objective
The learner will write a business email or memo that states its main point or request in the first sentence or two, use clear scannable formatting, and explicitly identify what action, if any, the reader is expected to take.

## Core Understanding
Business writing requires priorities that are the genuine OPPOSITE of academic essay convention: state the main point, request, or conclusion in the FIRST sentence or two, rather than building context toward it. A busy workplace reader is scanning, not closely reading, and needs to know immediately what's being asked of them or what decision is needed — supporting context belongs AFTER the main point is already clear, not before it. Separately, visual FORMATTING (short paragraphs, bullet points for lists/action items, clear subject lines) is not cosmetic — it is what makes information actually usable by a reader who is scanning rather than carefully parsing dense prose; the same accurate content, buried in one unbroken paragraph, is functionally less useful than the identical content presented as a scannable list.

## Mental Models
- **Road-Sign-at-Speed Anchor**: a road sign with the critical information (which exit to take) buried in small print at the bottom fails a driver passing at speed. Business writing needs to put the destination — the main point or request — right at the top, in large, unmissable letters, with supporting details after.
- **Grocery-List Anchor**: a grocery list scribbled as one run-on sentence contains the same information as a list with one item per line, but only one can actually be scanned at a glance in a store aisle. Formatting determines whether accurate content is actually usable.

## Why Students Fail
Learners who've just mastered building an argument's context before stating a thesis (the academic essay convention) apply that same instinct to business writing, not recognizing it's a genuinely different register optimized for a different reading behavior (scanning, not close reading). Separately, learners believe that as long as content is accurate and complete, its visual organization is a cosmetic afterthought — missing that formatting is a functional tool determining whether a busy reader can actually extract and act on the information at all.

## Misconceptions
- **MC-A (Type 6, analogy overextension from academic essay-building conventions)**: "Business writing should build up to the main point like an academic essay." Diagnostic: present two emails with the same request — one building context before the request, one stating the request immediately — and ask which a busy manager with fifty unread emails could act on fastest. Repair: road-sign-at-speed anchor — state the main point, request, or conclusion in the FIRST sentence or two; context comes after.
- **MC-B (Type 1, overgeneralization from "content matters, form doesn't")**: "A business document just needs to contain the right information — formatting doesn't matter." Diagnostic: present the same three action items as one dense paragraph versus a bulleted list, and ask which a reader could scan and extract in five seconds. Repair: grocery-list anchor — actively use short paragraphs, bullet points, and clear subject lines to make the document scannable rather than relying on the reader to carefully parse dense prose.

## Analogies
- Road-sign-at-speed anchor (above) for front-loading the main point.
- Grocery-list anchor (above) for functional (not cosmetic) formatting.
- Anti-analogy: front-loading is not "omit all context" — supporting context is still necessary, just SUBORDINATED to (placed after) the main point rather than preceding it; and formatting is not "bullet-point everything" — connected reasoning may still need brief prose, while discrete items suit bullets.

## Demonstrations
- Compare a schedule-change-request email that buries the request in paragraph three against one stating it in sentence one, asking which a busy reader could act on fastest.
- Compare the same three post-meeting action items as one dense paragraph versus a bulleted list with owner and deadline for each, asking which is scannable in five seconds.
- Full worked example: a schedule-change request email with the request stated immediately, brief supporting context, and a clear, specific subject line.

## Discovery Questions
- "If you were a busy manager with fifty unread emails, which version would you understand and act on fastest?"
- "Both versions contain the same information — which one could you scan and extract the action items from in five seconds?"
- "Is your main point or request in the first sentence, or does the reader have to read three paragraphs to find it?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Diagnostic: academic-essay-structure recognition check and basic audience-awareness check.
2. Anchor: road-sign-at-speed and grocery-list framing together.
3. TA-1: identify a buried main point in a draft email and rewrite the opening to state it immediately (targets MC-A).
4. TA-2: subordinate context to support (not precede) the main point.
5. TA-3: reformat a dense paragraph of action items into short paragraphs and bullets (targets MC-B).
6. TA-4: full business communication — front-loaded main point, subordinated context, scannable formatting.

## Tutor Actions
- SHOW: buried-request vs. front-loaded-request email pair; dense-paragraph vs. bulleted-list action-items pair.
- DO: delete everything in a draft before the sentence stating the actual request, then rewrite from there, adding back only necessary context.
- TEST-THINKING: predict how quickly a busy reader could extract the main action item from a given draft.
- ORGANIZE: identify every distinct action item/deadline/decision buried in a paragraph, then reformat as a bulleted list.

## Voice Teaching Notes
Learners who've just completed `eng.communication.academic-writing-advanced` may show active resistance, defending the build-up structure as "more thorough" or "more professional" — frame this explicitly as a genuinely different register with different goals, not a lowering of standards.

## Assessment Signals
- Probe: "Identify the actual main point of this email and where it currently appears." An answer that can't locate the buried request, or that defends the build-up structure as appropriate, signals MC-A.
- Probe: given a dense paragraph with three action items, "identify which parts could be reformatted as a bulleted list." A response treating the paragraph as already fine signals MC-B.
- Confident-wrong signature: a well-written, grammatically polished email that nonetheless states its actual request only in the final sentence — flag MC-A regardless of the prose quality preceding it.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If a learner overcorrects after MC-B into excessive bullet-fragmentation (breaking genuinely connected reasoning into disconnected bullet fragments), clarify that bullets suit discrete items while connected reasoning may still need brief prose — formatting should match the content's actual structure.

## Memory Hooks
"Big letters at the top of the sign" for front-loading; "one item per line, not a run-on sentence" for scannable formatting.

## Cross-Subject Connections
The front-loading discipline mirrors an executive summary's function in any professional report (state the conclusion first, then support it) and mirrors journalism's "inverted pyramid" structure — most important information first, supporting detail after, regardless of subject matter.
