# Complex Sentences — `eng.grammar.complex-sentences`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.complex-sentences` (canonical English
  KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Complex Sentences" —
  sentences formed by joining an independent clause with one or more
  dependent clauses.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.compound-sentences` — load-bearing
  because complex-sentence classification directly extends the clause-
  independence judgment already secured for compound sentences (the
  standalone test) to a NEW clause type — dependent clauses introduced
  by subordinating conjunctions — rather than introducing an unrelated
  new skill.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.sentence-combining`. That
  downstream concept teaches deliberately choosing clause order and
  combination method for stylistic effect, building directly on this
  concept's flexible-clause-order principle (TA-3/TA-5) — per the
  Blueprint's own explicit "anchor and kite" reuse mandate.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Apply the standalone test to sentences containing subordinating
   conjunctions, confirming the subordinate clause fails the test
   (dependent) while the main clause passes (independent).
2. Recognize common subordinating conjunctions (because, although,
   since, while, when, if, unless, after, before, whenever) as signal
   words that introduce a dependent clause, contrasted with the seven
   coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS).
3. Apply the correct comma rule for a dependent clause regardless of
   its position — comma after an introductory dependent clause; no
   comma when the dependent clause follows the independent clause.
4. Distinguish complex sentences from simple and compound sentences in
   a mixed batch, checking for both coordinating and subordinating
   conjunctions and applying the standalone test to every clause
   present.
5. Construct original complex sentences with the dependent clause in
   either position, correctly applying the position-dependent comma
   rule in each case.

## Core Understanding

A complex sentence directly extends the clause-independence judgment
already secured for compound sentences to a NEW clause type. Where
compound sentences join TWO independent clauses (each passes the
standalone test), a complex sentence joins ONE independent clause with
ONE OR MORE DEPENDENT clauses — introduced by a subordinating
conjunction (because, although, when, if, since, while) — that FAILS
the standalone test, sounding incomplete when read alone. Not every
clause-joining word signals a compound sentence: coordinating
conjunctions (FANBOYS) join two clauses that each stand alone, while
subordinating conjunctions create a clause that CANNOT stand alone,
making the resulting sentence complex, not compound. A second,
independent, load-bearing insight concerns WORD ORDER: the dependent
clause can appear BEFORE or AFTER the independent clause — the sentence
is complex either way, with identical meaning. What genuinely changes
based on position is only the COMMA: when the dependent clause comes
first (introductory), it is followed by a comma ("Because it was
raining, we stayed inside"); when the independent clause comes first, no
comma is typically needed before the following dependent clause ("We
stayed inside because it was raining"). The dependent clause never
"becomes" independent regardless of which side it sits on — its
inability to stand alone is a property of its own internal structure,
not its position in the sentence.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "any joining word between two clauses makes a
  sentence compound, and the dependent clause must always come first"**:
  the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct
  — a direct extension of the just-learned compound-sentence rule
  (over-applied to subordinating conjunctions too), compounded by a
  second, genuinely new assumption from having primarily seen or
  practiced dependent-clause-first examples early in instruction.
- **Intermediate model — "I apply the standalone test to subordinating-
  conjunction sentences, confirming the dependent clause fails while
  the independent clause passes"**: installed by TA-1 and TA-2, using
  the Component 3 Anchor and Kite anchor, resolving
  MC-A-SENTENCE-WITH-A-SUBORDINATING-WORD-IS-COMPOUND. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly classifying a fresh subordinating-conjunction sentence as
  complex, not compound, without hesitation.
- **Advanced model — "I apply the correct comma rule for a dependent
  clause regardless of its position"**: installed by TA-3, resolving
  MC-THE-DEPENDENT-CLAUSE-MUST-ALWAYS-COME-FIRST by directly
  demonstrating the same sentence, with the same meaning, correctly
  punctuated in both orders.
- **Expert model — "I distinguish complex from compound and simple
  sentences in mixed batches, and I construct original complex
  sentences with the dependent clause in either position, correctly
  punctuated"**: installed by TA-4 and TA-5, the concept's genuine
  mastery bar — applying both disciplines together and actively
  constructing, not merely classifying.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing
  (S9), do not certify mastery unless MP-1 (subordinating-word rejection)
  and MP-2 (position-based comma rule) both pass — a learner who
  recognizes subordinating conjunctions in isolation (MP-3) but still
  misclassifies sentence type or misapplies the comma rule by position
  has not yet integrated the full judgment this concept requires.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and the Blueprint
itself explicitly frames the first as a direct, predictable over-
extension of the just-learned prerequisite concept. The subordinating-
word-means-compound failure exists because the learner has JUST secured
"a joining word between two clauses signals compound" from the
prerequisite concept — extending that rule to subordinating
conjunctions too is a reasonable, near-inevitable generalization
absent explicit contrast, with nothing marking the dependent-clause
exception until a learner directly applies the standalone test to a
subordinate clause and finds it genuinely incomplete alone. The
dependent-clause-must-come-first failure exists because early examples
in most instruction sequences DO tend to place the dependent clause
first (a natural, common rhetorical pattern) — extending that observed
pattern into a fixed rule is a reasonable generalization from limited
exposure, until a learner directly compares the same sentence,
identical in meaning, correctly constructed with the dependent clause
in either position.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.complex-sentences.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with

(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-A-SENTENCE-WITH-A-SUBORDINATING-WORD-IS-COMPOUND (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature, and explicitly
  identified by the Blueprint itself as arising "having just learned
  compound sentences" — the freshly-secured rule (joining word between
  clauses = compound) is reasonably, if incorrectly, extended to
  subordinating conjunctions too, since both coordinating and
  subordinating conjunctions genuinely DO join clauses, just with
  structurally different results.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a fresh
  subordinating-conjunction sentence, spontaneously tests each clause
  alone for standalone completeness before classifying, rather than
  reacting to the joining word's mere presence, is showing the
  clause-independence discipline has generalized from compound to
  complex sentences.

**MC-THE-DEPENDENT-CLAUSE-MUST-ALWAYS-COME-FIRST (Blueprint Component
1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — early
  instructional examples genuinely do tend to place the dependent
  clause first, making that a reasonable pattern to generalize from
  limited exposure; the misconception survives because dependent-
  clause-first examples are common and rhetorically natural, delaying
  the moment a dependent-clause-second example directly contradicts the
  fixed-order assumption.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when constructing
  a fresh complex sentence with the dependent clause second,
  spontaneously omits the unnecessary comma (rather than inserting one
  out of habit from the first-position pattern) is showing the
  position-dependent comma rule has generalized beyond the specific
  TA-3 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the Anchor and the Kite itself is the analogy, not a
  supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): an "anchor" card
  (independent clause, self-sufficient, sits complete on its own) and a
  "kite" card (dependent clause, needs a string attached to the anchor
  or it flies away incomplete) — with the kite shown attaching on
  EITHER side of the anchor, still working, still a kite regardless of
  position — directly embodies both misconception fixes in one unified
  demonstration: the kite never becomes an anchor no matter how it's
  introduced, and the kite can attach on either side. Breaking point:
  the anchor-and-kite anchor demonstrates both insights well but
  doesn't on its own address the mixed-classification discrimination
  task (TA-4), which needs its own separate practice distinguishing
  complex sentences from BOTH simple and compound sentences together.
- **Alternative — a trailer hitched to a truck, front or back doesn't
  matter, but it can never drive itself**: a trailer (dependent clause)
  can be hitched in front of or behind the truck (independent clause)
  in a train of vehicles and the overall rig still works either way,
  but the trailer itself never gains its own engine (never becomes
  independent) regardless of position. Breaking point: useful for the
  flexible-position-but-still-dependent insight, but doesn't address
  the coordinating-vs-subordinating conjunction distinction, which the
  anchor-and-kite anchor's own TA-1/TA-2 practice handles directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe subordinating conjunctions as "just
  another kind of joining word, like 'and' or 'but'"**: this framing
  directly risks reinforcing
  MC-A-SENTENCE-WITH-A-SUBORDINATING-WORD-IS-COMPOUND by collapsing the
  coordinating/subordinating distinction into one undifferentiated
  category of "words that join clauses"; the two categories produce
  structurally different results (two independent clauses vs. one
  independent plus one dependent) and must be taught as distinct from
  the first introduction, not as variants of the same thing.

- **Physical/visual (the Anchor and the Kite)**: the Blueprint's

- **Home/no-equipment**: any genuine cause-and-effect or condition-
  and-result relationship the learner can state supplies real TA-1/TA-2

- **Teacher-demo (the standalone test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — testing "Because

  standalone sentence, making the dependent-clause failure directly

  test already established for compound sentences.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  classification, comma-rule application, or sentence-construction

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
subordinating-conjunction-creates-dependent-clause insight and the
flexible-position-comma-rule insight are both genuine, directly
testable phenomena well-suited to guided discovery: **need** (the
learner, having secured TA-1's standalone-test practice from compound
sentences, is shown "Because the game was cancelled, the fans went home
disappointed" and asked whether "because the game was cancelled" is a
compound-sentence-style joined clause, discovering it fails the
standalone test the same way a genuine dependent clause should);
**playground/invention** (TA-1's standalone-test practice and TA-2's
subordinating-conjunction recognition build initial confidence with the
base classification before the position-flexibility complication is
introduced); **collision** (the Blueprint's own compound-vs-complex
pair breaks the subordinating-word-means-compound assumption; the
same-sentence-both-orders pair breaks the dependent-clause-must-come-
first assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's original-construction task
in both orders, requiring articulation of both the clause-
classification insight and the position-based comma rule); **compression**
(TA-4's fluent mixed-batch classification, applying both insights
directly without each instance separately re-derived). The specific
terms "subordinating conjunction," "dependent clause," and "complex
sentence" are convention-bound grammatical terminology best introduced
by direct instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying
distinctions through the anchor-and-kite demonstration and TA-1–2.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (the standalone test for subordinating conjunctions) is placed
first as the concept's most direct extension of the prerequisite
compound-sentences concept's own test, per the Blueprint's own Anchor
and Kite anchor — re-applying the standalone-test discipline to a new
clause type before any vocabulary or position complication is
introduced. TA-2 (recognizing common subordinating conjunctions) builds
the specific vocabulary needed to spot dependent clauses, contrasted
explicitly with the FANBOYS coordinating conjunctions already secured.
TA-3 (dependent clause position and the comma rule) directly targets
MC-THE-DEPENDENT-CLAUSE-MUST-ALWAYS-COME-FIRST, addressing a SECOND,
independent dimension (position/punctuation, not clause-type
classification) once the base subordinating-conjunction recognition
from TA-1/TA-2 is secure. TA-4 (distinguishing complex from compound
and simple) is placed next as an integration-level classification task,
requiring both the standalone test AND the coordinating-vs-
subordinating conjunction check across a mixed batch. TA-5
(constructing original complex sentences in both orders) is placed last
as the concept's generative capstone, requiring the learner to actively
CONSTRUCT sentences in both dependent-clause positions with correct
punctuation. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-
turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously applies the
  standalone test to a fresh subordinating-conjunction sentence before
  classifying (rather than reacting to the joining word alone), and
  whether the learner spontaneously applies the correct, position-
  dependent comma rule for a fresh complex sentence regardless of
  clause order — these are the primary diagnostic signals for whether
  TA-1 and TA-3's disciplines have genuinely generalized beyond the
  specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before classifying a
  fresh subordinating-conjunction sentence, followed by a correct
  "complex, not compound" judgment based on the standalone test,
  suggests the learner is consciously applying the test rather than
  reacting to the joining word — a good sign, directly inherited from
  the prerequisite concept. Instant, confident insertion of a comma
  before a dependent clause that follows the independent clause
  (without checking position) is the concerning pattern for
  MC-THE-DEPENDENT-CLAUSE-MUST-ALWAYS-COME-FIRST.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: not specifically flagged for L1-
  transfer in this Blueprint — both misconceptions here stem from
  genuine, predictable overgeneralization from prior instruction
  sequencing (Type 1) rather than L1-specific structural transfer, so
  no S9-specific routing beyond standard practice depth is called for.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does this clause make a complete
  sentence all by itself?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for

  fresh original complex sentences in both orders.
- **Far**: this concept's flexible-clause-order principle and its

  `eng.grammar.sentence-combining`'s deliberate stylistic use of clause

- **Real-world**: every multi-clause sentence the learner constructs —
  in speech or writing — depends on correctly distinguishing
  independent from dependent clauses and correctly applying the
  position-based comma rule.
- **Expert-transfer**: varied, sophisticated sentence construction at
  any advanced level depends on the ability to deliberately choose
  dependent-clause position for rhetorical emphasis (front-loading a
  condition for suspense, or back-loading a reason for a punchier main
  point) — a stylistic choice that presupposes the learner can reliably
  produce either order with correct punctuation, not merely default to
  one habitual pattern.
