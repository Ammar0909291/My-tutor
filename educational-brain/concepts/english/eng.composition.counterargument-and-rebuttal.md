## Learning Objective
The learner will identify the strongest opposing view to their own claim, state it fairly in its own terms, and then rebut it with specific evidence or reasoning — rather than either ignoring opposition or attacking a weakened version of it.

## Core Understanding
A counterargument is a deliberately invited opposing view, stated as strongly and fairly as the arguer can manage, followed by a rebuttal that directly engages its substance. This is not a concession that weakens the writer's position — it is what makes an argument credible, because it proves the writer understands the terrain well enough to have tested their claim against its best challenge. A rebuttal that defeats a weak or invented version of the opposition (a straw man) proves nothing, because the real opposing view is untouched. The two-step discipline is: (1) find and state the STRONGEST real objection, in language its holder would recognize as fair, (2) answer THAT objection specifically — with evidence, a distinction, or a limiting condition — not with a restatement of the original claim.

## Mental Models
- **Boxing-Champion Anchor**: A boxing champion who only fights weak opponents never proves anything — the title means nothing until they beat the best challenger. An argument that only "defeats" a weak version of the opposing view is a title claimed against a weak opponent. Choosing the strongest real objection and beating that is what earns credibility.
- **Devil's Advocate framing**: Before rebutting, the writer must temporarily argue AS IF they held the opposing view, well enough that a real holder of that view would say "yes, that's what I think" — only then is the rebuttal aimed at something real.

## Why Students Fail
Learners default to picking the easiest opposing view to defeat, because the goal feels like "winning" rather than "testing." Confusing a rebuttal with re-assertion happens because restating the original claim more forcefully feels like a response, when it has not engaged the objection's specific mechanism at all.

## Misconceptions
- **MC-A (Type 1, overgeneralization from "make my argument look good")**: "I should pick the weakest opposing argument so I can easily defeat it." Diagnostic: ask the learner to state the counterargument in one sentence — if it's a claim no reasonable opponent would actually hold, this misconception is active. Repair: supply the strongest published or commonly-heard version of the opposition and have the learner rebut THAT.
- **MC-B (Type 5, instruction-induced from vague "respond to the other side" prompts)**: "Rebuttal means restating my original position more forcefully." Diagnostic: does the rebuttal name a specific weakness, limit, or missing evidence in the counterargument, or does it just repeat the thesis? Repair: require the rebuttal to name the counterargument's specific claim before responding to it — "the objection assumes X, but X fails because Y."

## Analogies
- Boxing-champion analogy (above) for choosing the strongest opponent.
- A legal cross-examination: a lawyer who ignores the opposing witness's actual testimony and just repeats their own opening statement has not cross-examined anything.
- Anti-analogy: this is not "being fair for its own sake" — the counterargument step exists to make the REBUTTAL more convincing, not to soften the writer's position into wishy-washy neutrality.

## Demonstrations
- Take a real claim (e.g., "school should start later") and have the learner brainstorm the single best objection a thoughtful opponent would raise, write it as that opponent would phrase it, then rebut it with a specific mechanism (evidence, distinction, or limiting condition).
- Show a straw-man rebuttal side-by-side with a real-opposition rebuttal on the same claim, and ask which one would actually change a skeptical reader's mind.

## Discovery Questions
- "If someone who disagreed with you read your counterargument paragraph, would they say 'yes, that's what I think' — or would they say 'that's not my argument'?"
- "What's the single strongest reason someone smart could disagree with you? Not the easiest one to knock down — the hardest."
- "Does your rebuttal answer the specific objection, or does it just say your original point again in different words?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Anchor: Boxing-champion framing — beating a weak opponent proves nothing.
2. Model: state a claim, generate 2 possible objections (one weak, one strong), show why the strong one is the right pick.
3. Guided: learner picks the strongest objection to their own thesis and states it fairly.
4. Guided: learner drafts a rebuttal, checked against MC-B (does it name the objection's specific claim before responding?).
5. Independent: full counterargument-and-rebuttal paragraph on a new claim.
6. Confirm: learner explains, in their own words, why picking the strongest objection matters.

## Tutor Actions
- SHOW: a real straw-man vs. real-opposition rebuttal pair.
- DO: have the learner voice the opposing view aloud "as that person," to test fairness.
- TEST-THINKING: ask the learner to predict what a real opponent would say back to their rebuttal.
- ORGANIZE: a two-column structure — "Their strongest claim" / "My specific answer" — to prevent restatement drift.

## Voice Teaching Notes
Watch for a rushed, dismissive tone when a learner states the opposing view aloud — a genuinely fair statement of an opposing view usually takes a beat longer to phrase carefully than a caricature does. Register should stay respectful even when the learner clearly disagrees with the position being stated.

## Assessment Signals
- Probe: "Rewrite this straw-man counterargument so a real opponent would agree it's fair." Correct answer strengthens the objection's actual claim; MC-A response leaves it a caricature.
- Probe: "Is this rebuttal answering the objection, or just repeating the thesis?" — given a rebuttal that restates the claim, learner should identify MC-B.
- Confident-wrong signature: a fluently-written but straw-manned counterargument, delivered with no hedging — flag as MC-A regardless of prose quality.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If a learner keeps picking weak objections after correction, don't just say "pick a stronger one" again — supply a real, sourced opposing argument on the same topic (a genuine op-ed line or common belief) and have them rebut that concrete example, since the failure is often not knowing what a strong objection looks like, not unwillingness.

## Memory Hooks
The boxing-champion image ("beating a weak opponent proves nothing") is the retrievable hook for MC-A; the two-column "their claim / my specific answer" structure is the retrievable hook for MC-B.
