# Semantic Fields — `eng.vocab.semantic-fields`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.semantic-fields` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Semantic Fields" — groups
  of words related in meaning that structure a shared conceptual domain
  (such as vocabulary for emotions or weather), distinct from simple
  synonym sets or words that merely tend to appear together. This is
  the final concept of the `eng.vocab` domain in the current curriculum
  sequence.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.thesaurus-and-dictionary-skills`,
  `eng.vocab.etymology` — both load-bearing: the reference-tool skill
  of looking up precise word meanings and finding synonyms is the
  foundation for distinguishing a semantic field's non-synonymous
  members from a true synonym set; etymology's word-history tracing
  provides the further insight that a domain's words can have entirely
  separate historical origins yet still share a conceptual field, per
  the Blueprint's own explicit Adaptive Flag naming this transfer.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none — this concept is terminal in the current
  curriculum sequence, closing out the `eng.vocab` domain.
- **Difficulty**: advanced · **Bloom**: analyze · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Distinguish a semantic field (a set of distinct, often non-
   synonymous words all connected to one shared conceptual domain) from
   a synonym set (words that mean nearly the same thing).
2. Distinguish genuine semantic-field membership from mere collocation
   (words that frequently appear near each other without sharing the
   same conceptual domain).
3. Generate a list of words that genuinely belong to a given conceptual
   domain, excluding words that are merely contextually associated with
   it.
4. Identify and exclude a false member from a candidate semantic-field
   word list — a word that merely co-occurs with the domain rather than
   belonging to it.
5. Fully analyze a genuinely unfamiliar conceptual domain, confirming
   shared-domain membership without synonymy, excluding merely-
   associated words, and producing a correctly mapped semantic field.

## Core Understanding

A semantic field is a group of distinct words connected not by shared
meaning but by belonging to the same broader conceptual domain — the
"emotion" semantic field includes "joy," "anger," "fear," and
"sadness," none of which are synonyms of each other, the same way a
family shares a real connection (they're related) without its members
needing to look identical. This is genuinely different from a synonym
set (words like "happy," "joyful," "elated," and "delighted," which DO
mean nearly the same thing) — a semantic field's defining test is
shared conceptual DOMAIN, not shared meaning. A second, equally load-
bearing fact is that genuine semantic-field membership is also distinct
from mere COLLOCATION — words that frequently co-occur in the same
contexts without actually sharing the same conceptual domain: "umbrella"
often appears alongside "rain," but "umbrella" is an object/tool, not a
weather phenomenon, the same way "lifeguard" and "ocean" are always
mentioned together in context despite one being a person and the other
a body of water. Frequent co-occurrence signals contextual association,
not shared conceptual domain, and a genuine semantic field must be
built by asking whether candidate words describe the same KIND of
thing, not merely whether they tend to appear near each other.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "a semantic field is just a longer list of
  synonyms for one word, or a list of words that show up together in
  the same context"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this
  concept exists to correct — a natural conflation of "semantic field"
  with the two more familiar lexical relationships the learner already
  knows (synonym sets from the domain's earlier concepts, collocation
  from further back), since "words related to each other" is the vague,
  shared surface description of all three genuinely distinct
  relationships.
- **Intermediate model — "I can distinguish a semantic field's shared-
  domain-not-shared-meaning structure from a true synonym set"**:
  installed by TA-1's practice, using the Component 3 family-
  resemblance anchor to make the shared-domain-without-shared-meaning
  principle concrete, resolving MC-A-A-SEMANTIC-FIELD-IS-JUST-A-LIST-
  OF-SYNONYMS-FOR-ONE-WORD. Upgrade trigger: correctly explaining WHY a
  fresh semantic field's members are not synonyms despite sharing a
  domain, rather than only confirming that they aren't.
- **Advanced model — "I can distinguish genuine shared-domain membership
  from mere collocation"**: installed by TA-2's practice, using the
  Component 3 lifeguard-vs-ocean anchor, resolving MC-B-ANY-WORDS-THAT-
  OFTEN-APPEAR-TOGETHER-IN-THE-SAME-CONTEXT-BELONG-TO-THE-SAME-
  SEMANTIC-FIELD. Upgrade trigger: correctly rejecting a frequently-
  co-occurring but conceptually-different word (like "umbrella" for
  weather) from a semantic field, without being told the word doesn't
  belong.
- **Expert model — "I can generate a genuine semantic field for a
  conceptual domain, correctly excluding both false-synonym
  assumptions and merely-associated collocations, for a genuinely
  unfamiliar domain"**: installed by TA-3's field-mapping practice and
  TA-4's full-synthesis task, requiring both TA-1's and TA-2's
  disciplines applied together productively. This is the concept's
  genuine mastery bar and the capstone of the entire `eng.vocab` domain.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, a
  learner who correctly distinguishes fields from synonym sets on
  provided examples (MP-1) but reverts to synonym-style thinking when
  mapping a genuinely new domain (MP-5) needs a MANDATORY "do these
  words mean the same thing, or just share a category?" check applied
  to every candidate word — this pattern indicates the distinction
  hasn't yet generalized to independent, unscaffolded mapping.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of the two more familiar lexical relationships this concept
must be carefully distinguished from. The synonym-conflation failure
exists because the domain's earlier concepts (synonyms/antonyms,
connotation/denotation, thesaurus skills) have all trained the learner
to look for shared-meaning relationships among related words, so when a
new "group of related words" (a semantic field) is introduced, the
learner reasonably applies the same shared-meaning expectation, with
nothing marking that THIS particular relationship (shared domain) works
by a fundamentally different criterion until directly tested against a
clear counterexample like joy/anger/fear/sadness. The collocation-
conflation failure exists because frequent co-occurrence is a genuinely
powerful, salient perceptual signal — words that always appear together
(umbrella, rain) FEEL deeply connected, and nothing in that felt
connection distinguishes "these words are contextually associated" from
"these words belong to the same conceptual category" until the two
words' actual KIND (object vs. weather phenomenon) is explicitly
examined and compared.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.vocab.semantic-fields.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-A-A-SEMANTIC-FIELD-IS-JUST-A-LIST-OF-SYNONYMS-FOR-ONE-WORD
(Blueprint Component 1) — Type 3, language contamination**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a Type 3 signature — the domain's own
  earlier, correctly-learned synonym-set concept (a genuinely different
  but adjacent lexical relationship) intrudes into this new domain,
  where a structurally different relationship (shared conceptual
  category, not shared meaning) actually applies; the learner is not
  misapplying a wrong rule, but rather correctly applying a genuinely-
  learned category from an earlier, adjacent concept into a context
  where a different category actually fits.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when given a fresh
  semantic field, spontaneously asks "do these actually mean the same
  thing, or do they just share a category?" before assuming synonymy is
  showing the shared-domain-not-shared-meaning model has generalized
  beyond the specific TA-1 practice fields.

**MC-B-ANY-WORDS-THAT-OFTEN-APPEAR-TOGETHER-IN-THE-SAME-CONTEXT-
BELONG-TO-THE-SAME-SEMANTIC-FIELD (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 2,
perceptual intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a genuine perceptual default rather than
  a wrongly-taught rule — frequent co-occurrence is a powerful,
  perceptually salient signal of "relatedness" in general, and nothing
  about that salient perceptual signal itself distinguishes contextual
  association from genuine shared-category membership; the learner is
  reasonably relying on a real perceptual cue that simply doesn't
  determine the specific relationship this concept requires.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when given a
  frequently-co-occurring word pair, spontaneously asks "is this
  actually the SAME KIND of thing, or just something that shows up
  alongside it?" before including it in a semantic field is showing the
  domain-verification discipline has generalized beyond the specific
  TA-2 practice pairs.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the family-resemblance and lifeguard-vs-ocean anchor
  itself is the analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint
  Component 3): a family whose members share a real connection without
  looking identical directly embodies MC-A's fix (shared domain, not
  shared meaning); "lifeguard" and "ocean" — always mentioned together
  in context, but one a person and one a body of water, not the same
  kind of thing at all — directly embodies MC-B's fix. Breaking point:
  a family's "connection" (shared ancestry/relation) is a single,
  unambiguous kind of relatedness, which can understate that some
  semantic-field boundaries genuinely are debatable at the edges (is a
  specific borderline word part of the "weather" field or not?) — this
  entry treats the family-resemblance anchor as the introductory frame
  for the dominant, clear-cut pattern, not a claim that every semantic-
  field boundary is unambiguous.
- **Alternative — a sports team's roster vs. players who happen to be
  friends off the field**: a team's roster (a genuine shared-category
  membership — all "on this team") is different from a group of
  players who happen to socialize together off the field (an
  associational relationship, not team membership). Breaking point:
  useful for reinforcing the category-membership-vs-mere-association
  distinction, but the lifeguard-vs-ocean anchor more sharply and
  memorably captures the specific PERSON-vs-THING categorical
  mismatch central to MC-B.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe a semantic field's non-synonymous
  members as "loosely related" or "vaguely connected"**: framing
  semantic-field membership as a fuzzy, weak relationship undersells
  the genuine, structural, well-defined nature of shared conceptual
  domains; "joy" and "anger" belong to the SAME field just as
  definitively and non-arbitrarily as "happy" and "joyful" are
  synonyms — the relationship type differs, but neither is a "looser"
  version of the other.

- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
shared-domain-not-shared-meaning principle and the domain-vs-
collocation distinction are both genuine, directly felt phenomena
well-suited to guided discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured
thesaurus/dictionary and etymology skills, is asked whether "joy" and
"anger" mean the same thing, even though both are "emotion" words);
**playground/invention** (TA-1's practice across the emotion domain
lets the learner notice the shared-category-without-shared-meaning
pattern); **collision** (the Blueprint's own joy/anger contrast breaks
the synonym-conflation assumption; the umbrella/rain contrast breaks
the collocation-conflation assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's full
analysis, requiring explicit statement of both distinctions applied to
a genuinely unfamiliar domain); **compression** (TA-4's fluent full-
synthesis application without each check separately re-derived). The
specific term "semantic field" itself is convention-bound linguistic
terminology best introduced by direct instruction once the learner has
already felt the underlying shared-domain distinction through the
anchor and TA-1.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (semantic fields vs. synonym sets) is placed first, directly
targeting MC-A, because distinguishing shared-domain from shared-
meaning is the concept's most foundational reframe — a learner must
first accept that field members needn't be synonyms before the
collocation distinction (TA-2) can even be meaningfully posed (since
otherwise the learner might simply substitute "loosely similar
meaning" for "synonym" as the field-membership test, missing the
domain-based criterion entirely). TA-2 (semantic fields vs.
collocation) follows, directly targeting MC-B — per the Blueprint's own
protocol routing, these two misconceptions are "largely independent and
often need separate, explicit correction," so TA-2 is sequenced as its
own distinct instructional step. TA-3 (mapping a semantic field)
extends both TA-1's and TA-2's disciplines into productive field-
generation, requiring the learner to actively include genuine members
and exclude both false-synonym-expectation errors and collocation
errors. TA-4 (full application: analyzing an unfamiliar semantic field)
is placed last as the concept's complete synthesis task and the
capstone of the entire `eng.vocab` domain, requiring TA-1's domain-not-
meaning test, TA-2's domain-not-collocation test, and TA-3's productive
mapping skill all applied together to a genuinely novel conceptual
domain. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn
session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously checks "do
  these mean the same thing, or just share a category?" and "is this
  actually the same KIND of thing, or just associated with it?" when
  building or evaluating a fresh semantic field — these are the primary
  diagnostic signals for whether TA-1 and TA-2's disciplines have
  genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice materials.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before excluding a
  frequently-co-occurring but conceptually-mismatched word (like
  "umbrella" for weather), followed by a correct exclusion with
  reasoning, suggests the learner is consciously applying TA-2's
  domain-verification test rather than including everything that feels
  contextually connected — a good sign. Instant, confident inclusion of
  every frequently-associated word with no domain check is the
  concerning pattern for MC-B.
- **Field-mapping stakes**: real and central to TA-3/TA-4 — this
  concept's mastery bar requires PRODUCTIVE field generation, not just
  receptive recognition, so the tutor should ensure the learner
  actively generates candidate words rather than only confirming or
  rejecting tutor-provided lists.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "do these words mean the same thing, or
  just share a broader category?" is this concept's load-bearing
  sentence for MC-A, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh semantic field is introduced, making the domain-
  not-meaning test a routinely-invited response rather than a default
  synonym assumption.
