# Rhyming — `eng.phonics.rhyming`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.phonics.rhyming` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / phonics / "Rhyming" — the first
  concept beyond the zero-prerequisite floor on the phonemic-awareness
  branch, sitting directly downstream of `eng.phonics.phonemic-
  awareness`.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.phonics.phonemic-awareness` — specifically the
  ability to isolate the first sound of a spoken word
  (`eng.phonics.phonemic-awareness.md`, Learning objectives 1–3) and to
  hold "sound" as a phoneme distinct from a whole word or syllable
  (that entry's P1 misconception, "sound means loudness or
  noise-in-general," must already be resolved). Rhyming additionally
  requires isolating the sound at the END of a word, not just the
  start — a genuinely new application of the same underlying
  segmentation skill, load-bearing enough that this entry's own PD-1
  diagnostic (below) checks it explicitly before teaching begins.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.phonics.blending-segmenting`. Rhyming's
  end-sound isolation is a direct rehearsal for blending/segmenting's
  requirement to manipulate sounds at any position in a word, not just
  the first — this concept is the bridge between "sounds exist and can
  be found at the start" (phonemic-awareness) and "sounds anywhere in a
  word can be isolated and manipulated" (blending-segmenting).
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: remember · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.80 · **Est. hours**: 1
- **Learning objectives** — the learner can:
  1. Identify, from three spoken words, which two share an ending sound
     and which one does not — by ear alone, no print involved.
  2. Generate multiple words (real or invented) that share a given
     ending sound, demonstrating the rule generalizes beyond one
     matched example.
  3. Correctly judge a rhyme pair whose SPELLING misleadingly agrees or
     disagrees with its actual sound (e.g. "love"/"move" — similar
     spelling, no rhyme; "bear"/"chair" — different spelling, true
     rhyme), reasoning from sound alone.
  4. Distinguish rhyme (shared ENDING sound) from alliteration (shared
     STARTING sound) when given a mixed set of word pairs.
  5. State, in the learner's own words, the ear-based checking
     procedure — not a memorized definition, an operational habit.

## Core Understanding

Two words rhyme when their final stressed vowel and everything that
follows it — the linguistic "rime" — are phonetically identical,
regardless of how either word is spelled and regardless of how many
syllables precede that shared ending. This definition has two parts a
learner must hold simultaneously and neither part is optional: the
comparison is auditory, not visual (spelling is causally irrelevant to
whether two words rhyme, even though English spelling frequently
correlates with sound closely enough to make the shortcut feel safe
until it fails), and the comparison targets the END of the word
specifically, not any other position (a word sharing its START sound
with another is demonstrating a different, well-named phenomenon —
alliteration — not rhyme). Every misconception this concept encounters
is a violation of one of these two constraints: judging by eye instead
of by ear, or checking the wrong end of the word.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "some word-pairs just 'go together' when said
  aloud"**: an unanalyzed, global sense of phonological similarity,
  without yet applying a specific positional check. This model is
  genuinely pre-instructional rather than taught — children reliably
  show implicit sensitivity to rhyme (e.g. preferring rhyming word
  pairs in listening tasks) before they can explicitly justify a rhyme
  judgment. It is sufficient for passive recognition tasks with heavy
  modeling but not yet for independent generation (objective 2) or for
  resisting the spelling trap (objective 3), both of which require an
  explicit, statable procedure rather than a felt impression. Shelf-life
  warning to deliver at installation (Universal Principle 6,
  `../../foundations/04-universal-teaching-principles.md`): "right now
  we're just noticing when words 'sound the same' at the end — soon
  you'll be able to explain exactly WHY, and even make up your own
  rhymes."
- **Intermediate model — "rhyme means the ending SOUND matches, checked
  by listening"**: the correct, operational model — the learner
  actively says both words aloud (or attends closely if spoken by
  someone else) and compares specifically the final sound-chunk,
  independent of spelling. Upgrade trigger: the first spelling-trap item
  (WE-3/MP-3, "love"/"move" or "bear"/"chair") — a learner operating
  under the beginner model's vague "goes together" sense has no
  principled way to resolve these deliberately misleading pairs; only
  the explicit ending-sound check does.
- **Advanced model — "rhyme is one KIND of sound-match; matching at the
  start is a different, separately-named thing"**: the learner holds
  rhyme and alliteration as two distinct categories defined by WHERE
  the match occurs, rather than treating "sounds similar somehow" as
  one undifferentiated bucket. Upgrade trigger: TA-3's explicit
  three-way sort (rhymes / starts-the-same / neither) — this task
  cannot be passed under the intermediate model alone, since that model
  correctly identifies rhymes but has no mechanism for explicitly
  rejecting a start-matching distractor as "a different thing," only as
  "not a rhyme."
- **Expert model — "rhyme is a structural device with degrees and
  types"** (well past this node, informing later `eng.literature` work
  on poetry): eventual awareness of near-rhyme/slant-rhyme and rhyme's
  role in verse structure — not needed for mastery at this node, but
  the direction the beginner→advanced arc is heading.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner who cannot yet reliably perform
  the intermediate model's ending-check on clear, non-tricky pairs
  should not be given spelling-trap items "to see if they can" — the
  spelling trap specifically tests whether the ear-based check has
  displaced a visual shortcut, which presupposes the ear-based check
  already exists to be tested.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, each tied to a
different one of Core Understanding's two constraints. The
spelling-based failure exists because it is a genuinely useful heuristic
gone wrong: for a very large fraction of ordinary English word pairs,
similar spelling and similar sound co-occur (cat/hat/bat all share both
spelling pattern and sound), so "check the letters" is a real,
frequently-correct shortcut a print-exposed learner discovers on their
own — the failure is not laziness, it is a validated strategy applied
past its silent boundary. The alliteration-confusion failure exists for
a different, curriculum-shaped reason: early phonological-awareness
instruction typically introduces rhyming songs and alliterative games
(tongue-twisters, "Peter Piper") in close proximity, under a shared
loose banner of "sound play," without ever explicitly naming that
these are two DIFFERENT skills distinguished by WHICH end of the word
must match — a learner who has correctly absorbed "listen for a sound
match" but was never told there are two positions to check reasonably
treats any felt similarity as sufficient.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.phonics.rhyming.md`, Component 1) already documents two
misconceptions with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination
content — reused by reference below, not re-copied, with the birth-type
classification (`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`) added since
the Blueprint predates that taxonomy and did not classify them. A third
misconception, discovered by applying the taxonomy's diagnostic
procedure (`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md §7`) directly to
this concept, is new content the Blueprint does not contain.

**MC-SPELLING-MUST-MATCH (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: "similar spelling implies similar sound"
  is not an arbitrary error — it is genuinely, statistically true for
  the majority of simple English word families a beginning reader meets
  first (cat/hat/bat/mat), which is exactly Type 1's signature: a rule
  validated in a real, common prior domain (regular spelling-sound
  correspondence) extended into a domain where it silently breaks
  (English's many irregular spelling-sound mismatches). Asked directly
  about a regular family, the learner will apply the rule correctly and
  confidently — the error surfaces only at the irregular boundary.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition — free detection channel)*: a
  learner who, unprompted, justifies a rhyme judgment by referencing
  letters ("they both have 'ove' at the end") rather than by re-saying
  the words aloud is showing this misconception even before a formal
  spelling-trap probe is run — the justification channel itself is
  diagnostic, independent of whether the answer happens to be correct.

**MC-FIRST-SOUND-IS-ENOUGH (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 5,
instruction-induced**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: this is a curriculum-shaped confusion,
  not an individually-varying one (Type 5's defining contrast with
  Type 2) — it appears near-universally across learners exposed to
  early "sound play" instruction that bundles rhyming and alliteration
  together (nursery rhymes and tongue-twisters taught in close
  proximity) without ever naming that WHICH END matches is what
  distinguishes them. Per Type 5's mechanism, the narrow taught case
  ("sounds matching somewhere = a match") was never given its boundary,
  so the learner reasonably generalized it.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: per Type 5's repair implication
  (`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md §5`), this confusion is
  fully preventable by naming the boundary explicitly the FIRST time
  rhyme is introduced ("rhyme is specifically an ENDING match — there's
  a different name, alliteration, for words that match at the start")
  — a tutor who names this distinction before TA-3 rather than only
  after a wrong answer is closing the gap at its actual source, not
  merely correcting a symptom.

**MC-SYLLABLE-COUNT-MUST-MATCH (new content, not in the existing
Blueprint) — Type 5, instruction-induced**
- *Why*: if a learner's early rhyme exposure consistently used
  same-syllable-count exemplar pairs (most nursery-rhyme pairs happen
  to be single-syllable: cat/hat, sun/fun), the learner may reasonably
  but incorrectly conclude that matching syllable COUNT is part of the
  definition — a narrow teaching case whose boundary (syllable count is
  irrelevant; only the ending sound matters) was never named. This is
  distinct in mechanism and symptom from both Blueprint misconceptions:
  it is neither a spelling-based error nor a wrong-position error, but
  a wrong ADDITIONAL constraint layered onto an otherwise-correct
  ending check.
- *Symptom / phrase*: asked whether "cat" and "butterfly" rhyme (they
  do not, so this is a fair rejection) versus "cat" and "acrobat"
  (which DO rhyme despite differing syllable counts), a learner showing
  this misconception rejects the second pair too, often justifying it
  with "they don't match because one is longer."
- *Detection probe (verbatim)*: "Do 'cat' and 'acrobat' rhyme? One word
  is much longer than the other — does that matter? Say the ENDINGS
  out loud: '...cat' and '...bat'. Do those endings sound the same?"
- *Recovery*: per Type 5's collision design, treat this explicitly as a
  boundary-naming promotion, not a correction: "good instinct that
  you're checking something — but length doesn't matter at all for
  rhyming, only the very end. A short word and a long word can rhyme
  just fine, as long as their endings match."
- *Verification*: correct rhyme judgment on at least one pair with
  clearly mismatched syllable counts but a genuinely matching ending
  (e.g. "star"/"superstar"), without hesitation or a length-based
  objection.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — two different songs that happen to end on the same
  few notes**: the songs can start completely differently, but if their
  final notes match, that's the rhyme. Breaking point: "notes" implies
  musical pitch-matching, which is not what rhyme actually requires
  (phoneme identity, not pitch) — the analogy should not be extended to
  suggest singing ability or pitch-matching is relevant to judging
  rhyme.
- **Alternative — two people wearing different hats but the same
  shoes**: the hats (word beginnings) can differ freely; the shoes
  (word endings) must match. Breaking point: implies exactly two
  "parts" of fixed size, which oversimplifies words of very different
  lengths (see MC-SYLLABLE-COUNT-MUST-MATCH above) — useful for
  introducing the beginning/end distinction, not for explaining why
  length is irrelevant.
- **Story analogy**: "Words are little trains — rhyming words have
  totally different engines up front, but the very same caboose at the
  back."
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT use "rhyming words look alike"**: this is not
  a helpful simplification but a direct installation of
  MC-SPELLING-MUST-MATCH, since it frames the visual channel as the
  test rather than the auditory one. If a learner proposes this framing
  themselves, correct it immediately and specifically: "not look alike
  — SOUND alike. Let's always close our eyes and just listen instead
  of picturing the letters," matching the Blueprint's own s6_path
  language.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/auditory (odd-one-out ending-match game)**: the
  Blueprint's Component 3 anchor (three spoken words, two rhyme, one
  does not; hold up two fingers together for the match, one apart for
  the odd one out) — canonical for this concept, referenced not
  restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: the odd-one-out game requires nothing beyond a
  speaking voice; the picture-card variant (Blueprint Component 3) uses
  any simple picture set already in the home.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-2's rhyme-generation task is
  itself an interactive production activity — the learner generates
  candidates (real or invented) and self-checks each by saying the pair
  aloud.
- **Teacher-demo (spelling trap)**: the tutor models saying "love" and
  "move" slowly and deliberately, showing that careful listening — not
  a glance at the spelling — is how the judgment is actually made.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming whether
  a pair rhymes, ask the learner to commit to a guess first ("do you
  think these two rhyme?") — Universal Principle 3, shared with every
  SHOW-family action.

## Discovery Questions

**Discovery wins here — argued, not defaulted to**, in deliberate
contrast to this concept's own prerequisite and sibling nodes, both of
which correctly chose direct instruction for content that is pure,
arbitrary convention. Rhyming is different: the RULE to be found (match
the ending sound, ignore everything before it and ignore spelling
entirely) is a genuine, statable regularity a learner can be led to
induce, not an arbitrary fact requiring only demonstration. The
Blueprint's own TA-1/TA-2/WE-3 sequence already supplies every step of
a genuine discovery design, and this entry makes that structure
explicit: **need** (the odd-one-out anchor creates a real puzzle — two
of three words feel connected, one doesn't, and the learner does not
yet have a stated rule for why); **playground** (TA-1's several
odd-one-out sets let the learner test candidate rules against multiple
examples); **invention** (TA-2 asks the learner to propose new rhyming
words themselves, which requires an implicit working rule even before
it is stated aloud); **collision** (WE-3's spelling-trap pair —
"love"/"move" — directly breaks any rule the learner may have silently
adopted based on spelling, forcing a revision); **formalization** (MP-5
explicitly asks the learner to state the rule in their own words); and
**compression** (fluent, fast rhyme judgment on novel pairs without
re-deriving the rule each time). This is a genuinely different design
choice from both this concept's own prerequisite entries, made because
the underlying content genuinely differs — a discoverable regularity,
not an arbitrary label.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (odd-one-out by ear) must precede TA-2 (generation) because
recognizing a rhyme is a strictly easier task than producing one —
generation requires the learner to search their own vocabulary against
an internalized rule, which is not yet available until recognition has
supplied enough positive and negative examples to build that rule from.
TA-3 (rhyme vs. alliteration contrast) is placed AFTER both recognition
and generation, not folded into TA-1, because the three-way sort
requires the advanced model (rhyme and alliteration as two distinct,
separately-named categories) — introducing this distinction before the
basic ending-check is fluent would compound two new discriminations at
once. The spelling-trap items (WE-3, MP-3) are deliberately placed near
the END of the teaching arc, after TA-1/TA-2 have built confidence in
the ear-based check on straightforward pairs — presenting a spelling
trap before the ear-based check is habitual would simply produce a
guess rather than a genuine test of whether the check has displaced the
visual shortcut. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own
turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References
below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner actually SAYS both words
  aloud before judging, or answers immediately from a silent, purely
  visual read of a picture card or printed word — audibly saying the
  pair is itself evidence the intermediate model (ear-based checking)
  is being actively applied, and its absence is a warning sign even
  when the answer happens to be correct.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a long pause before a spelling-trap
  item (WE-3/MP-3) suggests the learner senses a conflict between two
  competing signals (visual and auditory) and is genuinely working
  through it — a good sign, distinct from a fast, confident WRONG
  answer on the same item, which suggests the visual shortcut has fully
  displaced the ear-based check rather than merely competing with it.
- **Pronunciation stakes**: real — rhyme judgment is dialect-dependent
  (the Blueprint's own S9 flag notes "again/pen" rhymes in some English
  varieties, not others), so a tutor must judge against the LEARNER's
  own pronunciation, never against a single reference accent; a
  dialect-consistent rhyme judgment must never be marked wrong.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "say both words out loud — do they
  sound the same at the end?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence
  across nearly every item and should be given genuine space, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3`.

## Assessment Signals

- **Diagnostic (golden probe)**: "Listen: 'hen, pen, cup.' Which one
  does not rhyme?" (Blueprint WE-1) — cleanly separates a learner with
  no rhyme-recognition yet from one with at least the beginner model
  active, before deciding where in TA-1 through TA-3 to begin.
- **Guided practice items → independent practice items**: the
  Blueprint's WE-1 → WE-2 → WE-3 (escalating: identify → generate →
  resist the spelling trap) → MP-1 through MP-5 fading ladder is reused
  directly (Components 5–6) — not re-authored here.
- **Mastery gate set** (`../../assessment/05-mastery-verification.md
  §3`): production (odd-one-out and generation on fresh, never-heard
  words — Blueprint MP-1/MP-2); new-surface (a spelling-trap pair not
  used during teaching — MP-3); mixed (embed an alliteration distractor
  among ordinary rhyme items — MP-4 — to confirm TA-3's distinction
  survives mixed presentation); delayed (a next-session opening
  retrieval check).
- **Interpretation of response pattern**: fast-and-correct on a
  straightforward pair confirms the intermediate model is automatic;
  slow-and-correct on a spelling-trap pair is a GOOD sign here
  specifically (deliberate conflict resolution, not confusion) — this
  concept is one of the places where latency does not follow the usual
  D1-grid pattern in the expected direction, and a tutor should not
  read a spelling-trap pause as struggle. Fast-and-WRONG on a
  spelling-trap item, by contrast, IS the dangerous quadrant — an
  instant, confident spelling-based judgment signals the visual
  shortcut has fully displaced the ear-check, not merely gone
  untested.
- **Transfer items**: near (a new odd-one-out set, same skill); far
  (generating a rhyme for a genuinely unusual word with few common
  rhymes, testing generalization under sparser support); real-world (a
  rhyme for the learner's own name, real or invented if none exists).

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** shrinks to the odd-one-out anchor task specifically
  — rather than re-explaining the rule, re-present three familiar,
  unambiguous words (not the item that triggered the failure) and ask
  only "which two sound the same at the end?", rebuilding confidence on
  the easiest form of the task before returning to whatever prompted
  the "I don't know."
- **Likeliest utterance at this node**: a learner is more likely to
  produce a confident but WRONG spelling-based justification than to
  say "I don't know" outright (per MC-SPELLING-MUST-MATCH's mechanism)
  — when this specific pattern appears, route directly to that
  misconception's recovery (drop the spelling frame entirely, "say both
  words out loud, forget how they're written") rather than treating it
  as a generic wrong answer.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who mouths or
  whispers a candidate rhyming word silently before answering aloud —
  rather than answering immediately — is likely running the ear-based
  check correctly but quietly; this should not be mistaken for
  uncertainty and rushed.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedure — a checking PROCEDURE (say both words,
compare endings, ignore spelling) rather than a stored fact, so it
follows a skill-fluency pattern: correctness first, then speed, with
continued practice even after the procedure is first performed
correctly, since the goal is an automatic habit that resists the
spelling shortcut under time pressure, not merely a one-time correct
demonstration. Interleaving partners: mix rhyme items with alliteration
items (from TA-3) on an ongoing basis, specifically because
MC-FIRST-SOUND-IS-ENOUGH is a curriculum-wide (Type 5) pattern likely
to resurface from OTHER sources (songs, media) outside this concept's
own teaching — periodic mixed review is the direct verification that
the distinction has generalized beyond this session.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: recognizing and generating rhymes for new, unpracticed
  words in isolation.
- **Far**: recognizing rhyme within a full spoken sentence or short
  rhyme/poem, where the rhyming words are embedded rather than
  presented as an isolated pair.
- **Real-world**: any song, nursery rhyme, or media the learner already
  enjoys is an immediate, high-engagement rhyme-recognition context
  outside the lesson.
- **Expert-transfer**: eventual poetic-structure awareness
  (`eng.literature`, well beyond this node) — this concept is the
  ground-floor auditory skill that later, much more abstract discussion
  of rhyme scheme and verse form ultimately rests on.
