## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Explain why examining large-scale corpus data can reveal patterns that individual intuition or a small handful of examples cannot reliably detect.
2. Explain that corpus data reflects frequency and typical usage (what people DO say), a different, complementary question from grammaticality (what people COULD say).
3. Identify a practical use of corpus data (collocations, dictionary definitions, language change over time).
4. Reason through a new language question using corpus-linguistics thinking, correctly separating frequency from grammaticality.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **personal intuition about how frequently a word or structure is used is not as reliable as actual large-scale corpus data** — individual intuition about frequency is notoriously unreliable compared to genuinely large-scale empirical evidence, and people's frequency intuitions are often measurably wrong when checked against real usage data. Second, **corpus frequency and syntactic grammaticality are related but distinct questions** — a corpus showing a construction is rare does not make it ungrammatical; a perfectly grammatical construction can simply be infrequently needed for stylistic, contextual, or topical reasons unrelated to its structural validity.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "my gut feeling about how common something is must be right, and if corpus data shows something is rare, it must be wrong or broken."**
The simulation: confidently assert a frequency claim based on personal feeling, and dismiss a rare-but-valid construction as ungrammatical simply because it's uncommon.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — an intuition-versus-actual-corpus-data comparison for a frequency question, and a rare-but-grammatical construction tested against fluent speakers' acceptability judgment.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Systematic data beats individual intuition for frequency questions — and rare doesn't mean broken."

**Intermediate — "I can now check my intuition against corpus data, but I still conflate rarity with ungrammaticality when I see an unfamiliar construction."**
The simulation: correctly defers to corpus data for frequency questions but still assumes an unfamiliar, infrequent construction must be wrong.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct frequency-versus-grammaticality classification practice, given constructions mixing frequent/rare and grammatical/ungrammatical combinations.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Ask two separate questions: how often does this happen, and is it structurally valid? They don't have to have the same answer."

**Advanced — "I reason through a new language question using corpus-style thinking, correctly separating frequency findings from grammaticality judgments."**
The simulation: integrates the intuition-check discipline and the frequency/grammaticality separation into one coherent empirical-reasoning judgment. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: `eng.linguistics.computational-linguistics-intro`'s use of computational tools to process and analyze language at even larger scale, building directly on this concept's empirical-method foundation.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the core corpus-reasoning toolkit. Computational tools for processing language at scale get their own deeper study next."

**Expert — "at fluency, checking intuition against evidence and separating frequency from grammaticality happens as an integrated part of ordinary linguistic reasoning, without a separate deliberate checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit predict-check-separate routine is a scaffold for an empirical-reasoning habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of linguistic analysis.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **personal intuition feels immediate and confident**, while checking actual corpus data requires an extra deliberate step, so intuition gets trusted by default until directly and repeatedly shown to diverge from real evidence.

The second mechanism is that **"correct" and "common" are frequently conflated in casual language feedback** (an unusual sentence gets marked "wrong" rather than "unusual"), reinforcing the idea that rarity itself is evidence of a structural defect rather than a separate, unrelated property.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-MY-OWN-INTUITION-ABOUT-HOW-LANGUAGE-IS-USUALLY-USED-IS-ALWAYS-AS-RELIABLE-AS-CORPUS-DATA
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from personal frequency impressions feeling immediate and confident regardless of their actual accuracy against large-scale evidence.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **have the learner make a testable frequency prediction and then check it**, since the unreliability is far more convincing demonstrated through a personal miss than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given a frequency-comparison question, the learner checks their intuition-based prediction against actual corpus data and explains any mismatch.

### MC-B-IF-CORPUS-DATA-SHOWS-SOMETHING-IS-RARE-OR-UNUSUAL-IT-MUST-BE-UNGRAMMATICAL
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 3 (language contamination), from "correct" and "common" being casually conflated in everyday feedback, treating rarity itself as evidence of a structural defect.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **directly testing a rare construction against fluent speakers' acceptability judgment, separately from its frequency**, since the frequency/grammaticality separation is far more convincing demonstrated than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given a rare but structurally well-formed construction, the learner explains why its rarity does not make it ungrammatical.

## Analogies

**Best — the Forest-Survey-and-Rare-Medical-Treatment Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a systematic forest survey revealing the true, large-scale distribution of species, contrasted with an individual's unreliable gut feeling based on limited encounters — directly seeding the fix for MC-A. A rare but medically valid treatment, its infrequent use unrelated to its actual validity — directly seeding the fix for MC-B.
*Breaking point*: a forest survey and a medical-validity question both have a clean, external ground truth to check against, while some corpus-linguistics questions (is this usage "acceptable" in a given register) involve genuinely more contested, judgment-dependent boundaries than these anchors' cleaner truth conditions suggest.

**Alternative — a weather forecast versus a single person's memory of "it's always sunny here."** A systematic weather record across years reveals the true climate pattern, correcting a vivid but unrepresentative personal memory — just as corpus data corrects an unrepresentative personal intuition about frequency.
*Breaking point*: weather is a physically measurable, non-linguistic phenomenon with no analogous "grammaticality" question, so the analogy only covers MC-A's intuition-versus-data theme, not MC-B's frequency-versus-validity distinction.

**Visual anchor — the Forest-Survey-and-Rare-Medical-Treatment Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"If it feels more common to me, it probably is."** Directly installs the intuition-reliability misconception as a stated definition.
- **"If a sentence sounds unusual, it must be grammatically wrong."** Directly installs the rarity-equals-ungrammatical misconception as a stated definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Forest-Survey-and-Rare-Medical-Treatment Anchor (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Does a systematic survey usually confirm or contradict a quick personal impression?"
2. **The intuition-versus-corpus-data collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Which of these two phrasings do you think is more common — and does the data agree?"
3. **The rarity-versus-grammaticality collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Is this rare construction actually broken, or just less frequently needed?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by testing predictions against actual corpus data and grammaticality judgments.

1. **Need**: "If you had to guess which of two phrasings people use more often, how confident would you be — and how would you actually check?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely predicts frequency for several word-pair comparisons before being told the official corpus-checking method.
3. **Invention**: "Can you think of a sentence that's grammatically fine but that you'd rarely actually say or write?"
4. **Collision**: present a rare passive-voice construction that is fully grammatical, and ask whether its rarity makes it broken.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — corpus data beats intuition for frequency questions, and frequency is separate from grammaticality — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Check frequency against data, not intuition; check validity separately from rarity."

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for updating a frequency belief, and for classifying a construction's rarity separately from its validity**, since a correct-looking judgment with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated intuition-check** ("let me see if the data actually agrees with my guess") applied to a new frequency question — the direct behavioral signature of the intuition-unreliability fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary separation of frequency from grammaticality** ("this is rare, but is it actually broken?") — direct evidence the two-question fix is internalized.
- **Dismissing a rare construction as wrong purely because it's uncommon** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-B still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Check frequency against data, not intuition — and rare doesn't mean broken."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But I'm pretty sure that's the more common one"** — take this as an accurate report of a genuine impression, not a mistake; respond by adding the stricter test: "That's your honest impression, that's true — but does the actual data agree, or does it show something different?"
- **"That sentence sounds weird, so it must be wrong"** — a genuine, reasonable reaction to an unfamiliar construction; respond by naming the additional layer: "It sounds unusual to you, yes — but is it actually structurally broken, or is it just less common for stylistic reasons?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full corpus-linguistics reasoning task down to **checking one single intuition-based frequency guess against provided data, with grammaticality classification set aside.** Isolates the most basic intuition-check before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just go with what sounds right to you."** This directly licenses the intuition-trust pattern, undermining the evidence-checking discipline this concept specifically builds.
