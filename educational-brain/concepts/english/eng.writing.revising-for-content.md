## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Sort a list of draft issues into content concerns (revision) versus surface concerns (editing).
2. Identify content gaps and evidence insufficiency regardless of a draft's length or paragraph count.
3. Evaluate whether provided evidence genuinely supports a claim, or needs strengthening.
4. Fully revise a draft for content — strengthening evidence, reorganizing for logic, cutting or developing sections — without addressing surface-level errors.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **revising is not fixing spelling, grammar, and punctuation errors** — those activities belong to editing; revising for content means reconsidering the big-picture questions (is the argument clear, is the organization logical, is evidence sufficient, does each section serve the piece's purpose), and a draft with flawless spelling but confusing organization is a bigger problem than a well-organized draft with a few typos, since content revision addresses the bigger problem first. Second, **a draft with enough words or paragraphs is not automatically content-complete** — length and paragraph count don't measure content quality; a long draft can still repeat the same point with minimal new evidence, have real content gaps, or be poorly organized, while a shorter draft can be genuinely well-developed, so content adequacy must be judged by evaluating argument, evidence, and organization directly, independent of length.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "revising means fixing spelling and grammar, and a long draft doesn't need more work."**
The simulation: focus entirely on correcting typos and comma splices when asked to revise, and assume a lengthy draft must already be well-developed.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a flawlessly-spelled but confusingly-organized draft compared against a reorganized version with a few typos, and a large but empty-calorie meal plate compared against a smaller, genuinely nutritious one.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A recipe redesign fixes what's actually IN the meal — a surface spray just shines the plate. And a big plate can still be empty calories."

**Intermediate — "I can now sort content from surface issues and evaluate evidence, but I still sometimes judge a long draft as basically fine without checking directly."**
The simulation: correctly identifies content versus surface concerns in isolated examples but reverts to a length-based impression when facing a genuinely long draft.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct evaluation practice, comparing a long, repetitive draft against a shorter, well-developed one for which genuinely needs more content revision.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Don't let the word count reassure you — evaluate the argument, evidence, and organization directly, every time."

**Advanced — "I fully revise a draft for content — strengthening evidence, reorganizing, cutting or developing sections — without touching surface-level errors."**
The simulation: integrates content/surface sorting, direct evidence evaluation, and length-independent judgment into one coherent, complete content revision. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: `eng.writing.editing-and-proofreading`'s appropriate, later "spray and polish" work, which this concept's discipline defers to until content is genuinely solid.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you a content-solid draft. Surface-level polish happens next, now that it's appropriate."

**Expert — "at fluency, distinguishing content from surface concerns and evaluating length-independently happens as an integrated part of ordinary revision, without a separate deliberate sort."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit sort-and-evaluate routine is a scaffold for a revising habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of composition.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **"revise" and "edit" are used loosely interchangeably in casual speech and even some instruction**, collapsing revision's genuine content-focus into a vague, general notion of "improving the writing" that defaults to the more mechanically obvious task (fixing visible surface errors).

The second mechanism is that **length is a genuinely easy, immediately visible proxy for effort and completeness**, so a long draft FEELS finished in a way that requires no further scrutiny, even though effort spent (word count) and content quality (argument strength, evidence sufficiency) are entirely separate dimensions.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-REVISING-MEANS-FIXING-SURFACE-ERRORS-LIKE-SPELLING-AND-GRAMMAR
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 3 (language contamination), from "revise" and "edit" being used loosely interchangeably in casual speech, collapsing a genuine content/surface distinction into one vague notion.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **have the learner sort real draft issues into content versus surface columns**, since the distinction is far more convincing demonstrated through direct classification practice than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given a list of draft issues, the learner correctly sorts each as content (revision) or surface (editing).

### MC-A-DRAFT-WITH-ENOUGH-WORDS-OR-PARAGRAPHS-DOESNT-NEED-CONTENT-REVISION
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of length being a genuinely visible proxy for effort, extended into treating it as a proxy for content quality specifically.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct comparison of a long, repetitive draft against a shorter, well-developed one**, since the length/quality independence is far more convincing demonstrated through a real contrast than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given a long but repetitive draft and a shorter, well-developed one, the learner correctly identifies which genuinely needs more content revision, independent of length.

## Analogies

**Best — Empty Calories vs. Nutritious Content** (Blueprint Component 3): a visually large plate full of empty-calorie filler versus a smaller, genuinely nutritionally complete plate — directly seeding the fix for MC-A (length isn't content quality). A surface-polish spray bottle versus a recipe-redesign card, showing spraying a badly-composed meal's plate doesn't fix its actual content problem — directly seeding the fix for MC-REVISING-MEANS-FIXING-SURFACE-ERRORS-LIKE-SPELLING-AND-GRAMMAR.
*Breaking point*: a meal's nutritional content is measurable by objective nutritional science, while a piece of writing's "content quality" involves more genuinely interpretive judgment about argument clarity and evidence sufficiency than a nutrition label's clean, quantifiable metrics.

**Alternative — a house's structural integrity versus its paint job.** A house with a beautiful paint job but a cracked foundation is in worse shape than a plain-looking house with a solid foundation — cosmetic polish (editing) can't fix a structural problem (content), and a big house isn't automatically structurally sound just because it's big.
*Breaking point*: a house's structural integrity is verifiable by objective engineering inspection, while a draft's "content soundness" requires more interpretive judgment about whether an argument is genuinely convincing, a difference in verifiability the house analogy understates.

**Visual anchor — Empty Calories vs. Nutritious Content**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Revising means checking your spelling and grammar one more time."** Directly installs the surface-correction misconception as a stated definition.
- **"If your essay is long enough, it's probably done."** Directly installs the length-equals-completeness misconception as a stated definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **Empty Calories vs. Nutritious Content (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Does the visually larger plate actually nourish more than the smaller one?"
2. **The content-versus-surface collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Which draft is actually the stronger piece of writing — the flawlessly-spelled but confusing one, or the reorganized one with a few typos?"
3. **The length-versus-content collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Which draft actually needs more content revision — the longer, repetitive one, or the shorter, well-developed one?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by sorting real draft issues and comparing drafts of different lengths for actual content quality.

1. **Need**: "If your essay has perfect spelling but a confusing argument, have you 'revised' it well by fixing the spelling?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely sorts several draft issues into content versus surface columns before being told the official test.
3. **Invention**: "Could a really long essay still not have enough evidence for its main point?"
4. **Collision**: present a long, repetitive draft and a shorter, well-developed one, and ask which needs more revision.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — revision addresses content, not surface errors, and length doesn't measure content quality — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Revise the content, not the commas; length isn't quality."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Content vs. surface concern sorting (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the first misconception before deeper content-evaluation is introduced.
- **Evaluating evidence sufficiency (TA-2) comes second**, building the direct-evaluation skill needed before the second misconception can be addressed.
- **Direct content evaluation, not length-based judgment (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting the second misconception once evidence-evaluation is established.
- **Reorganizing for logical flow (TA-4) comes fourth**, extending content evaluation into an active reorganization skill.
- **Full content revision of an original draft (TA-5) comes last**, the generative capstone integrating both misconception fixes — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for classifying a draft issue as content or surface, and for judging content adequacy independent of length**, since a correct-looking revision with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from rote compliance.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated content-versus-surface check** ("does this issue change what the piece argues, or just fix a small error?") applied to a new draft issue — the direct behavioral signature of the content/surface distinction taking hold.
- **Voluntary direct evaluation of evidence and organization**, independent of a draft's length — direct evidence the length-independence fix is internalized.
- **Fixing spelling and grammar when asked to revise, or declaring a long draft "done" without checking argument/evidence/organization** — the direct behavioral signature of either misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Revise the content, not the commas — and length isn't quality."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"I fixed all the typos and grammar, isn't that revising?"** — take this as an accurate report of real effort, not a mistake; respond by adding the content test: "You fixed real errors, that's true — but did you change anything about the argument, evidence, or organization? That's what revision actually targets."
- **"My essay is five pages long, it must be well-developed"** — a genuine, reasonable impression given how much effort a long draft represents; respond by naming the additional layer: "Five pages is a lot of writing, yes — but does each page actually add new evidence, or does it repeat the same point? Check directly."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full content revision down to **sorting one single list of 4 draft issues into content versus surface, with evidence evaluation and reorganization set aside.** Isolates the most basic content/surface distinction before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just proofread it one more time."** This directly licenses the surface-correction pattern, undermining the content-evaluation discipline this concept specifically builds.
