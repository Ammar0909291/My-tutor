## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Conduct a genuine critical evaluation of a well-argued text and honestly conclude it is credible.
2. Distinguish citation quality (specific/recent/sourced vs. vague/outdated/unsourced) rather than trusting any citation equally.
3. Identify at least one unstated assumption an argument relies on.
4. Detect at least two bias signals in a passage (loaded language, omitted counterarguments, one-sided evidence).
5. Produce a full critical evaluation of a novel text covering evidence quality, assumptions, and bias, with a justified overall verdict.

## Core Understanding

"Critical" in critical reading does not mean "negative" — it means **evaluative**: genuinely examining evidence, assumptions, and credibility rather than accepting claims at face value, and this genuine examination can validly conclude a text IS credible and well-argued. This is the concept's first load-bearing fact, correcting the common conflation of "critical" with "fault-finding." The second, equally load-bearing fact is that **citation presence is not citation quality** — a vague reference to "a study" carries far less evidentiary weight than a specific, recent, relevant, peer-reviewed citation, even though both superficially "cite a source"; a critical reader must evaluate the specificity, currency, relevance, and reliability of a cited source, not merely register that a citation exists. Both facts converge on the same underlying skill: genuine evaluation replaces both reflexive skepticism and reflexive trust with an actual examination of the evidence in front of the reader.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "critical reading means finding what's wrong with the text; and if it cites sources or statistics, it must be credible."**
The simulation: search for flaws even in well-argued texts to satisfy the "critical" label, and accept any citation as sufficient proof.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a well-researched, flawless article, asked to "critically read" it, contrasted with a genuine evaluation concluding it IS credible; and a vague "a study" citation versus a specific, recent, sourced one, asked whether both deserve equal trust.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Critical means evaluative, not negative. A citation's presence isn't proof — its quality is what matters."

**Intermediate — "I evaluate now instead of forcing criticism, but I still tend to trust any cited source without checking its quality closely."**
The simulation: genuinely weighs evidence rather than manufacturing disagreement, but under-scrutinizes the citations themselves.
*Upgrade trigger*: the evidence credential check (TA-2), applying a specificity/relevance/currency/qualification checklist to citations of varying quality.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Cross-examine every citation: is it specific, recent, relevant, and from a qualified source? Don't just check that a citation exists."

**Advanced — "I evaluate evidence quality, surface unstated assumptions, detect bias signals, and produce a justified credibility verdict on novel texts."**
The simulation: integrates evidence-quality checks, assumption-surfacing, and bias-detection into one coherent evaluative judgment. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the cross-linked `eng.reading.evaluating-sources`, which formalizes this concept's source-quality evaluation into a systematic framework for research contexts.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you genuine evaluative judgment on any text. The next step formalizes source evaluation specifically for research use."

**Expert — "at fluency, evaluative reading happens continuously and automatically while reading, without a separate deliberate checklist pass."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit checklist (evidence, assumptions, bias) is a scaffold for a reading habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of ordinary reading comprehension.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **"critical" carries a strong negative connotation in everyday usage** ("don't be so critical"), so learners map the academic term onto that everyday meaning, producing manufactured fault-finding even against genuinely strong arguments.

The second mechanism is that **citations function as a visual/rhetorical signal of authority** regardless of their actual substance — the mere presence of a number, a study reference, or an expert quote triggers a "this is backed by evidence" heuristic that most readers rarely interrogate further, since checking a citation's specificity and currency requires effort most casual reading doesn't invest.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-CRITICAL-READING-MEANS-FINDING-FAULT-OR-DISAGREEING-WITH-THE-TEXT
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-A — includes the well-argued-article/manufactured-criticism discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 3 (language contamination), from the everyday negative connotation of "critical" bleeding into its distinct academic/evaluative meaning.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **explicitly separate the word's everyday and academic senses**, since the misconception is rooted in vocabulary, not reasoning — a learner may reason perfectly well once the word's academic meaning is clarified.
- **Verification of death**: given a well-argued, well-supported text, the learner conducts a genuine evaluation and honestly concludes the text is credible, rather than manufacturing criticism.

### MC-IF-A-TEXT-CITES-SOURCES-OR-STATISTICS-ITS-CLAIMS-ARE-AUTOMATICALLY-CREDIBLE
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — includes the vague-study/specific-study discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from citations functioning as a surface authority signal that triggers automatic trust without deeper scrutiny.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair supplies a **concrete checklist** (specificity, currency, relevance, qualification) rather than an abstract warning ("don't trust citations blindly"), since the underlying failure is a missing evaluative procedure, not a lack of awareness that evaluation is needed.
- **Verification of death**: given two citations of differing quality, the learner correctly ranks their credibility rather than trusting both equally.

## Analogies

**Best — the Evidence Credential Check** (Blueprint Component 3): each piece of evidence is a "witness" cross-examined via a checklist (specific? recent/relevant? qualified?) before being trusted, directly seeding the citation-quality fix; a genuinely strong text receiving an honest "well-supported" verdict seeds the evaluation-not-verdict fix.
*Breaking point*: a courtroom witness can be definitively proven credible or not through cross-examination, while a text's credibility often remains a matter of degree and reasonable disagreement — useful for the scrutinize-before-trusting intuition, less precise for implying evaluation always yields a clean verdict.

**Alternative — a home inspection versus judging a house by its curb appeal.** A house that looks impressive from the street (citations present) might have serious structural problems only a genuine inspection (evidence-quality evaluation) reveals — or might turn out to be genuinely sound, which the inspection also confirms.
*Breaking point*: a home inspection has objective, checklist-verifiable structural facts, while evaluating an argument's assumptions and bias requires more interpretive judgment.

**Visual anchor — the Evidence Credential Check**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"A good critical reader always finds something to disagree with."** Directly installs the fault-finding misconception as a stated expectation.
- **"If it's got statistics or expert quotes, you can trust it."** Directly installs the citation-presence-equals-credibility misconception as a stated rule.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Evidence Credential Check (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Which of these four pieces of evidence will survive cross-examination?" Then test each against the checklist.
2. **The well-argued-text/manufactured-criticism collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-A. *Predict first*: "Does this article have real weaknesses, or are we inventing some to seem critical?"
3. **The vague-study/specific-study collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Do these two citations deserve equal trust?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both evaluative facts are directly checkable by applying the credential-check procedure to real evidence and texts.

1. **Need**: "If an article cites 'a study' with no other details, do you actually know anything about how strong that evidence is?"
2. **Playground**: the learner cross-examines several evidence cards freely before being told the official checklist.
3. **Invention**: "Can you construct a checklist question that would catch a vague citation as weaker than a specific one?"
4. **Collision**: present a strong article and ask whether "critical reading" requires finding a flaw in it.
5. **Formalization**: name the credential-check criteria (specificity, currency, relevance, qualification) and the evaluation-not-verdict principle from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Evaluate, don't assume — and check the credentials, not just the presence, of every piece of evidence."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Evaluation, not a predetermined verdict (TA-1) comes first**, establishing the correct meaning of "critical" before any evidence-scrutiny procedure is introduced, since a learner still hunting for flaws will misapply later evaluative tools toward manufactured criticism.
- **Evidence credential checks (TA-2) come second**, installing the concrete evaluative procedure for citation quality.
- **Identifying unstated assumptions (TA-3) and detecting bias (TA-4) come third and fourth**, extending evaluative scrutiny beyond citations to the argument's structure and framing.
- **Full critical evaluation of a novel text (TA-5) comes last**, integrating all four skills into one coherent judgment — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for a credibility verdict**, since a correct-looking verdict with no articulated evidence-quality or bias reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess or a reflexive default.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **An honest "this is well-supported" verdict on a genuinely strong text**, stated without hedging or manufactured caveats — the direct behavioral signature of the fault-finding misconception being repaired.
- **A stated distinction between two citations' quality** ("this one names the study, this one doesn't") — direct evidence the credential-check habit is internalized.
- **Spontaneous surfacing of an unstated assumption or bias signal without being prompted for that specific category** — the strongest transfer evidence for TA-5/MP-5-level integration.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Evaluate honestly, and check the credentials of every piece of evidence — not just whether it exists."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads:

- **Producing a genuine positive verdict (MP-1) and distinguishing citation quality (MP-2).** Per the Blueprint's S9 routing, mastery must NOT be certified unless both pass — a learner who identifies assumptions and bias correctly (MP-3/MP-4) but still forces a predetermined verdict or trusts weak citations has not learned the core evaluative judgment.
- **Identifying an unstated assumption (MP-3).** Confirms structural argument analysis beyond evidence-surface scrutiny.
- **Detecting bias signals (MP-4).** Confirms recognition of framing-level credibility factors.
- **Full novel-text evaluation (MP-5).** The synthesis item; listen for a justified overall verdict integrating all three evaluative dimensions, not just a list of separate findings.

**Mastery certification trigger**: an honest positive verdict on a strong text; a correct citation-quality ranking; at least one correctly identified unstated assumption; at least two correctly identified bias signals; a full novel-text evaluation with a justified overall verdict.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But isn't 'being critical' about pointing out problems?"** — take this as a reasonable inference from the word's everyday meaning; respond by naming the academic sense directly: "In reading, 'critical' means evaluating carefully — sometimes that means finding real flaws, sometimes it means confirming the argument really does hold up."
- **"It has a study cited, so it must be true"** — a common, reasonable-sounding shortcut; respond with the checklist: "Let's check — does it name the study? Is it recent? Is it actually about this topic? A citation's presence isn't the same as its quality."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full novel-text evaluation down to **cross-examining a single pair of citations (one vague, one specific) with no assumption or bias analysis demanded.** Isolates the most basic evidence-quality test before returning to fuller evaluation.
- **Never shrink to "just say whether you agree or disagree."** This licenses a verdict with no evaluative reasoning behind it, undermining the entire evaluative-not-reflexive discipline this concept builds.
