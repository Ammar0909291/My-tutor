# Adjectives — `eng.grammar.adjectives`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.adjectives` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Adjectives" — words
  that modify nouns by describing quality, quantity, or type, taught
  with explicit attention to both attributive and predicate positions
  and conventional multi-adjective ordering.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.nouns` — load-bearing because
  adjectives are, by definition, noun-modifiers; a learner who cannot
  yet reliably identify nouns has no stable modification target to
  work from.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.adverbs`,
  `eng.grammar.comparatives-and-superlatives`. Adjectives and adverbs
  share the general modification-word structure, differing only in
  target (noun vs. verb/adjective/adverb); the comparative/superlative
  forms this concept introduces (TA-3) are formally extended in the
  downstream concept.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.80 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify attributive adjectives — adjectives sitting directly before
   the noun they modify.
2. Identify predicate adjectives — adjectives that follow a linking
   verb and describe the subject from a distance.
3. Identify comparative (-er, more) and superlative (-est, most)
   adjective forms and their base adjective.
4. Arrange multiple adjectives in the conventional order (opinion,
   size, age, shape, color, origin, material, purpose) before a noun.
5. Identify all adjectives in an extended passage, recognizing both
   attributive and predicate positions in realistic reading context.

## Core Understanding

Adjectives can appear directly before a noun (attributive position:
"the red ball") OR after a linking verb, describing the subject from a
distance (predicate position: "The ball IS red"). Both are genuine
adjective uses — the position changes, but the describing job stays the
same: "red" modifies "ball" in both sentences, whether it sits directly
adjacent to the noun or across the linking verb "is." A second, equally
load-bearing fact is that when stacking multiple adjectives before a
noun, English speakers follow a conventional order (roughly: opinion →
size → age → shape → color → origin → material → purpose) even without
consciously knowing the rule — "a lovely old wooden table" sounds
natural, while "a wooden old lovely table" sounds distinctly off, even
though every individual word is grammatically correct and describes the
same table. Checking multiple adjectives against this conventional
order, rather than placing them arbitrarily, is the reliable practice
for natural-sounding English.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "adjectives always come right before the noun they
  describe, and their order among each other doesn't matter"**: the
  arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct — a
  natural default since attributive position (before the noun) is the
  most visually obvious and commonly first-taught pattern, and since
  adjective order is genuinely unconscious even for fluent native
  speakers, giving no explicit signal that either assumption is
  incomplete.
- **Intermediate model — "I can identify attributive adjectives and
  recognize that predicate adjectives are also genuine adjective
  uses"**: installed by TA-1's attributive-position practice and TA-2's
  predicate-position practice, using the Component 3 describing-word-
  detective anchor to demonstrate both positions describe the same
  object, resolving MC-ADJECTIVES-MUST-COME-BEFORE-THE-NOUN. Upgrade
  trigger: correctly identifying a fresh predicate adjective without
  hesitation, recognizing it still describes the subject despite its
  distant position.
- **Advanced model — "I can identify comparative and superlative
  adjective forms"**: installed by TA-3's practice, extending the
  concept to degree-of-comparison forms while treating them as
  adjective FORMS, not a separate category.
- **Expert model — "I arrange multiple adjectives in the conventional
  order, and I can identify all adjectives, in both positions, within
  extended text"**: installed by TA-4's adjective-order practice
  (resolving MC-ADJECTIVE-ORDER-DOESNT-MATTER) and TA-5's extended-text
  identification, integrating both patterns into realistic reading
  application. This is the concept's genuine mastery bar — treating
  adjective order as a real, learnable, checkable pattern rather than
  an arbitrary or unconscious-only skill.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 learners whose L1 has a different or no fixed adjective order, or
  places adjectives after nouns by default (French, Spanish for many
  descriptive adjectives), both TA-1's basic pre-noun word-order
  pattern and TA-4's multi-adjective order should be expected to
  require deliberate, explicit practice — these are significant,
  well-documented transfer challenges, not minor details to be
  quickly absorbed.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of how adjectives are typically first encountered. The
attributive-only failure exists because attributive position genuinely
IS the most visually obvious, most commonly first-taught pattern (the
adjective sits right next to the noun it describes, making the
relationship perceptually direct), so a learner reasonably extends
"adjectives sit before nouns" from this consistently reinforced
pattern, with nothing marking the equally legitimate predicate-
position exception until directly tested against a sentence like "The
ball IS red." The order-doesn't-matter failure exists because
adjective ordering is genuinely UNCONSCIOUS even for fluent native
speakers — nobody explicitly teaches most native speakers the opinion-
size-age-shape-color-origin-material-purpose sequence, they simply
absorb it through massive exposure — so a learner without that
exposure history has no felt sense that an order exists at all, with
nothing marking the pattern's reality until a scrambled-order sentence
is directly contrasted against its natural-order counterpart.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the describing-word detective anchor itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): generating
  descriptive words for one object and then arranging them both before
  the noun ("the big red bouncy ball") and after a linking verb ("The
  ball is big. The ball is red.") directly embodies the position-
  independent describing function. Breaking point: the detective
  framing works well for demonstrating the two-position insight but
  doesn't naturally extend to the multi-adjective-ordering insight
  (TA-4), which requires a separate demonstration once several
  adjectives are stacked together — this entry treats the anchor as
  the introductory frame for position specifically, not the full
  concept.
- **Alternative — a recipe's ingredient list following a conventional
  order (wet ingredients, then dry, then mix-ins), not random order**:
  recipes often follow an unstated but real conventional sequence that
  "sounds right" to experienced cooks even without an explicit rule
  being taught — parallels how adjective order feels natural without
  being consciously learned. Breaking point: useful for the felt-but-
  unstated-pattern insight, but doesn't address the attributive/
  predicate position distinction the describing-word-detective anchor
  covers directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe predicate adjectives as "less
  common" or "a special exception" compared to attributive
  adjectives**: framing predicate position as rare or exceptional
  risks reinforcing MC-ADJECTIVES-MUST-COME-BEFORE-THE-NOUN by
  implying attributive position is the "real" default and predicate
  position is a minor add-on; predicate adjectives are extremely
  common in ordinary English (any "is/seems/looks + adjective"
  construction) and should be presented as equally central, not
  secondary.

- **Home/no-equipment**: any object in the room supplies real TA-1/TA-2

- **Teacher-demo (position and order contrasts)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstrations — asking whether
  "red" in "The ball is red" still describes "ball," and comparing "a

- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming an

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
position-independent-function principle and the conventional-order
pattern are both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to
guided discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured nouns, is asked
whether "red" still describes "ball" in "The ball is red"); **
playground/invention** (TA-1's attributive-position practice builds
initial confidence with the most visible pattern); **collision** (the
Blueprint's own "is red" probe breaks the attributive-only assumption;
the "wooden old table" vs. "old wooden table" probe breaks the order-
doesn't-matter assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's own-words
statement of both the two-position principle and the conventional-
order pattern); **compression** (TA-5's fluent identification across
both positions in extended text without each instance separately
re-derived). The specific terms "attributive," "predicate,"
"comparative," and "superlative" are convention-bound grammatical
terminology best introduced by direct instruction once the learner has
already felt the underlying distinctions through the anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (attributive adjectives) is placed first as the concept's most
visible, intuitive entry point, per the Blueprint's own describing-
word-detective anchor. TA-2 (predicate adjectives) follows immediately,
directly targeting MC-ADJECTIVES-MUST-COME-BEFORE-THE-NOUN — per the
Blueprint's own session architecture, this conflict-evidence probe is
placed right after TA-2, once the learner has secure attributive
practice to contrast against. TA-3 (comparative and superlative forms)
extends the concept to degree-of-comparison, building on the now-secure
basic adjective-identification skill. TA-4 (adjective order with
multiple adjectives) is placed next, directly targeting MC-ADJECTIVE-
ORDER-DOESNT-MATTER, addressing the SECOND, independent dimension
(multi-adjective sequencing, not position or form) once the learner
already has a secure sense of what an adjective is across positions and
forms. TA-5 (identifying adjectives in extended text) is placed last as
the concept's integration capstone, applying both position patterns
(attributive and predicate) fluently within realistic, continuous
reading. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn
session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously identifies
  a fresh predicate adjective without hesitation (rather than only
  scanning before nouns), and spontaneously checks a multi-adjective
  sequence against the conventional order before speaking or writing —
  these are the primary diagnostic signals for whether TA-2 and TA-4's
  disciplines have genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice
  items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before arranging
  multiple fresh adjectives, followed by a correct, order-consistent
  sequence, suggests the learner is consciously applying the checklist
  rather than guessing — a good sign. Instant, confident but scrambled-
  order adjective stacking is the concerning pattern for MC-ADJECTIVE-
  ORDER-DOESNT-MATTER.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags — a learner who already uses
  adjectives fluently in speech but lacks explicit order-awareness
  should be reassured that this rule is genuinely unconscious even for
  native speakers, and that making it explicit is a real advantage,
  not remedial work.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does it still describe the noun, even
  though it's not sitting right in front of it?" is this concept's
  load-bearing sentence for MC-ADJECTIVES-MUST-COME-BEFORE-THE-NOUN,
  per `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` —
  asked whenever a fresh predicate-adjective sentence is introduced,
  making the position-independent check a routinely-invited response
  rather than an afterthought.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework — a two-position recognition system
(attributive/predicate) combined with a form category (comparative/
superlative) and a sequencing rule (conventional multi-adjective
order), applied to genuinely novel sentences and text; mastery is
demonstrated by correctly identifying adjectives across both positions
and correctly ordering unfamiliar adjective sets (MP-2/MP-4) rather
than by recognizing a fixed, previously-taught example list.
Interleaving partners: mix position-identification items (testing
TA-1/TA-2) with form items (testing TA-3) and ordering items (testing
TA-4) in ongoing review, so the learner continues exercising all three
distinct dimensions as separately-verified competencies.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: identifying fresh attributive and predicate adjectives and
  arranging fresh multi-adjective sets in conventional order.
- **Far**: this concept's modification-word framework is the direct
  foundation for `eng.grammar.adverbs`'s parallel structure (modifying
  verbs/adjectives/adverbs instead of nouns) and
  `eng.grammar.comparatives-and-superlatives`'s formal extension of
  TA-3's degree-of-comparison forms.
- **Real-world**: every description the learner produces — in speech
  or writing — offers immediate, ungated opportunities to apply
  correct adjective positioning and natural multi-adjective ordering.
- **Expert-transfer**: vivid, natural-sounding descriptive writing at
  any advanced level depends on fluent command of both adjective
  positions and, especially, correct multi-adjective ordering, since
  scrambled ordering remains a noticeable marker of non-native
  production even at otherwise advanced proficiency.
