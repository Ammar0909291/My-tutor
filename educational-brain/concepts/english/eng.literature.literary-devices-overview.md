## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Name a device in a passage AND explain its specific effect and likely purpose, not stopping at the label.
2. Apply the removal test to a device-containing passage, confirming specific meaning that would be lost without it.
3. Categorize unlabeled device examples into figurative, structural, or sound-based categories.
4. Identify and explain how one specific device reinforces a passage's already-identified theme.
5. Fully analyze a new passage: identify two devices, categorize them, explain their effects, and connect at least one to theme.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **identifying a device by name is not the goal of literary analysis — it's the first step** — correctly labeling something "a metaphor" is like correctly naming a tool before using it; real literary analysis requires explaining WHAT specific effect the device creates and WHY the author likely chose it here rather than a plainer alternative. Second, **literary devices are not decorative extras separate from meaning** — devices are often integral tools authors use to construct and deliver meaning, theme, and effect; removing a device (like irony framing a "victory" the reader recognizes as devastating loss) doesn't just make a passage "less pretty," it can genuinely remove or flatten the actual meaning being constructed.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "identifying a device by name completes the analysis; literary devices are decorative extras separate from a story's real meaning."**
The simulation: spot and label a device ("that's a metaphor!") and stop there, and treat devices as optional stylistic flourishes layered on top of the "actual" story.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — "that's a metaphor" versus an explanation of the specific effect and purpose, asked which demonstrates actual analysis; and an ironic "victory" passage with the irony removed, asked what would be lost if stated plainly.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Naming is the first step, not the last. Removing a real device removes real meaning, not just decoration."

**Intermediate — "I explain a device's effect now, but I still sometimes treat other devices I encounter as purely stylistic without testing whether removing them would change the meaning."**
The simulation: correctly explains effect for devices already flagged as significant but hasn't yet applied the removal test universally to check for meaning-construction.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-2's direct removal-test practice, imagining/writing a passage with the device removed to confirm what meaning is genuinely lost.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Test it: imagine the passage without this device. Does the meaning stay exactly the same, or does something get lost?"

**Advanced — "I name devices and explain their effects, apply the removal test, categorize devices correctly, connect devices to theme, and fully analyze a new passage integrating all of this."**
The simulation: integrates name-then-explain, removal-testing, categorization, and theme-connection into one coherent literary analysis. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the three direct downstream cross-links — metaphor and simile, irony, and poetry basics, each deepening one specific device category established here.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the general device-analysis framework. Each downstream concept deepens one specific family — figurative, ironic, or poetic devices."

**Expert — "at fluency, name-then-explain analysis and the removal test happen as an integrated part of ordinary literary reading."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit name-explain-removal-test routine is a scaffold for a reading habit that, at true fluency, operates as an integrated part of literary comprehension.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **naming a device is a discrete, checkable, and often explicitly rewarded task in early literature instruction** ("find the metaphor"), while explaining its specific effect requires a further, more open-ended interpretive step that no single "correct" label satisfies — so the labeling task, being easier to complete and verify, becomes the de facto stopping point.

The second mechanism is that **the everyday, casual sense of "decoration" or "figure of speech" implies something optional or purely stylistic**, so the technical use of "device" inherits that connotation, obscuring that many devices are doing genuine, load-bearing interpretive work rather than adding surface polish.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-IDENTIFYING-A-DEVICE-BY-NAME-IS-THE-GOAL-OF-LITERARY-ANALYSIS
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry — the bare-label/effect-explanation discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from early literature instruction typically rewarding device-spotting and naming as a discrete, checkable task, without consistently requiring the further effect-explanation step.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **structurally require a second step after naming**, since the fix requires a procedural habit change, not merely being told naming is insufficient.
- **Verification of death**: given a passage with a device, the learner names it AND explains its specific effect and likely purpose, not stopping at the label.

### MC-LITERARY-DEVICES-ARE-DECORATIVE-EXTRAS-SEPARATE-FROM-MEANING
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — the ironic-victory/plain-statement discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 3 (language contamination), from the everyday connotation of "decoration" or "figure of speech" as optional embellishment, contaminating the technical understanding that many devices are integral, meaning-constructing tools.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair supplies the **concrete removal test**, since the fix requires the learner to directly experience meaning-loss upon removal, not merely be told devices matter.
- **Verification of death**: given a device-containing passage, the learner applies the removal test and confirms specific meaning that would be lost without the device.

## Analogies

**Best — the Structural Beam, Not the Wallpaper** (Blueprint Component 3): a model house's structural beams (removing one makes the roof genuinely sag) versus decorative wallpaper (removing it just looks plainer) directly seeds the meaning-construction fix; a tool ID card (naming a hammer) beside a tool-use demonstration (driving a nail) directly seeds the naming-is-not-analysis fix.
*Breaking point*: a physical beam's structural necessity is objectively, mechanically verifiable, while a literary device's meaning-construction role is a matter of interpretive judgment — useful for the some-devices-are-load-bearing intuition, less precise for implying every device's importance is as unambiguous as a beam holding up a roof.

**Alternative — a musical note versus a chord's harmonic function.** Simply naming a chord ("that's a G major") is analogous to naming a device; explaining how that specific chord creates tension or resolution within the song's progression is analogous to explaining a device's effect.
*Breaking point*: music theory has more codified, universally agreed-upon functional rules (tension/resolution) than literary device analysis, which involves more open interpretive latitude.

**Visual anchor — the Structural Beam, Not the Wallpaper**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Once you've spotted and named the device, you've done the analysis."** Directly installs the naming-is-the-goal misconception as a stated standard.
- **"Literary devices are just fancy decoration writers add to sound impressive."** Directly installs the decorative-extras misconception as a stated definition.

3. **The ironic-victory/plain-statement collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "What would be lost if the ironic framing were removed?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both analytical facts are directly checkable by pushing past a device's name and by testing removal against meaning.

1. **Need**: "If you just say 'that's a metaphor' and stop, have you actually explained anything about the passage?"
2. **Playground**: the learner practices naming several devices freely before being pushed to explain their effects, discovering the gap themselves.
3. **Invention**: "Can you rewrite this passage without its device and see what specifically changes?"
4. **Collision**: present an ironic "victory" passage with the irony removed; ask what specifically is lost.
5. **Formalization**: name the two analytical facts — naming is the first step, not the analysis itself; devices construct meaning, they don't just decorate — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Name it, then explain what it DOES — and test what's lost if you take it away."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Name-then-explain practice (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the naming-is-the-goal misconception before the removal test is introduced.
- **The removal test for meaning (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting the decorative-extras misconception once the effect-explanation habit is established.
- **Categories of literary devices (TA-3) comes third**, adding the organizational framework (figurative, structural, sound-based) once the core analytical discipline is secure.
- **Connecting devices to theme (TA-4) and full device analysis of a novel passage (TA-5) come last**, extending analysis to the theme-connection level and integrating everything into a generative capstone — matching this program's established sequencing principle.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated explanation of a device's effect and their reasoning through the removal test**, since a correct device label with no further explanation, or a device dismissed as decorative with no removal test applied, cannot be distinguished from surface-level pattern-matching.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A device named, immediately followed by an unprompted explanation of its specific effect and likely purpose** — the direct behavioral signature of the name-then-explain fix taking hold.
- **A spontaneous removal-test application** ("if we took this out, we'd lose...") to a newly encountered device — direct evidence the meaning-construction fix is internalized.
- **A device correctly named with no further comment, or a device dismissed as "just for style"** — the direct behavioral signature of either misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Name it, then explain what it DOES — and test what's genuinely lost if you take it away."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"I found it — it's a metaphor!"** — take this as a correct, valuable first step, not a mistake; respond by asking for the next step: "Great catch — now what effect does comparing these two things create? Why might the author have chosen this instead of just saying it plainly?"
- **"Isn't this device just there to make the writing sound nicer?"** — a reasonable-sounding assumption from the everyday sense of "decoration"; respond by testing it: "Let's check — if we removed it, would the passage mean exactly the same thing, or would something real be lost?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full novel-passage analysis down to **explaining the effect of a single, already-named device, with no categorization or theme-connection task demanded.** Isolates the most basic effect-explanation skill before returning to fuller analysis.
- **Never shrink to "just find and name one device in this passage."** This directly re-licenses the naming-is-sufficient pattern this concept specifically works to dismantle.
