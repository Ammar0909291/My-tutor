## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify a text's primary purpose (inform, persuade, entertain) and any genuinely present secondary purpose, rather than forcing a single label onto a blended text.
2. Identify an author's tone from specific word-choice evidence, distinguishing it from the reader's own personal emotional reaction to the passage.
3. Recognize structural and stylistic signals characteristic of each purpose category.
4. Cite at least two specific word choices as evidence for a tone identification.
5. Produce a combined purpose-and-tone analysis of a novel passage, with specific textual evidence for each claim.

## Core Understanding

A text's purpose is not always a single, clean category — this is the concept's first load-bearing fact: while every text has a **primary** purpose (to inform, to persuade, or to entertain), many genuine texts also carry a **secondary** purpose operating alongside it, such as an entertaining personal essay that also subtly persuades toward a viewpoint, or an informative article written in a deliberately humorous style. Forcing every text into exactly one of the three categories, with no room for blending, misrepresents how real writing actually works; the correct approach is to identify the primary goal first, then check explicitly whether a secondary purpose is also genuinely present, naming both when it is. The concept's second, equally load-bearing fact concerns tone: **tone is a property of the text, evidenced by the author's specific word choices and stylistic decisions — not a property of the reader's personal emotional reaction**, which can vary from person to person even when the text's tone itself is fixed and analyzable. A clinically-worded passage about a difficult historical event has a detached, factual tone regardless of whether a given reader happens to feel sad, angry, or unmoved reading it; identifying tone means pointing to specific words or stylistic choices in the text, not reporting how the passage made the reader feel.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "every text has exactly one purpose (inform, persuade, or entertain), and tone is just however the passage makes me feel."**
The runnable simulation: force a single purpose label onto every text regardless of blending, and report personal emotional reaction when asked about tone. This is a reasonable default, since introductory purpose categories are often taught as mutually exclusive, and "tone" in everyday speech frequently refers to one's own emotional response rather than an analyzable textual property.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — being shown a text that both entertains and persuades and asked to name "the" single purpose, then asked whether it's doing more than one job at once; and being shown a clinically-worded passage and asked what the *author's* tone is, versus how different readers might personally feel about it.
*Shelf-life warning at replacement*: "Ask which purpose is primary, then check for a genuine secondary one — and identify tone from the author's specific word choices, not from your own feelings."

**Intermediate — "I can name a primary and secondary purpose when both are present, and I check word choices for tone, but I sometimes still slip into reporting my own reaction instead of the text's evidence."**
The simulation: correctly identifies blended purposes, but occasionally conflates tone-as-authorial-attitude with tone-as-reader-feeling, especially on passages that evoke a strong personal response.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — being asked to separately state a passage's tone (its evidenced authorial attitude) and how it made a specific reader feel, and discovering the two can genuinely differ.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Your feeling about a passage is real, but it's not the same thing as the author's tone. Point to specific words for the tone; keep your own reaction as a separate observation."

**Advanced — "I identify primary and secondary purposes accurately, and I identify tone strictly from cited word-choice evidence, keeping it fully separate from my own emotional reaction."**
The simulation: analyzes any text for both primary and secondary purpose using structural/stylistic signals, and identifies tone using specific, cited word-choice evidence, correctly distinguishing it from personal reaction throughout. This is the target model, matching TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the direct unlock `eng.reading.close-reading`, where this concept's word-choice-as-evidence discipline extends to a wider range of literary-analysis questions beyond purpose and tone alone.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the core evidentiary discipline — point to specific words, don't rely on feeling. The next step applies that same discipline much more broadly."

**Expert — "at full analytical fluency, purpose and tone identification happen as an integrated, near-automatic part of reading any text critically, with explicit word-choice citation reserved for building a formal, defensible written analysis."**
Named here to mark the arc; this concept's explicit, step-by-step evidence-citation routine is a scaffold for a judgment that, at true critical-reading fluency, operates largely as an integrated background process during ordinary reading — named here so the tutor understands the routine's eventual goal is fluent, automatic critical reading, not a permanently maintained conscious citation exercise.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant failure mechanism is that **introductory purpose instruction typically presents inform/persuade/entertain as mutually exclusive categories using clearly single-purpose example texts**, so a learner's assumption that every text fits exactly one category is a reasonable generalization from genuinely representative early practice material — the assumption breaks down specifically on more sophisticated, blended texts the learner hasn't yet had reason to classify multi-dimensionally.

The second mechanism is that **the everyday word "tone" is frequently used to describe a listener's or reader's own emotional state** ("that comment had an angry tone to me," used to describe the speaker's felt reaction, not just the speaker's intent), so a learner reasonably imports this reader-centered usage into a technical reading-analysis context that specifically requires the reverse framing — analyzing the author's crafted attitude as an independent, text-evidenced property.

The third mechanism, activated specifically when a passage evokes a strong personal reaction, is that **an intense emotional response can feel, from the inside, indistinguishable from a fact about the text itself** — a reader who feels strongly moved by a passage may reasonably (if incorrectly) treat that intensity of feeling as direct evidence about the author's tone, rather than checking the actual word choices independently of how strongly the passage happened to land for them personally.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns the trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-TEXT-CAN-ONLY-HAVE-ONE-PURPOSE-INFORM-OR-PERSUADE-OR-ENTERTAIN
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-A — includes the humorous-persuasive-essay and single-vs-primary-plus-secondary discrimination pairs.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of a genuinely correct rule for simple, single-purpose texts, extended past its actual scope to blended, more sophisticated texts that combine purposes.
- **Teaching consequence of the birth type**: because the single-purpose default is correct often enough to be well-reinforced, the repair must **add the possibility of a secondary purpose as an additional check, not replace the primary-purpose judgment** — the Blueprint's own bridge text does this precisely, framing the task as "primary, then check for secondary," not as abandoning categorization altogether.
- **Verification of death**: given a genuinely blended text, the learner identifies both a primary and a secondary purpose rather than forcing a single label or refusing to commit to any primary judgment at all.

### MC-TONE-IS-JUST-WHATEVER-EMOTION-THE-READER-HAPPENS-TO-FEEL
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-TONE-IS-JUST-WHATEVER-EMOTION-THE-READER-HAPPENS-TO-FEEL — includes the clinical-battle-description discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 3 (language contamination), where the everyday, reader-centered sense of "tone" (my emotional reaction) gets carried into a technical context that specifically requires the author-centered, text-evidenced sense.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **a concrete, checkable test** (can you point to a specific word choice, independent of how the passage made you feel?) rather than simply asserting tone and reaction are "different," since the two really can coincide much of the time, making the distinction easy to miss without an explicit separating test.
- **Verification of death**: given a passage and a description of a reader's strong personal reaction, the learner identifies the text's authorial tone using cited word-choice evidence, explicitly distinguishing it from the described reader's individual response.

## Analogies

**Best — the Word-Choice Detective Board** (Blueprint Component 3): pinning specific highlighted words as evidence cards next to a verdict card, with the explicit requirement that the verdict be pinned to word evidence, not to "how I feel about the subject," directly seeds the tone-versus-reaction fix; a second board showing two verdict cards simultaneously pinnable ("primarily informs" and "secondarily entertains") directly seeds the single-purpose-only fix.
*Breaking point*: a detective's evidence board implies a single, eventually-determinable "true" verdict, while a tone judgment can legitimately admit some reasonable disagreement about degree or precise labeling even among careful readers citing the same words — useful for the evidence-over-feeling and dual-verdict intuitions, less precise for implying purpose/tone analysis always converges on one uncontestable answer.

**Alternative — a restaurant critic's written review versus a diner's private feelings about the meal.** The critic's review (the text's tone) is crafted through specific word choices ("the sauce was cloyingly sweet" vs. "the sauce had a pronounced sweetness") that any careful reader can point to and analyze, while a specific diner's personal enjoyment or distaste (the reader's reaction) is their own separate experience, which might differ from another diner's even when reading the identical review.
*Breaking point*: a restaurant review is explicitly evaluative by genre convention, while many texts requiring tone analysis (a news report, a historical account) are not primarily evaluative at all — useful for the crafted-text-versus-personal-experience distinction, less precise for texts whose tone is subtler than a review's overt evaluative language.

**Visual anchor — the Word-Choice Detective Board** as already described above, doubling as both the primary teaching metaphor and the actual working instrument this concept's core teaching is organized around.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"A text is either informative, persuasive, or entertaining — pick the one that fits best."** Directly installs MC-A as a stated procedure, precisely the forced-single-label error this concept's first misconception consists of.
- **"If a passage makes you feel sad, that's the tone."** Directly installs MC-TONE-IS-JUST-WHATEVER-EMOTION-THE-READER-HAPPENS-TO-FEEL as a stated rule, exactly the reaction-as-tone conflation this concept's second misconception's repair must counter.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Word-Choice Detective Board (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Do you think this text has just one purpose, or could it be doing two things at once?" Then pin evidence cards and confirm both verdicts if warranted.
2. **The humorous-persuasive-essay collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "What's the single purpose of this text?" Then reveal the blend and resolve with a primary-plus-secondary judgment.
3. **The clinical-battle-description collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "What's the author's tone here?" Then separately ask how different hypothetical readers might feel, and resolve the distinction.
4. **The combined novel-text analysis (learner activity, TA-5).** *Predict first*: "What purpose and tone do you expect from this passage, just from a first skim?" Then analyze fully with cited evidence and confirm together.

## Discovery Questions

This concept is well suited to **guided discovery for the multi-purpose fact (via direct blended-text sorting, per TA-1)**; the tone-versus-reaction fact benefits from more **direct instruction with an explicit separating test**, since the relevant distinction (crafted attitude vs. personal response) is easy to state but genuinely easy to conflate without a concrete practice routine.

1. **Need**: "Can a text be trying to do more than one thing at once — like entertain you while also trying to convince you of something?"
2. **Playground**: the learner sorts several texts by purpose freely, before being told which are single-purpose and which are blended.
3. **Invention**: "For the blended one, what's the main job, and what's the second job happening alongside it?" The learner names both.
4. **Collision**: present the clinical-battle-description passage and ask what the author's tone is, then separately ask how a specific hypothetical reader might feel.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — a text can have a primary and secondary purpose, and tone is text-evidenced, not reader-felt — from the learner's own evidence.
6. **Compression**: "Check for a second purpose — and point to the words, not your feelings, for tone."

## Teaching Sequence

The pedagogical logic behind this arc:

- **Multi-purpose text sorting (TA-1) is established first**, directly confronting MC-A before any tone content is introduced, since purpose and tone are analytically independent judgments best built up separately before being combined in the culminating task.
- **Word-choice evidence for tone (TA-2) comes second**, building the core evidentiary habit (point to specific words) that TA-3's tone-versus-reaction discrimination directly depends on.
- **Tone vs. reader reaction (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting MC-TONE-IS-JUST-WHATEVER-EMOTION-THE-READER-HAPPENS-TO-FEEL only once the word-choice-evidence habit (TA-2) is secure, since distinguishing tone from reaction presupposes the learner already has a positive, evidence-based method for identifying tone to contrast against mere feeling.
- **Author's purpose signals (TA-4) and the combined novel-text analysis (TA-5) come last**, with TA-4 adding structural/stylistic vocabulary for purpose-signal recognition and TA-5 requiring the learner to integrate both purpose analysis (TA-1/TA-4) and tone analysis (TA-2/TA-3) on genuinely novel material — matching this program's established sequencing principle that a concept's most integrative task is reserved for its culminating stage.

In recommended order, from `../teaching-actions/`:

1. **Demonstration** (SHOW) — the Word-Choice Detective Board and both collision scenarios.
2. **Error Analysis** (TEST-THINKING) — both collisions function as guided error analysis against the learner's own plausible-but-incorrect assumption (single-purpose forcing, or tone-as-feeling).
3. **Matching** (DO) — multi-purpose text sorting (TA-1) and purpose-signal recognition (TA-4) are fundamentally matching/classification actions.
4. **Concept Map** (ORGANIZE) — a simple two-column map (text evidence / personal reaction) built from the tone-versus-reaction contrast (TA-3), giving a compact, reusable artifact distinguishing the two categories this concept's second misconception conflates.

**Does not fit**: **Game** — the genuine analytical care this concept requires (checking specific word evidence rather than reacting from feeling) could be undermined by speed-pressured competition, which would reward quick impression over careful citation. **Role-Play** — no natural social/dialogic dimension to a textual-evidence-analysis skill.

## Voice Teaching Notes

This concept's core evidence is **spoken analysis with cited textual evidence** — the learner's stated purpose and tone judgments plus, critically, the specific words or phrases cited as evidence, since a correct-sounding judgment with no cited evidence cannot be distinguished from an intuitive guess or, for tone specifically, a personal reaction mistaken for analysis.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated purpose judgment immediately followed by a check for a secondary purpose, without being prompted** ("this is mainly informing, but I think it's also a little persuasive because...") — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A's repair having been internalized.
- **A tone judgment accompanied by at least one specific cited word or phrase, offered before or without reference to how the passage made the learner feel** — the direct target evidence for MC-TONE-IS-JUST-WHATEVER-EMOTION-THE-READER-HAPPENS-TO-FEEL's repair.
- **A tone judgment stated purely in terms of personal feeling ("it made me feel sad, so the tone is sad") with no reference to any specific word choice** — the direct behavioral signature of the second misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Check for a second purpose — and point to the actual words, not your own feelings, when you name the tone."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads, which the Blueprint does not own:

- **Primary and secondary purpose (MP-1) and tone vs. personal reaction (MP-2).** Per the Blueprint's own S9 protocol routing, mastery must NOT be certified unless both pass — a learner who identifies purpose signals correctly (MP-3) but forces single-purpose labels or conflates tone with reaction has not yet learned the precise analytical judgment this concept requires.
- **Purpose-signal recognition (MP-3).** Listen for the learner naming the actual structural/stylistic signal (fact-heavy and neutral for informational; opinion language and calls to action for persuasive; narrative elements and vivid sensory description for entertaining), not just a correct final label with no cited signal.
- **Tone identification with evidence (MP-4).** Require at least 2 specific word choices as cited evidence, per the Blueprint's own item design — a correct tone label with only one or zero cited words is weaker evidence of the full skill.
- **Combined novel-text analysis (MP-5).** This concept's synthesis item; a strong performance here with a weak MP-1 or MP-2 score suggests scripted-practice success without full internalization — treat MP-5 success as confirmatory, not substitutive, for MP-1/MP-2.

**Mastery certification trigger**: correct primary-plus-secondary purpose identification on a blended novel text; correct tone identification distinguished from a described reader's personal reaction, using cited word-choice evidence; correct purpose-signal recognition across informational, persuasive, and entertaining passages; a tone judgment citing at least 2 specific word choices; and a full combined purpose-and-tone analysis of a novel passage with specific evidence for each claim.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But this text is obviously just trying to inform, why would it need a second purpose?"** — take this as an accurate observation about the primary purpose, not a mistake to be dismissed; respond by adding rather than contradicting: "You're right that informing is the main job here — now let's specifically check the style. Is there anything about how it's written that's also trying to entertain or persuade you, even a little, alongside informing?"
- **"This passage made me feel really strongly, so that has to be the tone"** — a genuine, understandable conflation given how intense feelings can seem self-evidently informative; respond by naming the actual test directly: "Your feeling is real and worth noticing separately — but let's check the words themselves. What specific choices did the author make that we could point to, regardless of how strongly it landed for you personally?"
- **The smaller question to shrink to**: from a full combined purpose-and-tone analysis, down to **a single, clearly single-purpose text with an unambiguous, strongly-worded tone and no personal-reaction complication at all.** This removes both the multi-purpose and tone-versus-reaction discrimination demands entirely, isolating the most basic evidence-citation skill before returning to harder, blended or reaction-conflating cases.
- **Never shrink to "just go with your gut feeling about what the text is doing."** This directly licenses both misconceptions' underlying shortcut (categorical forcing, and feeling-as-evidence), undermining the evidence-based discipline this concept specifically builds.
