## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify imagery appealing to each of the five senses in a text, citing the specific sensory words or phrases.
2. Explain that imagery does not require figurative language — precise, literal sensory description also counts as imagery.
3. Explain the specific effect a piece of imagery creates, not just which sense it appeals to.
4. Analyze a new passage: identify at least two instances of imagery, name the sense each appeals to, and explain the specific effect each creates.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **imagery does not require figurative language to qualify** — precise, vivid, entirely literal sensory description ("the sharp, acrid smell of burnt toast filled the kitchen") is genuine imagery, just as much as a metaphor for the same sensation; imagery is defined by sensory specificity and vividness, not by whether it uses figurative comparison. Second, **naming which sense a piece of imagery appeals to is only the first half of the analysis, not the whole task** — genuine analysis must go further and explain the SPECIFIC EFFECT that imagery creates (what mood, atmosphere, association, or emphasis it produces in that particular context), since stopping at sense-identification treats the easy, mechanical step as if it were the complete analytical job.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "imagery has to use a metaphor or simile to count, and identifying which sense it appeals to is the whole analysis."**
The simulation: overlook purely literal sensory description as "not really imagery," and stop analysis at "this appeals to smell" with no further explanation.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a purely literal sensory description tested against a figurative one for equal vividness, and two analyses of the same imagery, one stopping at sense-naming and one continuing to explain effect.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A clear, unstyled photograph can be just as vivid as a stylized one — literal sensory description is still imagery. And naming the sense is only half the analysis."

**Intermediate — "I can now identify literal imagery and I'm starting to explain effect, but my effect explanations stay generic."**
The simulation: correctly identifies both figurative and literal imagery but writes vague effect statements ("it makes the scene more vivid") rather than specific ones.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's sentence-stem requirement ("This imagery appeals to [sense], creating the effect of ___ because ___"), forcing a specific, context-grounded effect explanation.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Push past 'more vivid' — what EXACTLY does this sensory detail suggest or emphasize in THIS particular context?"

**Advanced — "I identify at least two instances of imagery, literal or figurative, name the sense each appeals to, and explain each one's specific effect."**
The simulation: integrates broad imagery recognition with specific effect analysis into one coherent reading of a new passage. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: none within the current curriculum sequence — this concept is confirmed terminal.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the full imagery-analysis toolkit for any passage you read."

**Expert — "at fluency, recognizing sensory language and explaining its specific effect happens as an integrated part of ordinary literary reading, without a separate deliberate checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit identify-classify-explain routine is a scaffold for a reading habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of literary comprehension.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **imagery is introduced immediately after metaphor and simile**, priming an assumption that this newly-learned figurative-language lens applies here too, when imagery is actually defined by an entirely different criterion (sensory appeal, whether literal or figurative).

The second mechanism is that **sense-identification is the easier, more mechanical half of imagery analysis**, so it becomes a comfortable stopping point that feels like a complete answer, while the harder, more interpretive effect-explanation step goes undone unless explicitly and structurally required.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-IMAGERY-MUST-BE-FIGURATIVE-USING-METAPHOR-OR-SIMILE-TO-COUNT
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 6 (analogy overextension), from the immediately-prior metaphor-and-simile concept's figurative lens being overextended to a genuinely different device defined by sensory appeal, not figurativeness.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **show purely literal sensory description directly**, since the misconception is sustained by never having examined a genuine non-figurative example closely.
- **Verification of death**: given three passages containing only literal (non-figurative) sensory description, the learner identifies the specific sense each appeals to, confirming these count as imagery.

### MC-B-IDENTIFYING-WHICH-SENSE-IMAGERY-APPEALS-TO-IS-THE-WHOLE-ANALYTICAL-TASK
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of sense-identification's genuine usefulness as a first step, extended into treating it as the entire analytical task.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **a structural requirement (a sentence stem) forcing the effect-explanation step**, since the deeper analysis is easy to skip unless made structurally unavoidable.
- **Verification of death**: given a piece of imagery with its sense already identified, the learner writes a specific explanation of the mood or emphasis it creates in context.

## Analogies

**Best — the Unstylized-Photograph and Music-Critic Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a perfectly clear, precisely focused, entirely literal photograph that is nonetheless vivid and evocative — directly seeding the fix for MC-A. A music critic who stops at "this piece uses violins" without describing the mood those violins create — directly seeding the fix for MC-B.
*Breaking point*: a photograph's literalness is visually unambiguous, while a text's literalness (whether a phrase counts as figurative or literal) sometimes involves genuinely closer judgment calls a photograph doesn't have to make.

**Alternative — a chef's precise plating versus an abstract art plating.** A chef who plates food with exact, realistic precision (each element clearly itself) can create just as much sensory appeal as one using abstract, artistic arrangement — precision and figurative flourish are two different, equally valid routes to the same vivid effect.
*Breaking point*: food plating's "vividness" is primarily visual and immediate, while imagery's sensory appeal spans five distinct senses with different evocative mechanisms, a broader scope than a single-sense plating analogy fully captures.

**Visual anchor — the Unstylized-Photograph and Music-Critic Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"If it doesn't use a metaphor or simile, it's not imagery."** Directly installs the figurative-requirement misconception as a stated definition.
- **"Just say which sense it's talking about and you're done."** Directly installs the sense-naming-is-enough misconception as a stated definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Unstylized-Photograph and Music-Critic Anchor (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Can a perfectly clear, unstyled photograph be just as vivid as a stylized one?"
2. **The figurative-versus-literal-imagery collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Does purely literal sensory description still create a vivid sensory experience?"
3. **The sense-naming-versus-effect-explanation collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Which analysis actually tells us how this imagery is functioning — just naming the sense, or explaining the effect too?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by testing candidate passages against the figurative-requirement and sense-naming-is-enough assumptions.

1. **Need**: "If a sentence describes a smell with no metaphor at all, can it still be vivid and evocative?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely identifies which sense five short phrases appeal to before being told the official literal-counts-too test.
3. **Invention**: "Can you find or write a piece of purely literal sensory description that's still genuinely vivid?"
4. **Collision**: present two analyses of the same imagery, one stopping at sense-naming and one continuing to effect, and ask which is more complete.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — literal sensory description counts as imagery, and naming the sense is only the first step, not the whole analysis — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Literal counts too; naming the sense is step one, not the finish line."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Identifying imagery across the five senses (TA-1) comes first**, establishing the basic sense-recognition skill before either misconception is directly confronted.
- **Literal sensory description also counts (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting the first misconception using the sense-recognition skill just established.
- **Explaining the specific effect of imagery (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting the second misconception once a broad, accurate imagery inventory is secure.
- **Full application to a new passage (TA-4) comes last**, the generative capstone integrating both misconception fixes — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for classifying literal description as imagery, and their specific, context-grounded effect explanations**, since a correct-looking sense-label with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **Voluntary identification of purely literal sensory description as genuine imagery**, without requiring a metaphor or simile — the direct behavioral signature of the figurative-requirement fix taking hold.
- **A specific, context-grounded effect explanation** ("this creates an unpleasant, jarring atmosphere because...") beyond mere sense-naming — direct evidence the effect-explanation fix is internalized.
- **Stopping analysis at "this is imagery appealing to X" with no further explanation** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-B still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Literal description counts too — and naming the sense is only step one, not the finish line."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But there's no metaphor here, so it can't be imagery, right?"** — take this as an accurate observation about the absence of figurative language, not a mistake; respond by adding the broader test: "There's no metaphor, that's true — but is this language still specific and vivid, appealing to one of the five senses? If so, it's still imagery."
- **"I said it's imagery appealing to smell — isn't that the answer?"** — a genuine, reasonable stopping point given how much easier sense-naming is; respond by naming the additional layer: "That's a great first step, yes — but what SPECIFIC mood or feeling does this particular smell detail create here?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full new-passage analysis down to **identifying which sense a single purely literal phrase appeals to, with effect-explanation set aside.** Isolates the most basic literal-imagery recognition before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just find the sense word."** This directly licenses the sense-naming-is-enough pattern, undermining the effect-explanation discipline this concept specifically builds.
