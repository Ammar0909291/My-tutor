# Conjunctions — `eng.grammar.conjunctions`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.conjunctions` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Conjunctions" — words
  that connect words, phrases, or clauses, including coordinating,
  subordinating, and correlative conjunctions, taught with explicit
  attention to structural equality/dependency and systematic comma
  rules.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.word-classes-overview` — load-bearing
  because identifying conjunctions as one of the eight word classes,
  and reliably using function-based reasoning, is exactly the skill
  this concept extends into conjunction-type subcategorization and
  clause-relationship analysis.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.compound-sentences`. Coordinating
  and subordinating conjunctions are the exact mechanism by which
  compound (and complex) sentences are formed from independent and
  dependent clauses — this concept's structural distinction is a direct
  prerequisite for that downstream sentence-construction skill.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Join two independent clauses using coordinating conjunctions
   (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so), confirming both original
   sentences could stand alone.
2. Join clauses using subordinating conjunctions (because, although,
   when, if, since, while), correctly identifying which clause becomes
   dependent.
3. Use correlative conjunction pairs (either...or, neither...nor,
   both...and, not only...but also) correctly to join parallel
   elements.
4. Correctly place a comma before a coordinating conjunction joining
   two independent clauses.
5. Correctly place a comma based on subordinate-clause position — after
   a fronted subordinate clause, typically omitted when the subordinate
   clause follows the independent clause.

## Core Understanding

Conjunctions connect words, phrases, or clauses, but the two major
types work in genuinely different, non-interchangeable ways.
Coordinating conjunctions (and, but, or, so — remembered as FANBOYS:
for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so) join two EQUAL, independent ideas that
could each stand alone as complete sentences. Subordinating
conjunctions (because, although, when, if, since, while) join a
DEPENDENT clause to an independent one, creating an unequal,
specifically-typed logical relationship (cause, contrast, time,
condition) — the dependent clause cannot stand alone as a full
sentence. Simply substituting one type for the other changes the
sentence's meaning and grammaticality, not merely its style. A second,
equally load-bearing fact is that comma placement with conjunctions
follows learnable, systematic patterns, not randomness: a comma goes
BEFORE a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses ("I
was tired, but I kept working"), while for subordinating conjunctions,
a comma goes AFTER the subordinate clause when it comes FIRST
("Although I was tired, I kept working") but is typically omitted when
the subordinate clause comes SECOND ("I kept working although I was
tired") — checking conjunction TYPE and clause ORDER, not placing
commas by feel, reliably determines correct placement.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "conjunctions are all just connecting words that
  work the same way, and comma placement around them is a matter of
  feel"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to
  correct — a natural default since all connecting words share the
  single label "conjunction," and since comma rules are often absorbed
  informally through reading rather than explicitly taught, giving no
  built-in signal of either the equal/dependent structural distinction
  or the systematic comma pattern.
- **Intermediate model — "I can join clauses with coordinating and
  subordinating conjunctions"**: installed by TA-1's coordinating-
  conjunction practice and TA-2's subordinating-conjunction practice,
  using the Component 3 equal-bridge/leaning-bridge anchor to make the
  structural distinction concrete, resolving MC-ALL-CONJUNCTIONS-WORK-
  THE-SAME-WAY. Upgrade trigger: correctly identifying which clause
  becomes dependent when joining a fresh sentence pair with a
  subordinating conjunction, without confusing it with coordinating
  behavior.
- **Advanced model — "I can also use correlative conjunction pairs to
  join parallel elements"**: installed by TA-3's practice, extending
  the conjunction framework to a third, paired-structure category.
- **Expert model — "I place commas correctly based on conjunction type
  and clause order, following systematic rules rather than guessing"**:
  installed by TA-4's coordinating-conjunction comma practice and
  TA-5's subordinating-conjunction, clause-order-dependent comma
  practice, resolving MC-COMMA-PLACEMENT-WITH-CONJUNCTIONS-IS-RANDOM.
  This is the concept's genuine mastery bar — treating comma placement
  as a checkable rule, not a feel-based judgment.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 learners whose L1 has a different clause-joining/punctuation
  convention, or doesn't distinguish coordinating/subordinating
  relationships the same way, this concept should be treated as
  requiring explicit, dedicated contrastive teaching — comma-with-
  conjunction rules are English-specific conventions that don't
  reliably transfer from other punctuation systems, warranting
  dedicated practice rather than assumed intuitive transfer.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of how conjunctions are typically first absorbed. The all-
conjunctions-work-the-same failure exists because all connecting words
genuinely do share the single grammatical label "conjunction," and
because in casual speech, the structural distinction between equal and
dependent clauses is often glossed over without communicative
consequence — a learner reasonably treats "conjunction" as one
undifferentiated category, with nothing in casual usage marking the
genuine grammatical difference until a subordinating conjunction is
directly swapped for a coordinating one and the meaning shift is
noticed. The random-comma-placement failure exists because comma usage
is very often absorbed informally through reading exposure rather than
explicitly taught rule-by-rule, so a learner develops an intuitive "by
feel" sense that works reasonably well but has no explicit,
articulable basis — nothing in that informal acquisition process marks
the underlying systematic pattern (conjunction type + clause order)
until it is directly named and tested.

  category name) intrudes into the learner's expectation of uniform
  behavior, when in fact two structurally distinct subtypes operate
  under that shared label; the learner is not misapplying a wrong
  rule, but rather reasonably assuming a shared category label implies
  shared grammatical behavior, which happens not to hold here.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when joining two

  want one to depend on the other?" before choosing a conjunction is
  showing the structural-distinction discipline has generalized beyond

- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a genuine perceptual default rather than

  through extensive reading exposure rather than through explicit rule
  instruction, producing a functional but inarticulate "by feel" sense;
  the learner isn't following a false rule, they simply never received
  the explicit, checkable rule underlying their intuitive practice.

- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, before placing a

  showing the systematic-rule discipline has generalized beyond the
  specific TA-4/TA-5 practice sentences.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the equal-bridge vs. leaning-bridge anchor itself is
  the analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): two
  same-height blocks connected by a flat card (coordinating,
  representing equal, independent ideas) versus one taller block with a
  smaller, leaning block connected by a card (subordinating,
  representing a dependent relationship) directly embodies the
  structural equal-vs-dependent distinction. Breaking point: a bridge
  metaphor implies a single, static structural relationship, which can
  understate that the SAME pair of ideas can often be joined either
  way (coordinating OR subordinating) depending on the writer's
  intended emphasis — this entry treats the bridge anchor as the
  introductory frame for recognizing the two structures once chosen,
  not a claim that any given pair of ideas has only one correct
  joining method.
- **Alternative — two business partners vs. a manager and an
  assistant**: coordinating conjunctions join two "business partners"
  (equal standing, either could operate independently); subordinating
  conjunctions join a "manager and assistant" (the assistant/dependent
  clause exists in relation to, and cannot function independently of,
  the manager/independent clause). Breaking point: useful for
  reinforcing the equal-vs-dependent relationship structure, but less
  visually concrete and hands-on than the physical bridge anchor for
  building the classification habit.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe subordinating conjunctions or their
  clauses as "lesser" or "weaker" than coordinating structures**:
  framing the dependent clause as somehow inferior risks implying
  subordination is a grammatically deficient construction; both
  coordinate and subordinate clause structures are equally legitimate,
  fully grammatical, and each serves genuinely different communicative
  purposes (emphasis, logical relationship type) — "dependent" is a
  structural term, not a value judgment.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
equal-vs-dependent structural distinction and the systematic comma
pattern are both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to
guided discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured basic word
classes, is asked whether substituting "and" for "because" preserves
the cause-and-effect meaning); **playground/invention** (TA-1's
coordinating-conjunction practice across several sentence pairs builds
initial confidence with joining equal ideas); **collision** (the
Blueprint's own and/because substitution probe breaks the all-
conjunctions-work-the-same assumption; the comma-before-vs-after "but"
probe breaks the random-comma-placement assumption); **formalization**
(MP-5's own-words statement of both the structural distinction and the
comma-placement rules); **compression** (TA-5's fluent, clause-order-
dependent comma placement without each case separately re-derived).
The specific terms "coordinating," "subordinating," and "correlative"
are convention-bound grammatical terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying
distinctions through the anchor and TA-1–2; "FANBOYS" is explicitly
taught as a memory aid per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (coordinating conjunctions) is placed first as the concept's most
straightforward entry point, joining two independently-standing
sentences with FANBOYS conjunctions. TA-2 (subordinating conjunctions)
follows immediately, directly targeting MC-ALL-CONJUNCTIONS-WORK-THE-
SAME-WAY — per the Blueprint's own session architecture, this
conflict-evidence probe is placed right after TA-2, once the learner
has secure coordinating-conjunction practice to contrast against. TA-3
(correlative conjunctions) extends the framework to a third,
specialized paired-structure category, building on the now-secure
coordinating/subordinating distinction. TA-4 (comma placement with
coordinating conjunctions) is placed next, directly targeting MC-
COMMA-PLACEMENT-WITH-CONJUNCTIONS-IS-RANDOM, addressing the SECOND,
independent dimension (punctuation, not conjunction type) once the
learner already has secure conjunction-type classification to apply
the comma rule to. TA-5 (comma placement with subordinating
conjunctions, clause order) completes the comma-placement pattern,
extending it to the more complex, order-dependent subordinating case.
This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session
script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously identifies
  a fresh clause pair's relationship as equal or dependent before
  choosing a conjunction (rather than treating all connecting words as
  interchangeable), and spontaneously checks conjunction type and
  clause order before placing a comma — these are the primary
  diagnostic signals for whether TA-2 and TA-4/TA-5's disciplines have
  genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before placing a comma
  in a fresh sentence, followed by a correct, rule-based placement
  ("this is coordinating, so the comma goes before"), suggests the
  learner is consciously applying the systematic rule rather than
  guessing by feel — a good sign. Instant, confident but inconsistent
  comma placement across similar sentences is the concerning pattern
  for MC-COMMA-PLACEMENT-WITH-CONJUNCTIONS-IS-RANDOM.
- **Written-vs-spoken-fluency stakes**: real and specifically named in
  the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags — a learner who already joins
  clauses fluently in speech but writes run-ons or comma splices needs
  focused TA-4/TA-5 comma-placement practice specifically, since spoken
  fluency doesn't guarantee correct written punctuation.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "is this coordinating or subordinating,
  and does that change where the comma goes?" is this concept's
  load-bearing sentence for MC-COMMA-PLACEMENT-WITH-CONJUNCTIONS-IS-
  RANDOM, per `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md
  §3` — asked whenever a fresh conjunction-joined sentence is being
  punctuated, making the systematic check a routinely-invited response
  rather than a feel-based guess.
