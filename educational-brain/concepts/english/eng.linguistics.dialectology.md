## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Explain what an isogloss is and that different isoglosses for different features rarely align perfectly.
2. Explain that dialect regions involve gradual, overlapping transitions rather than sharp, uniform boundaries.
3. Explain that a named dialect is a bundle of many individually-varying features, not a monolithic category.
4. Analyze a new dataset of features' isoglosses and speaker profiles, explaining non-alignment and partial dialect membership.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **dialect boundaries are not sharp lines where speech changes abruptly and uniformly** — real dialect boundaries (isoglosses) are typically gradual, involve transition zones with mixed features, and different individual linguistic features often have DIFFERENT boundary lines that don't align with each other. Second, **a named dialect is not one single, monolithic thing everyone in a region either has or lacks entirely** — a dialect label is a convenient name for a BUNDLE of many individual features, and real speakers typically share MOST but rarely ALL of the bundle, with individual variation being the norm rather than an exception requiring explanation.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "dialect boundaries are sharp lines like a political border, and everyone labeled with a dialect must have every feature of it."**
The simulation: expect speech to change abruptly and uniformly at a single boundary line, and treat any speaker who lacks a "typical" feature of their named dialect as an anomaly or exception.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a dialect map showing multiple non-aligned isoglosses for different features, and two speakers of "the same dialect" who differ from each other on several specific features.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Each feature has its own isogloss; a dialect is a bundle of features, not a monolithic category."

**Intermediate — "I can now explain that isoglosses for different features don't align, but I still treat each individual dialect as internally uniform."**
The simulation: correctly accepts gradual, non-aligned isogloss transitions but still expects any given named dialect's speakers to share all its features identically.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct feature-bundle reasoning practice, given two speakers of the same dialect differing on specific features.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Accepting gradual boundaries between regions isn't the same as accepting variation within a single dialect label — both are true, separately."

**Advanced — "I explain non-aligned isoglosses and bundle-based, partial dialect membership together in a novel dataset."**
The simulation: integrates both the isogloss-non-alignment and feature-bundle understandings into one coherent regional-dialect analysis. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: none within the current curriculum sequence — this concept is confirmed terminal.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the core dialectology toolkit for analyzing any regional dialect dataset."

**Expert — "at fluency, recognizing gradual boundaries and feature-bundle variation happens as an integrated part of ordinary linguistic analysis, without a separate deliberate checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit isogloss-and-bundle-analysis routine is a scaffold for an analytical habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of linguistic reasoning.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **dialect maps and popular discussion of "regional accents" are commonly presented as single shaded regions with clean edges**, reinforcing a sharp-boundary visual model well before a learner ever sees multiple individual features' actual, non-aligned isoglosses.

The second mechanism is that **a dialect NAME functions grammatically like a single noun** ("Southern English," "Cockney"), which linguistically primes treating the named thing as one unified object rather than a convenient label for a loosely-correlated bundle of many separate, independently-varying features.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-DIALECT-BOUNDARIES-ARE-SHARP-LINES-WHERE-EVERYONE-ON-ONE-SIDE-SPEAKS-ONE-WAY-AND-EVERYONE-ON-THE-OTHER-SPEAKS-DIFFERENTLY
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from dialect maps' visual convention of shaded regions with clean edges, making a genuinely gradual reality look sharply bounded.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **show multiple real features' non-aligned isoglosses side by side**, since the gradual-transition reality is far more convincing demonstrated visually than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given two different features' isoglosses through the same region, the learner identifies where the lines diverge from each other.

### MC-B-A-DIALECT-IS-ONE-SINGLE-UNIFIED-THING-EVERYONE-IN-A-REGION-EITHER-HAS-OR-DOESNT-HAVE
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 3 (language contamination), from a dialect's NAME functioning grammatically as a single noun, priming a monolithic mental model of the thing it names.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct comparison of two real speakers labeled with the same dialect who differ on specific features**, since the bundle-based reality is far more convincing demonstrated through concrete individual variation than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given two speakers of the same named dialect differing on some features, the learner explains why this is expected, not contradictory.

## Analogies

**Best — the Climate-Zone-Gradual-Transition and Jazz-Musician-Family-Resemblance Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a climate map showing gradual, non-aligned transitions for temperature, rainfall, and vegetation — directly seeding the fix for MC-A. Jazz musicians sharing a broad family resemblance while each having a unique mix of specific techniques — directly seeding the fix for MC-B.
*Breaking point*: climate zones and jazz styles are both continuous, gradient-like categories in ways dialects partly are, but dialects also carry strong social identity and boundary-marking functions (a speaker may deliberately emphasize or suppress a feature for identity reasons) that climate and musical-style analogies don't capture.

**Alternative — a family resemblance among siblings.** Siblings share a recognizable family resemblance without any two looking identical or sharing every single trait — just as speakers of a named dialect share a resemblance without uniform, identical feature sets.
*Breaking point*: family resemblance arises from shared genetics with a clear biological mechanism, while dialect feature-sharing arises from social contact and exposure, a substantially different and more variable process.

**Visual anchor — the Climate-Zone-Gradual-Transition and Jazz-Musician-Family-Resemblance Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Dialect regions are like countries with clear borders on a map."** Directly installs the sharp-boundary misconception as a stated definition.
- **"If you're from that region, you speak that dialect, full stop."** Directly installs the monolithic-dialect misconception as a stated definition.

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by comparing real isogloss data and real speaker feature profiles.

1. **Need**: "If someone lives right between two 'dialect regions,' would you expect their speech to snap cleanly to one side or the other?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely compares two features' isoglosses on a sample map before being told the official non-alignment principle.
3. **Invention**: "List five features of a dialect you're familiar with — do you personally have all five, or just some?"
4. **Collision**: present two speakers labeled with the same dialect differing on several features, and ask whether that's a contradiction.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — isoglosses for different features rarely align, and a dialect is a feature bundle with normal partial membership — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Each feature has its own boundary; a dialect is a bundle, not a monolith."

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But the map shows one clear dialect region, so the boundary must be sharp"** — take this as an accurate observation about how maps are commonly drawn, not a mistake; respond by adding the stricter test: "The map simplifies it that way, that's true — but if you looked at each individual feature separately, would their boundaries fall in exactly the same place?"
- **"If they're both from that region, shouldn't they talk the same way?"** — a genuine, reasonable expectation given how dialect names function like single labels; respond by naming the additional layer: "They share the label, yes — but a dialect is really a bundle of many features. Do these two speakers have to share every single one?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full dataset analysis down to **comparing just two features' isoglosses for non-alignment, with speaker-profile bundle analysis set aside.** Isolates the most basic non-alignment observation before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just describe the region's typical dialect."** This directly licenses the monolithic pattern, undermining the feature-bundle discipline this concept specifically builds.
