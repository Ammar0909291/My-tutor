# Comparatives and Superlatives — `eng.grammar.comparatives-and-superlatives`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.comparatives-and-superlatives` (canonical
  English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Comparatives and
  Superlatives" — adjective and adverb forms used to compare two items
  (comparative) or three or more items (superlative), split between a
  "-er/-est" pattern for short words and a "more/most" pattern for
  longer words, plus a small irregular set.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.adjectives` AND `eng.grammar.adverbs`
  — load-bearing because comparative and superlative forms are
  DEGREE-MODIFICATIONS of adjectives and adverbs themselves; a learner
  who cannot yet reliably identify an adjective or adverb has no stable
  base form to apply the comparison rule to.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none listed — this is a terminal node in the
  current KG's grammar domain.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 1

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Produce comparative and superlative forms of short adjectives/
   adverbs using "-er/-est," including correct spelling changes
   (big→bigger, happy→happier).
2. Produce comparative and superlative forms of longer adjectives/
   adverbs using "more/most" (beautiful→more beautiful→most
   beautiful).
3. Select "-er/-est" vs. "more/most" correctly based on syllable
   count and word pattern, rather than applying one strategy
   universally.
4. Produce correct irregular comparative and superlative forms for a
   small, fixed set of common words (good/better/best, bad/worse/worst,
   far/farther-further/farthest-furthest, little/less/least,
   much-many/more/most).
5. Select and produce the correct form — comparative with "than" for
   exactly two items, superlative with "the" for three or more —
   based on the number of items being compared.

## Core Understanding

Comparing adjectives and adverbs in English is not one universal
pattern — it splits into two strategies chosen by SYLLABLE LENGTH and
word pattern, plus a small set of true exceptions. Short words (usually
1 syllable, and many common 2-syllable ones) take "-er/-est"
(tall→taller→tallest, happy→happier→happiest). Longer words (usually 3+
syllables, and some 2-syllable words ending in patterns like "-ful,"
"-ous," "-ing") take "more/most" instead (beautiful→more beautiful→most
beautiful). Neither strategy is universally correct — checking the
word's length and pattern is the reliable test, not personal preference
or defaulting to one approach for everything. A separate, equally
load-bearing fact is that a small set of very common, high-frequency
words (good, bad, far, little, much/many) have IRREGULAR comparative
and superlative forms (better/best, worse/worst, farther-further/
farthest-furthest, less/least, more/most) that follow neither the
"-er/-est" nor the "more/most" pattern — these must be memorized
directly as a fixed, learnable list, not derived from either regular
rule. Finally, the comparative and superlative forms are companion-word-
specific: comparative pairs with "than" for comparing exactly TWO items,
while superlative pairs with "the" for comparing THREE OR MORE items.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "there is one universal way to compare words, and
  irregular forms are just mistakes to be corrected"**: the arriving,
  pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct — a natural
  default because a learner's first-taught comparison pattern
  (typically "-er/-est," since it's introduced with the simplest, most
  concrete short words) feels like a general rule until tested against
  a longer word, and because irregular forms genuinely violate every
  regular pattern the learner has just learned, making a "correction"
  instinct reasonable.
- **Intermediate model — "I choose '-er/-est' for short words and
  'more/most' for longer words based on syllable count"**: installed by
  TA-1 and TA-2, using the Component 3 height-line comparison anchor,
  resolving MC-ALWAYS-ADD-ER-EST-OR-ALWAYS-USE-MORE-MOST. Upgrade
  trigger: correctly choosing "more/most" for a fresh multi-syllable
  word without defaulting to "-er/-est" out of habit.
- **Advanced model — "I recognize a small, fixed set of irregular
  comparative/superlative forms that must be memorized, not corrected
  to a regular pattern"**: installed by TA-3, resolving
  MC-IRREGULAR-COMPARATIVES-ARE-JUST-MISTAKES by directly validating
  these forms as genuine, correct exceptions.
- **Expert model — "I select comparative (with 'than,' two items) vs.
  superlative (with 'the,' three or more items) based on how many
  things are being compared"**: installed by TA-4, the concept's
  genuine mastery bar — applying both the form-selection rule and the
  quantity-based comparative/superlative choice together in realistic
  context.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 has a single universal comparison strategy (many
  languages use one particle or word regardless of adjective length),
  expect the "-er/-est" vs. "more/most" split, and especially the
  irregular forms, to be a persistent transfer challenge requiring
  dedicated, explicit practice — not a small detail to be quickly
  absorbed.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here. The one-universal-
strategy failure exists because a learner's first-taught comparison
examples are typically short, concrete words (tall, big, fast) that
correctly take "-er/-est" — extending that pattern universally is a
reasonable generalization from genuinely correct initial evidence, with
nothing marking the length-based split until a longer word like
"beautiful" is directly tested and "beautifuler" is shown to sound
wrong. The irregular-forms-are-mistakes failure exists because the
learner has JUST secured two reliable, rule-governed patterns
(-er/-est and more/most), making a word that follows neither pattern
feel like an error to be corrected back toward one of the two known
rules — nothing marks these as genuine, memorized exceptions until a
learner is directly asked whether "gooder" is a real word fluent
speakers use, and told instead about the small, fixed irregular list.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.comparatives-and-superlatives.md`, Component 1) documents
both misconceptions this concept produces, with full
trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content — reused by
reference below, with birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-ALWAYS-ADD-ER-EST-OR-ALWAYS-USE-MORE-MOST (Blueprint Component 1)
— Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — the first-
  taught comparison examples are typically short words that correctly
  take "-er/-est," so extending that pattern universally is a
  reasonable generalization from genuinely correct partial evidence;
  the misconception survives because the short-word pattern genuinely
  works for many common, frequently-used words, delaying the moment a
  longer word directly contradicts it.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when meeting a
  fresh multi-syllable adjective, spontaneously counts or estimates its
  syllable length before choosing between "-er/-est" and "more/most" is
  showing the syllable-count discipline has been genuinely
  internalized, not merely applied to the specific TA-1/TA-2 practice
  words.

**MC-IRREGULAR-COMPARATIVES-ARE-JUST-MISTAKES (Blueprint Component 1)
— Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — the learner has
  just secured two reliable, rule-governed comparison patterns, so a
  word following neither pattern reasonably feels like an error to be
  corrected toward one of the two known rules, rather than a legitimate
  third category; the misconception survives because regular rules
  genuinely do apply to the vast majority of adjectives/adverbs,
  making irregular forms feel like anomalies rather than a real,
  learnable exception category.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, upon meeting a
  fresh sentence requiring "worse" or "worst," reaches directly for the
  memorized irregular form rather than attempting "badder"/"baddest"
  first is showing the irregular-list-membership check has generalized
  beyond the specific TA-3 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the height-line comparison itself is the analogy,
  not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): lining up objects
  and comparing two of them ("taller than") versus all three
  ("tallest"), then repeating with a longer adjective using "more
  interesting"/"most interesting," directly embodies both the two-vs-
  three-or-more quantity distinction AND the short-word-vs-long-word
  strategy split within a single, concrete physical demonstration.
  Breaking point: the height-line anchor demonstrates the regular
  patterns well but doesn't on its own address the irregular-form
  exception category (TA-3), which needs its own separate, explicitly-
  named memorization practice.
- **Alternative — a ruler with two scales**: short measurements read
  directly off one scale (inches, "-er/-est"), long measurements switch
  to a second scale (feet, "more/most") — same underlying quantity
  (degree of comparison), different notation system depending on
  magnitude. Breaking point: useful for the length-based-switching
  insight, but doesn't address the irregular-form exception category,
  which the height-line anchor's dedicated TA-3 practice handles
  instead.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe irregular comparative/superlative
  forms as "random" or "arbitrary"**: this framing risks discouraging
  the memorization effort by implying the forms have no learnable
  structure; instead, frame them as a small, fixed, high-value list
  (five common words) worth memorizing directly precisely BECAUSE these
  words are used so frequently in everyday speech and writing.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the height-line comparison)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any two or three objects in the room (books,
  pencils, chairs of different sizes) supply real TA-1/TA-4 material;
  any two or three activities/experiences the learner has had supply
  real TA-2 material for comparing with longer adjectives (interesting,
  difficult, enjoyable).
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's two-vs-three-or-more
  selection task is inherently hands-on judgment practice, requiring
  the learner to count the items being compared before selecting the
  correct form, not merely recognize a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the real-word test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether
  "gooder" is a word fluent English speakers actually use, and eliciting
  "better" instead, making the irregular-form exception directly
  audible and memorable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  comparative/superlative form or type-selection attempt (TA-1 through
  TA-4), ask the learner to state their own attempt before the tutor
  confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions, though this
is a genuinely concise, one-hour concept per the KG's own estimate.**
The syllable-length split and the irregular-form exception category are
both genuine, directly testable phenomena well-suited to guided
discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured TA-1's short-word
"-er/-est" practice, is asked to form the comparative of "beautiful,"
discovering "beautifuler" feels wrong even before being told why);
**playground/invention** (TA-1's short-word practice builds initial
confidence with the clearly-taught pattern before its limits are
tested); **collision** (the Blueprint's own "beautifuler" probe breaks
the one-universal-strategy assumption; the "gooder" probe breaks the
irregular-forms-are-mistakes assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's
own-words statement of both the syllable-length decision rule and the
irregular-form exception list); **compression** (TA-4's fluent
selection between comparative and superlative based on item count,
applied directly without each instance separately re-derived). The
specific terms "comparative," "superlative," and "irregular" are
convention-bound grammatical terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying
distinctions through the height-line anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (short-word comparatives/superlatives) is placed first as the
concept's most stable, rule-governed entry point, per the Blueprint's
own height-line anchor — establishing the clearly-taught "-er/-est"
pattern, including spelling changes, before any exception is
introduced. TA-2 (long-word comparatives/superlatives) follows
immediately, directly targeting
MC-ALWAYS-ADD-ER-EST-OR-ALWAYS-USE-MORE-MOST — per the Blueprint's own
session architecture, this conflict-evidence probe is placed right
after TA-2, once the learner has secure short-word practice to contrast
the length-based split against. TA-3 (irregular comparatives/
superlatives) follows, directly targeting
MC-IRREGULAR-COMPARATIVES-ARE-JUST-MISTAKES, addressing a SECOND,
independent dimension (a small fixed exception set, not a length-based
rule) once the two regular strategies are secure. TA-4 (using
comparatives/superlatives in context — two vs. three+ items) is placed
last as the concept's integration capstone, requiring the learner to
select the correct FORM CATEGORY (comparative vs. superlative) based on
item count, applying all prior form-production skills in realistic
context. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn
session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously checks
  syllable length before choosing between "-er/-est" and "more/most"
  on a fresh word, and whether the learner spontaneously reaches for a
  memorized irregular form rather than attempting the regular pattern
  first on a known irregular word — these are the primary diagnostic
  signals for whether TA-2 and TA-3's disciplines have genuinely
  generalized beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before forming a fresh
  multi-syllable word's comparative, followed by a correct "more/most"
  choice, suggests the learner is consciously checking length rather
  than defaulting to "-er/-est" — a good sign. Instant, confident
  application of "-er/-est" to a known irregular or long word (without
  self-correction) is the concerning pattern for
  MC-ALWAYS-ADD-ER-EST-OR-ALWAYS-USE-MORE-MOST.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Protocol Routing — for S9 learners whose L1 uses a
  single universal comparison strategy, reassure that the length-based
  split and the irregular forms are well-documented, persistent
  transfer challenges requiring dedicated practice, not a sign of weak
  general English ability.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "is that a real word fluent speakers
  use?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for
  MC-IRREGULAR-COMPARATIVES-ARE-JUST-MISTAKES, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh over-regularized irregular attempt (like "gooder")
  is produced, making the irregular-list check a routinely-invited
  response rather than an afterthought.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a comparative/superlative production task
  shrinks to the Component 3 height-line anchor using the simplest,
  most familiar short-word example available, with the tutor modeling
  the "-er/-est" form before the learner attempts a fresh long-word or
  irregular sentence independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for
  MC-ALWAYS-ADD-ER-EST-OR-ALWAYS-USE-MORE-MOST, applying one strategy
  universally (either "-er/-est" everywhere or "more/most" everywhere)
  is more likely than "I don't know" — route directly to the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence probe rather than treating it as a
  generic vocabulary gap. For
  MC-IRREGULAR-COMPARATIVES-ARE-JUST-MISTAKES, over-regularizing a
  known irregular word is more likely than confusion — route directly
  to TA-3's real-word test, and explicitly validate the attempt as
  evidence of correct rule-learning before introducing the exception,
  per this domain's established over-regularization-is-healthy
  precedent from `eng.grammar.past-tenses`.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously self-corrects from an over-regularized form to the
  correct irregular form ("gooder — I mean, better") before being told
  is showing the irregular-list-membership discipline has generalized —
  this should be named and praised explicitly, per the Blueprint's own
  regulation-tail guidance (praising checking syllable length and
  irregular-list membership over guessing).

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedural/framework — a length-based two-strategy
production system (-er/-est vs. more/most) layered with a lexical
irregular-form exception set and a quantity-based comparative/
superlative selection rule, applied to genuinely novel words and
scenarios; mastery is demonstrated by correctly producing fresh long-
word and irregular forms and correctly selecting comparative vs.
superlative for fresh item-count scenarios (MP-1 through MP-4) rather
than by recognizing a fixed, previously-taught example list.
Interleaving partners: mix short-word and long-word production items
(testing TA-1/TA-2) with irregular-form items (testing TA-3) and
item-count-selection items (testing TA-4) in ongoing review, so the
learner continues exercising all three distinct dimensions as
separately-verified competencies.

- **Real-world**: every comparison the learner makes between two or
  more objects, people, or experiences — in speech or writing — offers
  immediate, ungated opportunities to apply the correct comparative or
  superlative form.
- **Expert-transfer**: precise, natural-sounding comparative language at
  any advanced level depends on correctly selecting among the three
  form categories (regular short, regular long, irregular) without
  conscious deliberation, since over-regularizing a common irregular
  word (or misapplying "-er/-est" to a long word) remains a noticeable
  marker of less fluent production even at otherwise advanced
  proficiency.
