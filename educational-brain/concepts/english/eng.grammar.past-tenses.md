# Past Tenses — `eng.grammar.past-tenses`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.past-tenses` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Past Tenses" — the
  simple, continuous, perfect, and perfect continuous past-tense forms,
  mirroring the present-tense system while adding the regular/irregular
  verb split.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.present-tenses` — load-bearing because
  the past-tense system directly mirrors the present-tense system's
  four-form architecture (simple/continuous/perfect/perfect-continuous);
  a learner without a secure present-tense contrast has no scaffolding
  to map the parallel past-tense distinctions onto.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.tense-consistency`,
  `eng.grammar.conditionals`. Maintaining a single, correctly-chosen
  tense across a passage (tense consistency) depends directly on first
  having secure, distinct past-tense forms to be consistent WITH; several
  conditional structures (e.g., the second and third conditionals)
  directly reuse this concept's simple past and past perfect forms in
  their own grammatical construction.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 3

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Produce regular simple past forms by adding "-ed," including correct
   spelling adjustments (hope→hoped, stop→stopped, cry→cried).
2. Produce irregular simple past forms for common high-frequency verbs
   (go, eat, see, have, be, take, make) from memorized forms, not the
   "-ed" rule.
3. Produce and identify past continuous forms (was/were + -ing) for
   actions in progress at a specific past moment, especially interrupted
   ones.
4. Produce and identify past perfect forms (had + past participle) for
   an action completed before another past action or time.
5. Produce past perfect continuous forms (had been + -ing) for an action
   ongoing for a duration up until another past point.

## Core Understanding

The past-tense system mirrors the present-tense system's four-form
architecture (simple/continuous/perfect/perfect-continuous), with one
major additional complication: forming the simple past splits verbs into
two groups. REGULAR verbs add "-ed" (walk→walked); a substantial set of
very common IRREGULAR verbs instead have their own unique, memorized
past form (go→went, eat→ate, see→saw) that does not follow the "-ed"
rule at all — no spelling adjustment can rescue "goed" into a correct
word. Beyond simple past, past continuous (was/were + -ing) marks an
action that was IN PROGRESS at a specific past moment, most
diagnostically used as the ongoing "background" for another action that
interrupts it ("I WAS eating dinner WHEN the phone rang") — this is
genuinely different from a sequence of completed actions in simple past
("I ate dinner, then watched TV"). Past perfect (had + past participle)
marks an action completed BEFORE another past action or time point — a
"past-before-past" sequencing tool ("I HAD finished dinner before she
arrived"). Past perfect continuous (had been + -ing) marks a duration
that was ongoing up until another past point ("I had been waiting for an
hour when the bus came"). All four forms answer the same four questions
the present-tense system asks, just relocated one step further into the
past.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "every past-tense verb is formed by adding -ed"**:
  the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct
  — a natural, even developmentally healthy, over-generalization of the
  one clearly-taught rule (regular "-ed" formation) to every verb,
  including the many common irregular verbs that don't follow it.
- **Intermediate model — "I can form regular past-tense verbs with -ed,
  and I recognize that common irregular verbs have their own memorized
  forms instead"**: installed by TA-1 and TA-2, resolving
  MC-ALL-PAST-TENSE-VERBS-ADD-ED. Upgrade trigger: correctly producing a
  fresh irregular verb's past form from memory without attempting "-ed"
  first.
- **Advanced model — "I distinguish past continuous (ongoing, often
  interrupted) from simple past (completed, often sequential)"**:
  installed by TA-3, using the Component 3 interrupted-action freeze-
  frame to demonstrate the ongoing-vs-completed distinction concretely,
  resolving
  MC-PAST-CONTINUOUS-AND-SIMPLE-PAST-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE. Upgrade
  trigger: correctly choosing continuous over simple past for a fresh
  interrupted-action scenario without prompting.
- **Expert model — "I produce past perfect for past-before-past
  sequencing and past perfect continuous for ongoing duration before a
  past point"**: installed by TA-4 and TA-5, the concept's genuine
  mastery bar — completing the four-form system in parallel with the
  present-tense architecture the learner already secured.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 lacks a similar irregular/regular verb split, or
  doesn't grammatically distinguish continuous/perfect aspect in the
  past, expect the irregular-verb memorization task (TA-2) and the
  aspectual distinctions (TA-3/TA-4/TA-5) to require substantial,
  dedicated, ongoing practice — this compounds the present-tense
  system's already-significant transfer challenges rather than
  introducing an unrelated new difficulty.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and one of them is
explicitly framed by the Blueprint as developmentally healthy rather
than a deficiency. The all-verbs-add-ed failure exists because the
regular "-ed" pattern genuinely IS the correctly-learned general rule —
over-regularizing it to irregular verbs is well-documented evidence the
learner has correctly generalized a pattern, not evidence of confusion;
the fix is adding a bounded, learnable, memorized exception list on top
of an already-correct rule, not re-teaching the rule itself. The
continuous-and-simple-are-interchangeable failure exists because both
forms genuinely describe something in the past, making them feel
functionally interchangeable at first contact — nothing marks past
continuous's specific "ongoing at a moment, often interrupted" job until
a learner directly contrasts an interrupted-action sentence against a
sequential-actions sentence and notices the two convey different
information about WHEN, relative to another event, the action was
happening.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.past-tenses.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions
this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-ALL-PAST-TENSE-VERBS-ADD-ED (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a textbook Type 1 signature, and one the
  Blueprint itself explicitly frames as developmentally healthy — the
  regular "-ed" rule is the first, clearly-taught pattern, and applying
  it universally (including to irregular verbs) is well-documented
  evidence of correct rule acquisition, not confusion; the misconception
  survives because the rule genuinely works for the majority of English
  verbs, with irregular verbs forming a bounded but high-frequency
  exception set.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when meeting a
  fresh irregular verb, pauses and reaches for a memorized form instead
  of automatically appending "-ed" is showing the regular/irregular
  split has been genuinely internalized, not merely memorized for the
  specific verbs drilled in TA-2.

**MC-PAST-CONTINUOUS-AND-SIMPLE-PAST-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — both forms
  genuinely describe something in the past, so treating them as
  interchangeable is a reasonable extension of "past tense = before
  now" until directly shown the ongoing-vs-completed distinction that
  separates them, most sharply visible in interrupted-action sentences.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when narrating a
  fresh interrupted-action scenario, spontaneously reaches for past
  continuous for the background action and simple past for the
  interrupting event, without being told which form serves which role,
  is showing the ongoing-vs-completed distinction has generalized beyond
  the specific TA-3 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the interrupted-action freeze-frame itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): acting out
  an ongoing action (washing dishes) interrupted by an event (a bell
  ringing), then narrating "I WAS washing dishes WHEN the bell rang,"
  directly embodies the ongoing-background-plus-interrupting-event
  structure, contrasted against acting out two sequential completed
  actions (wash, then dry) narrated entirely in simple past. Breaking
  point: the freeze-frame technique demonstrates continuous-vs-simple
  cleanly but does not on its own address the regular/irregular verb
  split (TA-1/TA-2) or the past-before-past sequencing of past perfect
  (TA-4) — those require their own separate demonstrations.
- **Alternative — a movie still-frame vs. a movie's finished scene
  list**: pausing a movie mid-action (past continuous — the action was
  captured still in progress) versus listing the scenes that already
  played out in order (simple past — each one complete before the next
  began). Breaking point: useful for the ongoing-vs-completed contrast,
  but doesn't naturally extend to the irregular-verb memorization task,
  which needs its own dedicated practice.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe irregular verbs as "broken" or
  "exceptions to be avoided"**: framing irregular verbs negatively risks
  discouraging a learner already coping with a genuine memorization
  burden, and understates that these are among the MOST common, high-
  frequency verbs in English (be, go, have, do, say, see) — they are not
  rare edge cases but core vocabulary that must be secured early, not
  minimized as an afterthought.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the interrupted-action freeze-frame)**: the
  Blueprint's Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any everyday action the learner can narrate
  (yesterday's routine) supplies real TA-1/TA-2 simple-past material;
  any genuine interrupted moment from the learner's own recent
  experience (was doing X when Y happened) supplies real TA-3 material.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-3's live freeze-frame acting-out
  is inherently hands-on, requiring the learner to physically enact and
  narrate the interruption moment, not merely recognize a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the goed test)**: the tutor models the Blueprint's own
  conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether "I goed to the
  store" sounds like something a fluent speaker would say, and eliciting
  the correct irregular form "went," making the regular/irregular split
  directly audible and memorable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a past-
  tense form identification or production attempt (TA-1 through TA-5),
  ask the learner to state their own attempt before the tutor confirms —
  Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
regular/irregular split and the continuous-vs-simple distinction are
both genuine, directly testable phenomena well-suited to guided
discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured TA-1's regular "-ed"
pattern, is asked to form the past tense of "go," discovering "goed"
feels wrong even before being told why); **playground/invention**
(TA-1's regular-verb practice builds initial confidence with the
clearly-taught rule before its limits are tested); **collision** (the
Blueprint's own "I goed to the store" probe breaks the all-verbs-add-ed
assumption; the interrupted-dinner probe breaks the continuous-and-
simple-are-interchangeable assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's own-
words statement of both the regular/irregular split and the continuous-
vs-simple distinction); **compression** (TA-5's fluent production of
past perfect continuous, treating it as a natural extension of the now-
secure perfect-and-continuous aspects rather than a fifth unrelated form
to separately memorize). The specific terms "regular," "irregular,"
"past continuous," "past perfect," and "past perfect continuous" are
convention-bound grammatical terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying
distinctions through the freeze-frame anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (regular simple past with "-ed") is placed first as the concept's
most stable, rule-governed entry point, per the Blueprint's own session
architecture — establishing the clearly-taught general pattern,
including spelling adjustments, before any exception is introduced.
TA-2 (irregular simple past) follows immediately, directly targeting
MC-ALL-PAST-TENSE-VERBS-ADD-ED — per the Blueprint's own session
architecture, this conflict-evidence probe is placed right after TA-2,
once the learner has secure regular-verb practice to contrast the
exceptions against. TA-3 (past continuous for ongoing/interrupted
actions) follows, directly targeting
MC-PAST-CONTINUOUS-AND-SIMPLE-PAST-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE using the freeze-
frame anchor, addressing a SECOND, independent dimension (aspectual
distinction, not verb-form irregularity) once basic simple-past
production is secure. TA-4 (past perfect for past-before-past
sequencing) extends the concept into relative time ordering between two
past events, building on the now-secure simple-past forms. TA-5 (past
perfect continuous for ongoing duration before a past point) is placed
last as the concept's integration capstone, combining the perfect
aspect from TA-4 with the continuous aspect from TA-3, completing the
four-form system in structural parallel with the present-tense system
the learner already secured. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's
own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References
below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously reaches for
  a memorized irregular form rather than defaulting to "-ed" on a fresh
  common verb, and whether the learner spontaneously chooses past
  continuous for the background action in a fresh interrupted-action
  narration — these are the primary diagnostic signals for whether
  TA-2 and TA-3's disciplines have genuinely generalized beyond the
  specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before producing a
  fresh irregular verb's past form, followed by the correct memorized
  form, suggests the learner is consciously retrieving from the
  irregular-verb set rather than guessing — a good sign. Instant,
  confident "-ed" application to a known irregular verb (without self-
  correction) is the concerning pattern for
  MC-ALL-PAST-TENSE-VERBS-ADD-ED.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Protocol Routing — for S9 learners whose L1 lacks a
  similar irregular/regular split or continuous/perfect aspect marking
  in the past, reassure that this compounds, rather than repeats, the
  present-tense system's already-acknowledged transfer challenges, and
  budget substantial ongoing practice accordingly.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "was that happening, or did it already
  finish?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for
  MC-PAST-CONTINUOUS-AND-SIMPLE-PAST-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh interrupted-action or sequential-actions sentence is
  introduced, making the ongoing-vs-completed check a routinely-invited
  response rather than an afterthought.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a past-tense production task shrinks to the
  Component 3 freeze-frame anchor using the simplest, most familiar
  regular verb available, with the tutor modeling the simple-past form
  before the learner attempts a fresh irregular or continuous-tense
  sentence independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for
  MC-ALL-PAST-TENSE-VERBS-ADD-ED, producing an over-regularized form
  like "goed" or "eated" is more likely than "I don't know" — route
  directly to the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence probe rather than
  treating it as a generic tense gap, and explicitly validate the
  attempt as evidence of correct rule-learning per the Blueprint's own
  Adaptive Flags. For
  MC-PAST-CONTINUOUS-AND-SIMPLE-PAST-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE, using simple
  past for an ongoing/interrupted action is more likely than confusion —
  route directly to TA-3's freeze-frame contrast.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously self-corrects from an over-regularized form to the
  correct irregular form ("I goed — I mean, I went") before being told
  is showing the regular/irregular split has generalized — this should
  be named and praised explicitly, per the Blueprint's own regulation-
  tail guidance (praising checking irregular-verb lists and interruption
  structure over defaulting to -ed).

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedural/framework — a four-form tense-and-aspect
system (simple/continuous/perfect/perfect-continuous) layered on top of
a lexical regular/irregular verb-form split, applied to genuinely novel
verbs and scenarios; mastery is demonstrated by correctly producing
fresh irregular forms and correctly choosing among the four aspectual
forms for fresh interrupted/sequential/past-before-past scenarios
(MP-1 through MP-4) rather than by recognizing a fixed, previously-
taught example list. Interleaving partners: mix regular/irregular
production items (testing TA-1/TA-2) with aspectual-choice items
(testing TA-3/TA-4/TA-5) in ongoing review, so the learner continues
exercising both the lexical-memorization and the grammatical-aspect
dimensions of the past-tense system.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: producing fresh regular and irregular simple-past forms;
  correctly choosing past continuous vs. simple past for fresh
  interrupted-action or sequential-action scenarios; producing past
  perfect for fresh past-before-past sequencing.
- **Far**: this concept's four-form tense-and-aspect architecture,
  layered on the regular/irregular verb-form split, is the direct
  prerequisite for `eng.grammar.tense-consistency`'s requirement to
  maintain a single, correctly-chosen tense throughout a passage — a
  skill that presupposes each individual past-tense form is already
  secure; the simple past and past perfect forms this concept installs
  are directly reused, unchanged, in `eng.grammar.conditionals`'s second-
  and third-conditional constructions.
- **Real-world**: every past-event narration the learner produces — in
  speech or writing — offers immediate, ungated opportunities to apply
  the correct regular/irregular form and the correct aspectual choice
  (ongoing vs. completed vs. before-another-past-event).
- **Expert-transfer**: fluent, natural-sounding storytelling and
  historical or personal-narrative writing at any advanced level depends
  on correctly sequencing simple past, past continuous, and past perfect
  to convey WHEN, relative to other past events, each action occurred —
  misuse of past perfect for past-before-past sequencing remains a
  noticeable marker of non-native production even at otherwise advanced
  proficiency.
