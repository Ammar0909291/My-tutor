## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify which story moments deserve full scene development and which should be summarized, with justification.
2. Convert a summarized conversation into rendered dialogue revealing character voice.
3. Develop a bare narrative moment with specific sensory details.
4. Combine summarized time and one fully-developed scene in a short passage.
5. Compose a complete original narrative using selective scene development, sensory detail, and rendered dialogue.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept, applying `eng.writing.transitions-and-cohesion`'s connective skills to full narrative pacing. First, **good narrative does not list everything that happened in order** — treating narrative as a chronological report of events, with equal attention to each, produces a report, not an experience; good narrative SLOWS DOWN on the 1-2 moments that matter most, developing them with sensory detail and specific dialogue, while moving quickly or skipping entirely over less important stretches of time. Second, **dialogue is not decoration that can always be summarized instead** — summarizing a conversation ("I told her I was nervous") tells the reader THAT something happened, while rendering the actual exchange ("'I don't think I can do this,' I whispered...") lets the reader experience HOW it happened, revealing character voice and creating immediacy that summary flattens away; for plot-critical or emotionally significant moments, actual dialogue is far more powerful than summary.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "a good narrative lists everything that happened in order; dialogue can always be summarized instead of written out."**
The simulation: recount events chronologically with uniform attention, and compress conversations into narration ("we talked about it").
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a flat event-list versus a narrative that slows down on one vivid moment, asked which makes the reader feel present; and a summarized reassurance versus rendered dialogue, asked which lets the reader experience the moment.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Slow down on what matters, summarize the rest. Render key dialogue — don't summarize it away."

**Intermediate — "I slow down on key moments now, but I still tend to summarize conversations even at emotionally significant points."**
The simulation: correctly selects moments for scene development but hasn't yet reliably rendered dialogue at those same key moments.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct practice converting a summarized conversation into actual rendered dialogue.
*Shelf-life warning*: "At your key moment, don't just summarize what was said — write the actual words."

**Advanced — "I select moments for development versus summary, develop scenes with sensory detail, render key dialogue, and compose a full original narrative integrating all three."**
The simulation: integrates selective pacing, sensory scene development, and dialogue rendering into one coherent, generative composition. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the two direct cross-linked concepts — the writing process (general composition strategy) and creative writing forms (extending selective development to other genres).
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you narrative pacing and dialogue. Creative writing forms extends this selective-development principle to other genres."

**Expert — "at fluency, selective pacing and dialogue rendering happen as an integrated part of ordinary narrative drafting."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit zoom-lens routine is a scaffold for a compositional judgment that, at true fluency, operates as an integrated part of drafting.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **a chronological, complete accounting of events is a genuinely easier organizing principle than selective, effect-driven pacing**, since "what happened, in order" requires no judgment calls about relative importance, while slowing down on some moments and skipping others demands the writer to actively evaluate significance.

The second mechanism is that **summarizing a conversation is a natural default from everyday storytelling** ("I told her I was scared, and she reassured me" is how most people recount conversations casually), so the habit transfers into writing without the deliberate craft decision to render key exchanges verbatim.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-GOOD-NARRATIVE-JUST-LISTS-EVERYTHING-THAT-HAPPENED-IN-ORDER
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry — the flat-event-list/developed-moment discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the genuinely useful chronological-accuracy habit, extended into treating uniform, equal-attention listing as sufficient for a compelling narrative, when selective development is actually required.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **require the learner to sort real events into slow-down-versus-summarize categories before drafting**, since the fix requires a deliberate significance-judgment step, not merely being told to "add more detail somewhere."
- **Verification of death**: given a sequence of story events, the learner correctly identifies which moments deserve full scene development and which should be summarized, with justification.

### MC-DIALOGUE-IS-JUST-DECORATION-AND-CAN-BE-SUMMARIZED-INSTEAD
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — the summarized-reassurance/rendered-dialogue discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from everyday casual storytelling's natural habit of summarizing conversations, transferring into written narrative without the deliberate craft correction.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct practice converting a summary into rendered dialogue**, since the fix requires developing the generative skill of writing actual exchange, not merely recognizing that dialogue is powerful.
- **Verification of death**: given a summarized conversation, the learner rewrites it as actual rendered dialogue revealing character voice.

## Analogies

**Best — the Camera Zoom Lens** (Blueprint Component 3): a "wide shot" quickly panning over unimportant stretches versus a "close-up" zooming tight on one key moment directly seeds the selective-pacing fix; a microphone attached specifically to the close-up setting, capturing actual voices rather than narration describing that talking happened, directly seeds the dialogue-rendering fix.
*Breaking point*: a camera's zoom is a purely visual, mechanical operation with an objective "close" versus "far" setting, while a writer's judgment about which narrative moments deserve development is a more interpretive, effect-driven decision — useful for the selective-attention intuition, less precise for implying scene-selection is as mechanically obvious as a zoom setting.

**Alternative — a documentary editor choosing which raw footage becomes a highlight reel versus a full unedited recording.** An editor doesn't include every second of raw footage; they select the moments that carry the story's emotional weight and compress or cut the rest — paralleling selective narrative pacing.
*Breaking point*: a documentary editor works from footage that already exists and must be selected FROM, while a narrative writer is generating the content itself, a meaningfully different creative task than selection alone.

**Visual anchor — the Camera Zoom Lens**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Just write down everything that happened, in the order it happened."** Directly installs the uniform-listing misconception as a stated instruction.
- **"You can always just say what people talked about instead of writing it out."** Directly installs the dialogue-as-decoration misconception as a stated shortcut.

3. **The summarized-reassurance/rendered-dialogue collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Which version lets you experience the moment versus just learn its outcome?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both pacing facts are directly checkable by comparing developed and undeveloped treatments of the same events.

1. **Need**: "If you write down every single thing that happened today in equal detail, would that be an interesting story?"
2. **Playground**: the learner sorts several story events into slow-down/summarize freely before being told the official selective-pacing principle.
3. **Invention**: "Can you take this summarized conversation and write out the actual words each person might have said?"
4. **Collision**: present a flat event-list and a one-moment-developed narrative; ask which feels more like an experience.
5. **Formalization**: name the two pacing facts — narrative slows down on what matters, not everything equally; key dialogue should be rendered, not summarized — from the learner's own comparisons.
6. **Compression**: "Zoom in on what matters, out on what doesn't — and let characters actually speak."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Slow-down vs. summarize sorting (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the uniform-listing misconception before scene-development technique is taught.
- **Developing a scene with sensory detail (TA-2) comes second**, teaching the positive technique for the moments TA-1 identified as deserving development.
- **Rendering dialogue instead of summarizing (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting the dialogue misconception once sensory scene-building is established.
- **Combining summary and scene (TA-4) and composing a full original narrative (TA-5) come last**, integrating both fixes into a generative capstone — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for why a moment deserves development or summary, and their drafted dialogue itself**, since a technically-complete narrative with no stated pacing rationale, or dialogue that reads as flat summary in quotation marks, cannot be distinguished from surface compliance without genuine craft understanding.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated significance check** ("this is the moment that matters, so I'm going to slow down here") applied before drafting — the direct behavioral signature of the selective-pacing fix taking hold.
- **Actual quoted dialogue with distinct character voices** at a key emotional moment, rather than narrated summary — direct evidence the dialogue-rendering fix is internalized.
- **A narrative giving equal weight to every event, or a key emotional exchange reduced to a single summarizing sentence** — the direct behavioral signature of either misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Zoom in on what matters, zoom out on what doesn't — and let your characters actually speak."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"I wrote down everything that happened, isn't that a complete story?"** — take this as a reasonable, thorough instinct; respond by asking for the significant moment: "It's complete, but which ONE moment here matters most? Let's slow down there and speed through the rest."
- **"I just said they talked about it, do I need to write out what they said?"** — a reasonable default from casual storytelling habits; respond by asking for the missed opportunity: "What if we heard their actual words? What would that reveal that 'they talked about it' doesn't?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full original narrative down to **converting a single summarized line into rendered dialogue, with no full-narrative or scene-development task demanded.** Isolates the most basic dialogue-rendering skill before returning to fuller composition.
- **Never shrink to "just add a little more detail somewhere."** This licenses vague elaboration without the selective, significance-driven pacing discipline this concept specifically builds.
