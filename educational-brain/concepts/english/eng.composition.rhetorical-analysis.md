## Learning Objective
The learner will analyze how a text's rhetorical choices work TOGETHER to achieve its purpose on its audience, producing a synthesized judgment of effectiveness — not a mere inventory of devices spotted.

## Core Understanding
Rhetorical analysis asks not "what devices are present" but "how do the devices work together to make this text effective (or not) for its purpose and audience." A device inventory ("the author uses a metaphor here, alliteration there, a rhetorical question here") is the RAW MATERIAL of an analysis, not the analysis itself — the actual analytic move is explaining how those choices combine to serve the text's persuasive or communicative goal, and then judging how well they succeed. This is the same distinction as a recipe (the ingredients and steps) versus a judgment about whether the finished dish actually works. Evaluating effectiveness must be argued from the text's own apparent purpose and audience — not from whether the analyst personally agrees with the argument being made. A rhetorically skillful text can argue for a conclusion the analyst disagrees with; the analysis judges the CRAFT, not the analyst's agreement.

## Mental Models
- **Recipe-versus-Ingredient-List Anchor**: listing "flour, sugar, eggs, butter" is not a review of a cake — a review says how the ingredients came together and whether the result actually tastes good for what it was trying to be (a birthday cake vs. a wedding cake have different standards). Rhetorical analysis is the review, not the ingredient list.
- **Chess-Judge extension (for MC-B)**: a chess commentator evaluating a game judges the QUALITY of the moves relative to the position, not whether they personally wanted that player to win. Judging a text's rhetorical effectiveness works the same way — evaluate the moves' quality relative to the audience and purpose, independent of the analyst's own agreement with the conclusion.

## Why Students Fail
Device-spotting is a concrete, checklist-like task that feels like "doing the analysis," so learners stop there without taking the harder synthesizing step of explaining how the devices function together. Separately, "evaluate effectiveness" gets misread as "say whether I agree," because personal agreement is the most available reaction, and distinguishing "this persuaded me because I already agreed" from "this is objectively well-crafted for its audience" takes deliberate effort.

## Misconceptions
- **MC-A (Type 5, instruction-induced from device-identification drills)**: "Rhetorical analysis means listing every device I can find in the text." Diagnostic: ask the learner what their thesis/central claim about the text's effectiveness is — if they can only produce a list of spotted devices with no overarching claim, MC-A is active. Repair: recipe-versus-ingredient-list anchor — require a one-sentence effectiveness claim BEFORE any device is discussed, then require every device mentioned to explicitly serve that claim.
- **MC-B (Type 3, language contamination from "evaluate" meaning "give my opinion" in casual usage)**: "Evaluating effectiveness means stating whether I personally agree with the argument." Diagnostic: give the learner a rhetorically skillful text arguing for a position they disagree with, and ask them to evaluate its effectiveness — if they rate it low purely because they disagree, MC-B is active. Repair: chess-judge framing — separate "is this move good for this position" from "do I want this player to win," then apply the same separation to text and argument.

## Analogies
- Recipe-versus-ingredient-list anchor (above) for the inventory-vs-synthesis distinction.
- Chess-judge extension (above) for the effectiveness-vs-agreement distinction.
- Anti-analogy: this is not "find the most devices" (a quantity contest) — a text with three well-integrated devices can be more effective than one with ten scattered, unintegrated ones.

## Demonstrations
- Take a short persuasive text; first produce a pure device list (the "ingredients"), then produce a one-paragraph synthesis explaining how those devices combine to serve the text's purpose for its specific audience (the "review").
- Present a rhetorically skillful text arguing a position most learners in the room would disagree with; have the class evaluate its rhetorical craft while explicitly separating that from their agreement.
- Compare two texts on the same topic — one device-dense but incoherent, one sparer but well-integrated — and have the learner judge which is more rhetorically EFFECTIVE, not which uses more devices.

## Discovery Questions
- "What's your one-sentence claim about how well this text works for its purpose and audience — before you list a single device?"
- "Does this device list add up to an explanation of HOW the text works, or is it just a list?"
- "Are you rating this text low because it's poorly crafted, or because you disagree with what it's arguing?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Anchor: recipe-versus-ingredient-list framing.
2. Model: produce a device inventory, then explicitly upgrade it into a synthesis paragraph on a shared example text.
3. Guided: learner writes a one-sentence effectiveness claim before touching any device (targets MC-A directly).
4. Introduce chess-judge framing; present a text the learner likely disagrees with and have them evaluate craft while separating it from agreement (targets MC-B directly).
5. Independent: full rhetorical analysis (claim + device synthesis + effectiveness judgment) on a new text.
6. Confirm: learner explains, unprompted, the difference between an inventory and an analysis, and between agreement and effectiveness.

## Tutor Actions
- SHOW: the ingredient-list vs. review pair on a shared text.
- TEST-THINKING: require a one-sentence effectiveness claim before any device discussion.
- DO: have the learner analyze a text arguing a position they disagree with, explicitly bracketing agreement.
- ORGANIZE: a "device → function → contribution to purpose" table to force synthesis rather than listing.

## Voice Teaching Notes
When a learner's tone shifts to visible frustration or dismissiveness while analyzing a text they disagree with, pause and explicitly re-invoke the chess-judge framing before continuing — this is exactly the moment MC-B is most likely to override the analytic task.

## Assessment Signals
- Probe: "Here's a device list for this text. What's missing to make this an analysis?" — correct answer names the synthesis/purpose claim.
- Probe: given a skillful text on a disputed topic, "rate its rhetorical effectiveness and justify your rating using only craft criteria" — an MC-B response smuggles in disagreement as the justification.
- Confident-wrong signature: a long, detailed device inventory delivered as if it were a completed analysis, with no synthesizing claim anywhere — flag as MC-A regardless of the inventory's accuracy.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If a learner keeps collapsing effectiveness into agreement (MC-B) even after the chess-judge framing, switch to a domain with lower personal stakes (e.g., an advertisement or speech on a topic the learner has no strong opinion about) to isolate the craft-judgment skill before returning to higher-stakes topics.

## Memory Hooks
"Ingredient list vs. review" as the retrievable check before submitting any rhetorical analysis; "would I say this move is good even if I wanted the other player to win?" as the retrievable check for effectiveness-vs-agreement.
