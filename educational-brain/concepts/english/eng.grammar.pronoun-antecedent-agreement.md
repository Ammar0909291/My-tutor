# Pronoun-Antecedent Agreement — `eng.grammar.pronoun-antecedent-agreement`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.pronoun-antecedent-agreement` (canonical
  English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Pronoun-Antecedent
  Agreement" — the rule that a pronoun must agree in number and gender
  with the noun it refers back to.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.pronouns` AND `eng.grammar.subject-

  pronoun antecedents (TA-2) directly reuses that concept's verb-
  agreement test (checking is/are) as proof of grammatical number,
  applying the identical true-governing-element discipline to a new
  pairing.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none listed — this is a terminal node in the
  current KG's grammar domain.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 1

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Select the correctly agreeing pronoun for clear singular and plural
   antecedents.
2. Confirm the singular grammatical status of indefinite-pronoun
   antecedents (everyone, someone, each) using the verb-agreement test,
   then select an appropriately agreeing pronoun.
3. Identify genuinely ambiguous pronoun reference — where a pronoun has
   two or more possible antecedents — using the antecedent-arrow
   technique.
4. Correct ambiguous pronoun reference by rewriting the sentence (using
   a name, restructuring, or adding clarifying detail).
5. Explain, in their own words, why indefinite pronouns are
   grammatically singular and why ambiguous reference is a genuine
   communication failure, not a stylistic nitpick.

## Core Understanding

A pronoun must agree in number and gender with its ANTECEDENT — the
noun it refers back to. The most load-bearing insight for indefinite-
pronoun antecedents (everyone, someone, each, everybody, anybody) is
that these words are grammatically SINGULAR, even though they
conceptually refer to a group of people — this is confirmed by their
singular verb agreement ("Everyone IS here," never "ARE"), directly
reusing the diagnostic test already established for subject-verb
agreement. Traditionally, formal grammar pairs these singular
antecedents with "his or her"; modern standard usage increasingly
accepts singular "they/their" as a gender-neutral option — but
recognizing the SINGULAR GRAMMATICAL STATUS itself (regardless of which
specific pronoun option is chosen) is the concept's key point. A
second, independent, equally load-bearing insight concerns AMBIGUOUS
reference: a pronoun must have exactly ONE possible antecedent in a
sentence. When two or more candidates exist ("Sarah told Maria that SHE
had won the award" — "she" could be Sarah or Maria), the reader
genuinely CANNOT determine the intended meaning without additional
information — this is a real communication failure, not something
"context makes obvious," since the writer's own inside knowledge isn't
available to the reader. This ambiguity must be actively caught by
re-reading one's own writing as if seeing it for the first time.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "words like 'everyone' should get a plural pronoun
  since they mean many people, and ambiguous pronouns are fine as long
  as I know what I meant"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this
  concept exists to correct — a natural default because indefinite
  pronouns like "everyone" genuinely DO conceptually refer to multiple
  people, making a plural pronoun feel logically consistent, and
  because the writer's own certainty about intended meaning creates a
  false sense that the sentence is equally clear to a reader without
  that inside knowledge.
- **Intermediate model — "I select the correctly agreeing pronoun for
  clear singular and plural antecedents"**: installed by TA-1, using
  the Component 3 antecedent-arrow-trace anchor, establishing the base
  agreement principle before any complication is introduced.
- **Advanced model — "I confirm indefinite-pronoun antecedents are
  singular using the verb-agreement test, then select an appropriately
  agreeing pronoun"**: installed by TA-2, resolving
  MC-INDEFINITE-PRONOUNS-ARE-ALWAYS-PLURAL by directly reusing the
  is/are diagnostic already established in the prerequisite subject-
  verb-agreement concept.
- **Expert model — "I identify and correct genuinely ambiguous pronoun
  reference"**: installed by TA-3 and TA-4, the concept's genuine
  mastery bar, resolving
  MC-AMBIGUOUS-PRONOUN-REFERENCE-IS-FINE-IF-CONTEXT-SEEMS-CLEAR by
  requiring the learner to actively check whether a pronoun has exactly
  one possible antecedent, from a reader's perspective, not the
  writer's.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 doesn't require pronoun-antecedent number
  agreement, or handles ambiguous reference differently (relying more
  heavily on context or omission), expect indefinite-pronoun agreement
  and ambiguity-detection to require dedicated, explicit practice,
  since both rely on English-specific grammatical conventions and
  proofreading habits that may not transfer directly.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and the Blueprint
itself explicitly frames the second as a proofreading skill as much as
a grammar rule. The indefinite-pronouns-always-plural failure exists
because words like "everyone" genuinely DO conceptually refer to
multiple people, making the plural-meaning-requires-plural-form
inference a reasonable, logically-motivated generalization — the same
failure pattern already established for collective nouns in the
prerequisite subject-verb-agreement concept, now applied to
pronoun-antecedent pairs; nothing marks the singular grammatical status
until a learner is directly asked whether "everyone" takes "is" or
"are." The ambiguity-is-fine-if-context-seems-clear failure exists
because the WRITER always already knows their own intended meaning,
creating a persistent, structurally-guaranteed blind spot — the
sentence genuinely does feel unambiguous to its own author, with
nothing marking the reader's actual confusion until the writer is
directly asked whether the sentence alone, without their own prior
knowledge, actually determines which of two candidate antecedents is
meant.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.pronoun-antecedent-agreement.md`, Component 1) documents
both misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/
bridge/replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below,
with birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-INDEFINITE-PRONOUNS-ARE-ALWAYS-PLURAL (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature, and directly
  parallel to `eng.grammar.subject-verb-agreement`'s
  MC-COLLECTIVE-NOUNS-ARE-ALWAYS-PLURAL-VERBS — a word conceptually
  referring to multiple people genuinely does make "plural pronoun"
  feel like the logically consistent choice, extending the standard
  plural-agreement rule reasonably but incorrectly, until the singular
  grammatical status is directly confirmed via verb agreement.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when uncertain
  about a fresh indefinite-pronoun antecedent's number, spontaneously
  checks its verb agreement ("does it take is or are?") before
  selecting a pronoun is showing the verb-agreement-test discipline has
  generalized from subject-verb agreement to this new pairing.

**MC-AMBIGUOUS-PRONOUN-REFERENCE-IS-FINE-IF-CONTEXT-SEEMS-CLEAR
(Blueprint Component 1) — Type 2, perceptual intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: Type 2, not Type 1 — this is not an
  overextension of a grammatical rule but a genuine, structurally-
  guaranteed perceptual blind spot: the writer's own certainty about
  intended meaning is a real psychological state, not a reasoning
  error, and it makes the sentence GENUINELY feel unambiguous to its
  own author regardless of how carefully any rule is explained; the
  misconception survives because the writer can never directly
  experience their own sentence the way a reader without their inside
  knowledge would.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when re-reading
  their own fresh sentence, spontaneously asks "could someone who
  DOESN'T already know what I meant tell which person this refers to?"
  before being told is showing the reader's-eye-view proofreading habit
  has been genuinely internalized.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the antecedent-arrow trace itself is the analogy,
  not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically drawing
  an arrow from a pronoun back to its antecedent, then attempting the
  same for an ambiguous sentence and discovering TWO possible arrows
  could be drawn, directly embodies the ambiguity problem as a
  concrete, visually-demonstrable failure rather than an abstract
  warning. Breaking point: the arrow-trace anchor demonstrates the
  ambiguity-detection insight well but doesn't on its own address the
  indefinite-pronoun singular-status insight (TA-2), which needs its
  own separate verb-agreement-test demonstration.
- **Alternative — a phone call with a name left out**: telling a friend
  "she called me" when the friend doesn't know which of two women you
  mean forces them to ask "who?" — the same genuine information gap
  exists in an ambiguous written pronoun, just without the immediate
  chance to ask for clarification. Breaking point: useful for the
  reader's-genuine-confusion insight, but doesn't address the
  indefinite-pronoun singular-status question, which the arrow-trace
  anchor's own TA-2 verb-agreement-test practice handles directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe ambiguous pronoun reference as "a
  minor style issue" or "something only strict teachers care about"**:
  this framing directly reinforces
  MC-AMBIGUOUS-PRONOUN-REFERENCE-IS-FINE-IF-CONTEXT-SEEMS-CLEAR by
  downgrading a genuine communication failure to an arbitrary
  correctness preference; per the Blueprint's own explicit Adaptive
  Flags, ambiguity must be framed around the reader's ACTUAL inability
  to determine meaning, which motivates the proofreading habit far more
  effectively than a pedantic-correctness framing.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the antecedent-arrow trace)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any sentence about a group activity the
  learner has participated in ("everyone brought ___ own snack")
  supplies real TA-1/TA-2 material; any real story involving two
  same-gender people the learner knows supplies real TA-3/TA-4
  ambiguous-reference material.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's ambiguity-correction task
  (rewriting an ambiguous sentence using a name, restructuring, or
  clarifying detail) is inherently hands-on, requiring the learner to
  actively repair a genuine communication gap, not merely recognize a
  stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the second-arrow test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — attempting to draw
  an antecedent arrow for "Sarah told Maria that she had won the
  award" and discovering two equally plausible arrows are possible,
  making the ambiguity directly visible and testable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  pronoun-selection, ambiguity-identification, or ambiguity-correction
  attempt (TA-1 through TA-4), ask the learner to state their own
  attempt before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

reader-lacks-writer's-inside-knowledge insight are both genuine,

directly without each instance separately re-derived). The specific
terms "antecedent" and "indefinite pronoun" (the latter already
established via the prerequisite pronouns concept) are reused and
applied here, not reintroduced from scratch.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (basic singular/plural pronoun-antecedent agreement) is placed
first as the concept's most stable entry point, per the Blueprint's own
session architecture — establishing the core agreement rule with clear

base agreement rules from TA-1/TA-2 are secure. TA-4 (correcting
ambiguous pronoun reference) is placed last as the concept's
integration capstone, requiring the learner to actively REPAIR, not

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously checks a
  fresh indefinite-pronoun antecedent's verb agreement before selecting
  a pronoun, and whether the learner spontaneously re-reads their own
  fresh sentence from a reader's perspective (without their own inside
  knowledge) when checking for ambiguity — these are the primary
  diagnostic signals for whether TA-2 and TA-3's disciplines have
  genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before selecting a
  pronoun for a fresh indefinite-pronoun antecedent, followed by a
  correct, singular-consistent choice, suggests the learner is
  consciously applying the verb-agreement test rather than defaulting
  to plural — a good sign. Instant, confident use of a plural pronoun
  for "everyone" or "each" (without checking verb agreement) is the
  concerning pattern for MC-INDEFINITE-PRONOUNS-ARE-ALWAYS-PLURAL.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Protocol Routing — for S9 learners whose L1 doesn't
  require pronoun-antecedent number agreement or handles ambiguous
  reference differently (relying more heavily on context or omission),
  reassure that both indefinite-pronoun agreement and ambiguity
  detection rely on English-specific conventions and proofreading
  habits requiring dedicated, explicit practice.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "could someone who doesn't already
  know what I meant tell which person this refers to?" is this
  concept's load-bearing sentence for
  MC-AMBIGUOUS-PRONOUN-REFERENCE-IS-FINE-IF-CONTEXT-SEEMS-CLEAR, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever the learner reviews their own fresh sentence containing a
  pronoun with more than one grammatically plausible antecedent, making

  an afterthought.
