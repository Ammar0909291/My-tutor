# Long Vowels and Silent E — `eng.phonics.long-vowels-silent-e`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.phonics.long-vowels-silent-e` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / phonics / "Long Vowels and Silent
  E" — the second major long-vowel spelling pattern, sitting immediately
  downstream of digraphs' vowel-team pattern and completing the pair of
  ways English marks a long vowel in print.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.phonics.short-vowels` — specifically the
  closed-syllable structural check that concept installs at a narrow,
  bounded scope ("this concept introduces the closed-syllable/silent-e
  CONTRAST only to the extent needed to correctly bound 'short vowel'");
  load-bearing because this concept is precisely the deferred full
  treatment that entry's own Adaptive Flags names — the earlier concept
  deliberately under-teaches silent-e mechanics so this one can teach
  them properly, without duplication.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.phonics.digraphs` in reverse is not the
  direction — checking the KG confirms this concept's own downstream
  unlock is `eng.phonics.sight-words`'s sibling concepts in the phonics
  arc; more precisely, this concept completes the long-vowel spelling
  system that subsequent reading-fluency and decoding work assumes is
  already automatic.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.80 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Read minimal pairs differing only by a final silent "e" (cap/cape,
   kit/kite), correctly producing the short vowel in one and the long
   vowel in the other.
2. Predict, from spelling alone before reading, whether an unfamiliar
   CVCe word will have a long vowel sound.
3. Spell a spoken long-vowel word correctly, including the final
   silent "e," rather than spelling it as a short-vowel CVC word.
4. Recognize a small set of high-frequency silent-e exception words
   (have, give, love, come) that keep a short vowel despite ending in
   "e," by rote rather than by forcing the rule.
5. Read genuinely unseen CVCe words fluently within connected text,
   defaulting to the rule while remaining alert to known exceptions.

## Core Understanding

A final silent "e" is not phonetically pronounced, but it is not
functionally inert — it is a spelling marker that reaches backward
across an intervening consonant to change the pronunciation of the
PRECEDING vowel from short to long. This is the single most
consequential fact the concept must install, and it is genuinely
counterintuitive: English orthography has few other conventions where a
silent letter actively changes a distant letter's sound rather than
simply being unpronounced itself. Like the vowel-digraph pattern taught
immediately prior in `eng.phonics.digraphs`, the silent-e rule is a
strong, useful default with a small, listable set of exceptions (have,
give, love, come, some, done) — not an absolute law — and the correct
model is the same asymmetric one this concept's sibling pattern already
established: apply the rule as the default expectation, hold it loosely
enough to accommodate a known exception word.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "the silent e reaches back and changes the
  vowel"**: installed directly by the Component 3 magic-e wand anchor,
  which makes the distance-acting effect physically visible by adding
  an "e" to a known short-vowel word and narrating the vowel-sound
  change. Sufficient for TA-1's minimal-pair recognition but not yet a
  predictive or spelling skill.
- **Intermediate model — "I can predict a word's vowel sound from its
  spelling before reading it"**: installed by TA-2's predict-then-
  confirm routine — the learner commits to a long-vowel prediction
  based on spotting the CVCe pattern, then verifies by reading. Upgrade
  trigger: correct prediction on a genuinely unfamiliar CVCe word,
  distinguishing rule-application from memorized-word recall.
- **Advanced model — "the rule also governs how I SPELL a long-vowel
  word, not just how I read one"**: installed by TA-3, which reverses
  the direction of the skill — given a spoken word, the learner must
  supply the correct silent-e spelling rather than merely recognizing it
  in print. This is a genuinely harder, generative application of the
  same underlying rule, not a repetition of TA-1/TA-2 at higher
  difficulty.
- **Expert model — "the rule is a strong default with a small,
  memorized exception list, applied with appropriate flexibility"**:
  installed by TA-4 (explicit exception introduction, deliberately
  placed AFTER the core rule is secure) and TA-5's integration into
  connected text. This mirrors the digraph concept's own advanced model
  exactly, applying the identical epistemic stance (rule-as-default,
  exceptions-as-list) to a second spelling pattern.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own explicit Adaptive
  Flag, the exception words (TA-4) must never be introduced before
  TA-1's core CVC/CVCe contrast is independently reliable — introducing
  both together risks the learner concluding the rule itself is
  unreliable before it has even been consolidated, which is a stronger
  and more damaging version of the caution already given for
  `eng.phonics.short-vowels`'s narrower silent-e preview.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, matching the
Blueprint's two misconceptions. The silent-e-does-nothing failure
exists because "silent" is, for every OTHER letter a learner has met so
far, a reasonably accurate synonym for "has no effect" — a silent
letter in this concept's own sibling nodes (the "k" in "knife," the "w"
in "write," from `eng.phonics.consonants`) genuinely does nothing beyond
not being pronounced. A learner correctly generalizing from those prior
cases reasonably expects this silent letter to behave the same way,
making the distance-acting exception here genuinely novel rather than
an application of a rule already half-known. The rule-over-application
failure exists for the identical structural reason as the vowel-
digraph misconception one concept earlier: having just learned a
reliable, checkable spelling-to-sound rule, a learner extends the
EXPECTATION of full reliability into a small set of high-frequency
words (have, give, love) where the pattern happens to break, produced
by the same over-generalization mechanism, now appearing for the second
time in this domain.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.phonics.long-vowels-silent-e.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-SILENT-E-DOES-NOTHING (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: the learner's only prior experience with
  silent letters (from `eng.phonics.consonants`'s TA-5 exception
  sorting) correctly established "silent = not pronounced, and has no
  further effect" — a rule valid for every silent consonant met so far,
  extended here into a genuinely different case where the silent letter
  DOES have a further effect, just not a pronounced one of its own.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, on first meeting
  the concept, spontaneously asks "so it does something even though you
  can't hear it?" (rather than accepting the wand demonstration
  silently) is showing productive engagement with the genuinely
  counterintuitive nature of this pattern — a good sign, not confusion
  to correct.

**MC-ANY-E-AT-THE-END-MEANS-LONG-VOWEL (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: structurally identical in mechanism to
  the immediately-prior `eng.phonics.digraphs` concept's
  MC-VOWEL-DIGRAPHS-FOLLOW-SHORT-VOWEL-RULES — both are Type 1
  overgeneralizations of a correctly-learned spelling rule extended past
  its real (if narrow) exception boundary. This is the third time in
  the phonics domain this same overgeneralization pattern has appeared
  (vowel digraphs, now silent-e), which is itself useful evidence that
  the domain's recurring theme — reliable-pattern-with-a-small-
  exception-list — deserves explicit, direct naming to the learner
  rather than being re-discovered as a surprise each time.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who reads "have" forcing
  a long /eɪ/ sound and then pauses, seeming to sense something is off
  even without being told, is showing early self-monitoring against the
  rule — a stronger and more advanced state than a learner who reads it
  wrong with full confidence and no hesitation.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the magic-e wand itself is the analogy, not a
  supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): the physical act of
  adding an "e" and narrating its effect on the earlier vowel IS the
  concept's primary explanatory device. Breaking point: "magic" framing
  should not be extended to suggest the effect is arbitrary or
  unexplainable — it is a real, consistent spelling convention, and the
  "magic" label is a memorable hook for young learners, not a claim
  about how English spelling actually works.
- **Alternative — a silent e is a note passed backward, not spoken
  itself, but changing what the earlier letter says**: reinforces the
  distance-acting, non-pronounced-but-consequential nature of the
  letter. Breaking point: "note passed" may suggest an active, ongoing
  process during reading, when in fact the effect is a fixed spelling
  fact to be recognized, not a live event each time the word is read.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe the final e as "just decoration" or
  "just there to look nice"**: this framing directly installs
  MC-SILENT-E-DOES-NOTHING by explicitly denying the letter any
  functional role. If a learner proposes this framing themselves
  ("so it's just extra?"), correct it immediately and specifically:
  "not extra — it's doing a real job, just a silent one, changing the
  vowel before it."

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/auditory-visual (magic-e wand)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any whiteboard, paper, or even finger-tracing
  in the air can substitute for the wand demonstration; the
  minimal-pair word list requires nothing beyond speech and print.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-3's spelling task (given a
  spoken word, produce the correct silent-e spelling) is itself a
  generative, hands-on production activity.
- **Teacher-demo (exception contrast)**: the tutor models reading a
  rule-following CVCe word and a genuine exception word back to back
  (e.g., "cake" then "have"), explicitly naming which behaved as
  expected and which required an adjustment — making the reliability
  asymmetry audible, paralleling the digraph concept's own reliability-
  contrast demonstration.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  CVCe word's vowel sound (TA-2), ask the learner to predict from
  spelling alone before reading — Universal Principle 3, and this
  concept's own explicitly named "standing habit" per the Blueprint's
  Adaptive Flags.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits the core rule; direct instruction
is correct for the exception list.** The silent-e-changes-the-preceding-
vowel pattern is a genuine, statable regularity, and the Blueprint's own
TA-1/TA-2 sequence supports a discovery framing: **need** (the learner,
having just secured short-vowel spelling checks, meets a minimal pair
like "cap"/"cape" and must explain the sound difference); **playground**
(TA-1 presents several such pairs to examine); **invention** (the
learner is guided to notice the ONLY spelling difference is the final
"e," and to propose it as the cause of the vowel change); **collision**
(TA-4's exception words — "have," "give," "love" — directly break a
rule adopted too rigidly, forcing the "strong pattern, known exceptions"
revision, exactly mirroring the vowel-digraph concept's own collision
step); **formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of the rule and its
exceptions); **compression** (TA-5's fluent reading of novel CVCe words
in connected text). The exception list itself (TA-4) is correctly
taught by direct instruction — there is no regularity to discover in
WHY "have" is an exception beyond the Blueprint's own historical note
(English spelling avoiding certain word-final letters); it is simply a
short list to be memorized.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (minimal-pair contrast) must precede TA-2 (predict-before-reading)
because recognizing the pattern in already-presented pairs is a
strictly easier task than generating a prediction for an unfamiliar
word. TA-3 (spelling application) is placed after both, since spelling
requires the learner to reverse the direction of the skill — producing
the correct silent-e pattern from a spoken word, rather than merely
recognizing it in print, which depends on the reading-direction rule
already being secure. TA-4 (exception recognition) is placed
deliberately AFTER TA-1 through TA-3, never before or alongside them,
per the Blueprint's own explicit and strongly-worded Adaptive Flag —
this is the single most emphasized sequencing constraint in this
concept's Blueprint, and this entry preserves it without modification.
TA-5 (connected-text reading) is placed last as the full integration
check, requiring the core rule, the spelling application, and exception
awareness to operate together fluently. This entry does not restate the
Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see
Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner's response to a genuine
  exception word is a brief, accepting correction or a resistant
  insistence on the expected long-vowel sound — the latter is the
  stronger signal of MC-ANY-E-AT-THE-END-MEANS-LONG-VOWEL as an
  entrenched expectation rather than a one-time slip.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a deliberate pause before reading an
  unfamiliar CVCe word, followed by a correct long-vowel prediction, is
  a good sign — it suggests TA-2's predict-then-confirm habit is
  becoming standing practice, exactly as the Blueprint's own Adaptive
  Flags intend it to generalize beyond this single concept.
- **Pronunciation stakes**: real for the core rule (a short vowel where
  a long one is expected, or vice versa, changes the word's identity,
  as with any CVC/CVCe minimal pair) but should be judged structurally
  (did the learner apply the rule/exception correctly) rather than
  against a single reference accent, since some long-vowel realizations
  vary slightly by dialect.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "before you read it, look — does it
  end in a silent e?" carries over directly from
  `eng.phonics.short-vowels`'s own load-bearing sentence, now made the
  full, primary object of instruction rather than a narrow preview, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3`.

## Assessment Signals

- **Diagnostic (golden probe)**: the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence
  probe (Component 1) — reading "cap" then "cape" and asking what
  changed — cleanly separates a learner who has not yet noticed the
  silent-e effect from one who has, before deciding how much time TA-1
  needs.
- **Guided practice → independent practice**: the Blueprint's WE-1 → WE-2
  → WE-3 → MP-1 through MP-5 fading ladder is reused directly
  (Components 5–6) — not re-authored here.
- **Mastery gate set** (`../../assessment/05-mastery-verification.md
  §3`): production (fresh minimal-pair reading, MP-1); prediction (a
  novel CVCe word predicted before reading, MP-2 — the strongest
  evidence the RULE, not memorized pairs, is what generalizes);
  generative-spelling (a spoken word spelled correctly with silent e,
  MP-3); exception-handling (a genuine exception word correctly
  identified as such rather than forced through the rule, MP-4 — this
  concept's single most diagnostic item, mirroring the digraph
  concept's own exception-item emphasis); explanation (MP-5).
- **Interpretation of response pattern**: fast-and-correct prediction on
  a fresh CVCe word confirms the rule is internalized as a genuine
  predictive tool, not merely a post-hoc recognition skill. Fast-and-
  WRONG on a known exception word (forcing the long vowel without
  hesitation) is the dangerous quadrant here, identical in structure to
  the vowel-digraph concept's own diagnostic pattern — it signals the
  rule has displaced flexible exception-checking entirely, and warrants
  returning to TA-4's exception list rather than more rule-practice.
- **Transfer items**: near (a fresh CVCe minimal pair); far (a CVCe
  word using a vowel not heavily rehearsed during teaching, e.g. "u" as
  in "cute," testing whether the rule generalizes beyond the most-
  practiced vowels); real-world (spotting a silent-e word, or a known
  exception, in text the learner is already reading).

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a CVCe reading task shrinks to the Component 3
  magic-e wand anchor using the most familiar, most-rehearsed pair
  (typically "cap"/"cape," the first taught), performed with the tutor
  modeling the wand gesture first, rather than asking the learner to
  attempt a fresh word cold.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: a confident but
  incorrect short-vowel reading of a CVCe word (ignoring the final e
  entirely) is more likely than "I don't know," per
  MC-SILENT-E-DOES-NOTHING's mechanism — route directly to that
  misconception's recovery (re-run the wand demonstration on this exact
  word, slowly) rather than treating it as a generic decoding error.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who visibly
  looks at the end of a word before predicting its vowel sound is
  showing the correct rule-checking behavior even on items where the
  final answer is uncertain — this checking behavior should be praised
  explicitly, per this concept's own emphasis on the predict-before-
  reading habit.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: mixed — the core silent-e rule is a procedure (a
checking habit applied to novel words, reviewed through continued
application rather than simple recall), while the small exception-word
list (have, give, love, come, some, done) is a fact set, reviewed via
standard spaced fact-review since these are a fixed, closed inventory
rather than a generalizable pattern. Interleaving partners: mix
rule-following CVCe words with exception words within the same practice
set, exactly paralleling the digraph concept's own interleaving
rationale, so the learner cannot predict "rule" or "exception" from
position in the set alone.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: reading and spelling fresh, unpracticed CVCe words.
- **Far**: this concept, together with `eng.phonics.digraphs`'s vowel-
  team pattern, completes the two major long-vowel spelling
  conventions a fluent reader must hold simultaneously — later
  spelling instruction (multisyllabic words, suffixing rules that
  interact with silent e, e.g. "hope" → "hoping") builds directly on
  this concept's core rule.
- **Real-world**: any CVCe word or known exception encountered in the
  learner's own reading is immediate, ungated practice.
- **Expert-transfer**: fluent, automatic long/short vowel discrimination
  during silent reading and spelling, requiring no conscious rule-
  checking, well past this node.
