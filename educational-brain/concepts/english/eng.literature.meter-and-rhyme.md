## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Analyze meter and rhyme scheme as two independent poetic dimensions, recognizing that rhyme does not imply regular meter (or vice versa).
2. Scan a line's meter by identifying its natural stressed/unstressed syllable pattern.
3. Recognize that minor metrical substitutions are a normal, accepted variation within an overall consistent pattern, not proof the poem has no meter.
4. Analyze a new poem, labeling rhyme scheme, scanning dominant meter separately, and correctly identifying any substitutions.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **identifying a poem's rhyme scheme is not the same task as identifying its meter, and a poem with a clear rhyme scheme does not necessarily have a regular meter (or vice versa)** — rhyme (end-sound repetition across lines) and meter (stress pattern within lines) are independent dimensions that can vary separately; a poem can have one without the other, both, or neither. Second, **a single line or syllable deviating from a poem's otherwise dominant stress pattern does not mean the poem "has no meter"** — poets commonly use accepted metrical substitutions and variations within an overall consistent pattern, and meter describes the DOMINANT, overall pattern across most of a poem, not an unbroken, rigid rule with zero exceptions.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "if a poem rhymes it must have a regular meter too, and any line that breaks the pattern means the poem has no meter at all."**
The simulation: assume a consistently rhyming poem must also have consistent rhythm, and declare a poem "has no meter" upon finding one varying line.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a consistent AABB-rhyming poem with irregular line rhythm compared against one with both regular rhyme and regular meter, and a poem with a single deliberate stress-reversal within an otherwise consistent pattern.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A song's chorus melody doesn't require a steady beat throughout every verse — rhyme and meter are separate. And one syncopated measure doesn't mean the song isn't in 4/4 time."

**Intermediate — "I can now assess rhyme and meter separately, but I still get thrown off by the first line that varies from the dominant pattern."**
The simulation: correctly separates rhyme and meter analysis but still hesitates or declares "no meter" too quickly when a single substitution appears.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-4's direct accepted-substitution recognition practice, given a poem with one deliberate variation within an otherwise consistent pattern.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Find the dominant pattern across MOST of the poem first — then treat any single variation as a likely deliberate substitution, not proof there's no pattern."

**Advanced — "I label a poem's rhyme scheme, scan its dominant meter separately, and correctly identify any substitutions, all in a new, unscaffolded poem."**
The simulation: integrates independent rhyme-analysis, meter-scanning, and substitution-recognition into one coherent poem analysis. Matches TA-4/WE-3.
*Upgrade trigger*: none within the current curriculum sequence — this concept is confirmed terminal.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the full toolkit for analyzing sound patterns in any poem you read."

**Expert — "at fluency, separating rhyme from meter and recognizing accepted substitutions happens as an integrated part of ordinary poetic reading, without a separate deliberate checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit label-scan-explain routine is a scaffold for a reading habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of literary comprehension.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **rhyme and meter are both introduced as "sound patterns in poetry" under one broad heading**, priming an assumption that they're really one combined phenomenon rather than two genuinely separate systems that happen to often co-occur in familiar forms.

The second mechanism is that **early metrical instruction typically uses simplified, perfectly regular example lines**, so a learner's mental model of "meter" becomes "every single syllable must fit the pattern exactly," leaving no conceptual room for the accepted substitutions real poems actually contain.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-A-POEM-THAT-RHYMES-MUST-ALSO-HAVE-A-REGULAR-CONSISTENT-METER
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of rhyme and meter being introduced together under one "sound pattern" heading, extended into treating them as one combined phenomenon rather than two independent systems.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **assess rhyme and meter as two explicitly separate questions on the same poem**, since their independence is far more convincing demonstrated through direct side-by-side analysis than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given a poem with clear rhyme but irregular meter, the learner confirms the rhyme scheme and separately assesses whether the meter is regular.

### MC-B-ANY-DEVIATION-FROM-THE-DOMINANT-STRESS-PATTERN-MEANS-THE-POEM-HAS-NO-METER
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from early metrical instruction using perfectly regular examples, leaving no conceptual room for accepted substitutions in real poems.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **identifying the dominant pattern across the majority of lines FIRST**, since evaluating a single line in isolation, before establishing the dominant pattern, is what causes the over-rigid rejection.
- **Verification of death**: given a poem with one substituted line within an otherwise consistent pattern, the learner identifies the dominant pattern and explains the substitution's likely purpose.

## Analogies

**Best — the Song-Chorus-vs-Steady-Beat and Syncopated-Measure-in-4/4-Time Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a song with a consistent chorus melody that doesn't require a steady, unchanging beat throughout — directly seeding the fix for MC-A. A song still counted as 4/4 time despite one measure containing a brief syncopated variation — directly seeding the fix for MC-B.
*Breaking point*: musical time signatures are formally notated and unambiguous in a way a poem's "dominant pattern" sometimes isn't — poetic meter can require more genuinely debatable interpretive judgment about where the dominant pattern lies than a clearly notated musical measure does.

**Alternative — a recurring visual motif versus a strict geometric grid in a painting.** A painting can repeat a specific color motif (like rhyme) without following a strict, regular grid structure (like meter) — the two visual organizing principles are independent, and a grid with one deliberately shifted element is still recognizably a grid.
*Breaking point*: visual composition doesn't have the same kind of aural, sequential "reading aloud" experience that makes meter's rhythmic effect immediately perceptible, a difference the visual analogy doesn't fully capture.

**Visual anchor — the Song-Chorus-vs-Steady-Beat and Syncopated-Measure-in-4/4-Time Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"If a poem rhymes, it has a regular rhythm too."** Directly installs the rhyme-implies-meter misconception as a stated definition.
- **"One irregular line means the whole poem has no meter."** Directly installs the any-deviation-means-no-meter misconception as a stated definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Song-Chorus-vs-Steady-Beat and Syncopated-Measure Anchor (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Does a song's consistent chorus melody require a steady, unchanging beat throughout every verse?"
2. **The rhyme-meter-independence collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Do both of these consistently-rhyming poems have the same kind of rhythm when read aloud?"
3. **The accepted-substitution collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Does one line with a variation mean the whole poem has no meter?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by scanning real lines for stress pattern and separately checking rhyme scheme.

1. **Need**: "If two poems have the exact same rhyme scheme, do they have to sound the same rhythmically when read aloud?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely labels a poem's rhyme scheme before being told the official rhyme-meter-independence test.
3. **Invention**: "Can you find a line in a metered poem that breaks the pattern slightly — does that one line ruin the whole pattern?"
4. **Collision**: present a poem with one deliberate stress-reversal within an otherwise consistent pattern, and ask whether the poem "has no meter."
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — rhyme and meter are independent, and a single substitution doesn't erase a dominant meter — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Assess rhyme and meter separately; find the dominant pattern before judging one variation."

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for treating rhyme and meter as separate questions, and for identifying the dominant pattern before evaluating a single variation**, since a correct-looking rhyme label with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated separate-questions check** ("what's the rhyme scheme, and separately, what's the dominant stress pattern?") applied to a new poem — the direct behavioral signature of the independence fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary identification of the dominant pattern before evaluating a single varying line** as a substitution rather than a disproof — direct evidence the accepted-substitution fix is internalized.
- **Assuming a rhyming poem must be regularly metered, or declaring "no meter" upon finding one deviation** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A or MC-B still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Assess rhyme and meter separately — and find the dominant pattern before judging one variation."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But it rhymes so consistently, shouldn't the rhythm be consistent too?"** — take this as an accurate observation about the rhyme's regularity, not a mistake; respond by adding the separate test: "The rhyme is consistent, that's true — but read the lines aloud. Does the STRESS pattern feel consistent too, or are they two separate things here?"
- **"This one line breaks the pattern, so the poem doesn't really have a meter"** — a genuine, reasonable conclusion from a single data point; respond by naming the additional layer: "That line does vary, yes — but what's the pattern across most of the OTHER lines? Could this one line be a deliberate substitution within that pattern?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full meter-and-rhyme analysis down to **labeling one single poem's rhyme scheme, with meter-scanning and substitution-recognition set aside.** Isolates the most basic rhyme-labeling skill before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just check if it rhymes and call that the meter too."** This directly licenses the rhyme-implies-meter pattern, undermining the independence discipline this concept specifically builds.
