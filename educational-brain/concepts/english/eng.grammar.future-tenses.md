# Future Tenses — `eng.grammar.future-tenses`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.future-tenses` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Future Tenses" — the
  various ways English expresses future time, including "will," "going
  to," and present-tense forms used for scheduled events and confirmed
  plans.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.present-tenses` — load-bearing because
  two of this concept's four future-expression strategies (present
  continuous for confirmed plans, simple present for schedules) directly
  reuse present-tense FORMS repurposed for future MEANING; a learner
  without secure present-tense forms cannot correctly produce these two
  future strategies at all.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.tense-consistency`. Maintaining a
  single, correctly-chosen tense across a passage depends on having
  secure, distinct future-tense forms to be consistent with, in parallel
  with the present- and past-tense systems already covered.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 3

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Produce "going to" future forms for prior plans and predictions based
   on visible present evidence.
2. Produce "will" future forms for spontaneous decisions, promises, and
   general opinions/predictions about the future.
3. Discriminate between "will" and "going to" based on whether the
   future action is a prior plan/evidence-based prediction or a
   spontaneous decision/promise/general opinion.
4. Produce present continuous forms with future meaning for confirmed
   personal plans and arrangements.
5. Produce simple present forms with future meaning for scheduled or
   timetabled events, and produce future continuous and future perfect
   forms for in-progress and completed-before-a-future-point actions.

## Core Understanding

English does not have a single, dedicated "future tense" the way it has
distinct simple-past or simple-present verb forms — instead, future
meaning is expressed through (at least) four different strategies, each
signaling a different relationship to the future action. "Going to"
signals a PRIOR PLAN already decided, or a PREDICTION based on visible
present evidence ("Look at those clouds — it's going to rain"). "Will"
signals a SPONTANEOUS decision made at the moment of speaking, a
promise, or a general prediction/opinion without necessarily citing
present evidence ("I think it will rain tomorrow"; "I'll help you with
that"). Present continuous, normally used for actions in progress right
now, is repurposed for CONFIRMED personal plans and arrangements ("I'm
meeting her tomorrow" — already arranged). Simple present, normally used
for habits and general truths, is repurposed for SCHEDULED or
TIMETABLED events ("The train leaves at 6pm tonight"). The load-bearing
insight is that the SAME grammatical form (present continuous, simple
present) can refer to either present or future time depending entirely
on context — nothing in the verb form itself signals which.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "will" and "going to" mean exactly the same thing
  in every context, and only "will"/"going to" can express future
  time"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to
  correct — a natural default because both forms genuinely DO overlap
  heavily in casual speech, and because "will"/"going to" are the most
  visibly future-marked words a learner first meets, giving no signal
  that present-tense forms can also carry future meaning.
- **Intermediate model — "I distinguish 'going to' (plans/evidence) from
  'will' (spontaneous decisions/promises/general predictions)"**:
  installed by TA-1 and TA-2, using the Component 3 weather-report
  scenario-matching anchor, resolving
  MC-WILL-AND-GOING-TO-ARE-FULLY-INTERCHANGEABLE. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly choosing between the two forms for a fresh scenario based on
  the plan/evidence-vs-spontaneous distinction, not by guessing.
- **Advanced model — "I recognize present continuous and simple present
  can both express future time"**: installed by TA-3 and TA-4, resolving
  MC-PRESENT-TENSE-CANNOT-EXPRESS-FUTURE-TIME by demonstrating the same
  grammatical form carrying different time meanings depending on
  context.
- **Expert model — "I produce future continuous for in-progress future
  actions and future perfect for actions completed before a future
  point"**: installed by TA-5, the concept's genuine mastery bar —
  completing the full future-expression system, including the two forms
  that most closely parallel the present- and past-tense systems'
  continuous and perfect aspects.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 uses a single future marker regardless of nuance,
  or lacks a present-tense-for-future pattern entirely, expect the full
  four-way system — and especially the present-tense-for-future patterns
  in TA-3/TA-4, which are genuinely distinctive to English and a limited
  set of related languages — to require substantial, dedicated, ongoing
  practice.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here. The will/going-to-are-
interchangeable failure exists because both forms genuinely DO overlap
heavily in casual, everyday speech — a learner reasonably concludes they
are simply free variants until directly probed with a scenario where the
plan/evidence-vs-spontaneous distinction produces a genuinely different,
more natural choice (a sudden decision made mid-conversation strongly
favors "will," not "going to"). The present-tense-cannot-express-future
failure exists because "will" and "going to" are the most visibly
future-marked forms a learner meets first, creating a reasonable
assumption that future time REQUIRES an explicitly future-looking word
— nothing marks the present-tense-for-future pattern (a genuinely
distinctive feature of English, not shared by every language) until a
learner is directly shown a sentence like "the train leaves at 6pm
tonight" and asked whether "leaves," despite its present-tense form, is
actually describing something that hasn't happened yet.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the four future-forms weather report itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically
  matching four scenario cards (an already-arranged trip, visible storm
  clouds, a spontaneous offer, a train timetable) to their correct
  future-expression form directly embodies the four-way system as a
  set of situational fits rather than an arbitrary list of forms.
  Breaking point: the four-card matching demonstrates the core will/
  going-to/continuous/simple-present distinctions well but does not on
  its own extend to future continuous or future perfect (TA-5), which
  require their own separate demonstration once the base four-way system
  is secure.
- **Alternative — a diary entry vs. a weather forecaster's guess**: a
  diary entry ("Saturday: dentist, 3pm") reflects a confirmed plan
  already written down (present continuous/going to), while a
  forecaster's spoken guess without a chart in hand ("I think it'll rain
  later") reflects a general opinion without cited present evidence
  (will). Breaking point: useful for the plan-vs-opinion contrast, but
  doesn't address the present-tense-for-future pattern, which needs its
  own separate demonstration (TA-3/TA-4).
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe "will" as the "default" or "basic"
  future form with the other three as optional extras**: this framing
  risks reinforcing both misconceptions by implying "will" is the
  correct default choice whenever future time is meant, understating
  that "going to," present continuous, and simple present are each the
  MORE natural, idiomatic choice in their own specific contexts (plans,
  confirmed arrangements, schedules respectively), not lesser
  substitutes for "will."

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the four future-forms weather report)**: the
  Blueprint's Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any genuine upcoming personal plan supplies
  real TA-1/TA-3 material (a decided-on weekend activity); any real
  class or transport schedule supplies real TA-4 material; any moment
  where the learner is asked to react spontaneously to an unplanned
  request supplies real TA-2 material.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-1's evidence-based-prediction
  practice (matching visible present evidence to a "going to" prediction)
  is inherently hands-on, requiring the learner to notice and interpret
  a concrete cue, not merely recognize a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the leaves test)**: the tutor models the Blueprint's
  own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether "leaves" in "the
  train leaves at 6pm tonight" is grammatically present tense, then
  asking whether the sentence is actually describing something that
  hasn't happened yet, making the present-tense-for-future pattern
  directly audible and testable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  future-form identification or production attempt (TA-1 through TA-5),
  ask the learner to state their own attempt before the tutor confirms —
  Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The will/
going-to distinction and the present-tense-for-future pattern are both
genuine, directly testable phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured TA-1's "going to" practice, is
asked what they would spontaneously say if a phone rang unexpectedly
mid-conversation, discovering "going to" feels awkward there even before
being told why); **playground/invention** (TA-1's plan-based practice
and TA-2's spontaneous-decision practice build initial confidence with
both forms independently before their contrast is named); **collision**
(the Blueprint's own spontaneous-help-offer probe breaks the fully-
interchangeable assumption; the "the train leaves at 6pm" probe breaks
the present-cannot-express-future assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's
own-words statement of both the will/going-to nuance and the present-
tense-for-future pattern); **compression** (TA-5's fluent production of
future continuous and future perfect, treating them as natural
extensions of the now-secure continuous and perfect aspects from the
present- and past-tense systems, rather than two unrelated new forms to
separately memorize). The specific terms "going to," "will," "future
continuous," and "future perfect" are convention-bound grammatical
terminology best introduced by direct instruction once the learner has
already felt the underlying distinctions through the weather-report
anchor and TA-1–4.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 ("going to" for plans and evidence-based predictions) is placed
first as the concept's most visually concrete entry point, per the
Blueprint's own weather-report anchor — establishing the plan/evidence-
based form before any contrast is introduced. TA-2 ("will" for
spontaneous decisions, promises, and general predictions) follows
immediately, directly targeting
MC-WILL-AND-GOING-TO-ARE-FULLY-INTERCHANGEABLE through explicit contrast
with TA-1 — per the Blueprint's own session architecture, this conflict-
evidence probe is placed right after TA-2, once the learner has secure
practice with both forms to contrast. TA-3 (present continuous for
confirmed future plans) follows, directly targeting
MC-PRESENT-TENSE-CANNOT-EXPRESS-FUTURE-TIME, addressing a SECOND,
independent dimension (form-repurposing, not will-vs-going-to nuance)
once the base will/going-to distinction is secure. TA-4 (simple present
for scheduled/timetabled events) extends the present-tense-for-future
pattern to its second strategy, completing the four-way base system.
TA-5 (future continuous and future perfect) is placed last as the
concept's integration capstone, extending the continuous and perfect
aspects the learner already secured from the present- and past-tense
systems into future time, converging all three tense systems'
structural parallels. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own
turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References
below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously reaches for
  "will," not "going to," when reacting to a genuinely unplanned,
  in-the-moment request, and whether the learner spontaneously uses
  present continuous or simple present for a confirmed plan or schedule
  rather than defaulting to "will"/"going to" — these are the primary
  diagnostic signals for whether TA-2 and TA-3/TA-4's disciplines have
  genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before choosing between
  "will" and "going to" for an ambiguous scenario, followed by a
  correct, nuance-consistent choice, suggests the learner is consciously
  applying the plan/evidence-vs-spontaneous test rather than guessing —
  a good sign. Instant, confident "going to" for a clearly spontaneous
  decision (without self-correction) is the concerning pattern for
  MC-WILL-AND-GOING-TO-ARE-FULLY-INTERCHANGEABLE.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Protocol Routing — for S9 learners whose L1 uses a
  single future marker regardless of nuance, reassure that the full
  four-way system, especially the present-tense-for-future patterns, is
  a genuinely complex, multi-form area requiring sustained practice, not
  a sign of weak general English ability.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "was this already planned, or did you
  just decide it this second?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence
  for MC-WILL-AND-GOING-TO-ARE-FULLY-INTERCHANGEABLE, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh will-vs-going-to scenario is introduced, making the
  plan/evidence-vs-spontaneous check a routinely-invited response rather
  than an afterthought.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedural/framework — a four-strategy future-
expression system (going-to/will/present-continuous-for-future/simple-
present-for-future) plus two extension forms (future continuous, future
perfect), applied to genuinely novel scenarios; mastery is demonstrated
by correctly selecting the appropriate strategy for a fresh, context-
dependent scenario (MP-1 through MP-4) rather than by recognizing a
fixed, previously-taught example list. Interleaving partners: mix all
four base strategies together in ongoing review scenario sets, so the
learner continues exercising the situational-fit judgment rather than
practicing each form in isolation.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: correctly choosing among "going to," "will," present
  continuous, and simple present for fresh future-time scenarios;
  producing fresh future continuous and future perfect sentences.
- **Far**: this concept's future-expression framework, alongside the
  present- and past-tense systems, is the direct prerequisite for
  `eng.grammar.tense-consistency`'s requirement to maintain a single,
  correctly-chosen tense throughout a passage — a skill that
  presupposes each individual tense system, including this one, is
  already secure.
- **Real-world**: every plan, promise, prediction, or scheduled event
  the learner describes — in speech or writing — offers immediate,
  ungated opportunities to apply the correct future-expression strategy.
- **Expert-transfer**: fluent, natural-sounding discussion of future
  plans and predictions at any advanced level depends on selecting the
  contextually appropriate future form without conscious deliberation,
  since defaulting to "will" for every future reference (ignoring the
  more natural "going to," present continuous, or simple present
  alternatives) remains a noticeable marker of non-native production
  even at otherwise advanced proficiency.
