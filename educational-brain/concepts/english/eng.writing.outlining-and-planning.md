## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Use whatever outlining format (bullet list, cluster diagram, formal Roman-numeral outline) actually organizes their ideas, rather than requiring a rigid formal template.
2. Sort a jumbled set of ideas into a main-point/sub-point hierarchy.
3. Revise an outline mid-draft when drafting reveals a better order or a new necessary point.
4. Create and use an original outline for a new writing topic, in a format of their choice.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **an outline does not have to follow the formal Roman-numeral/letter structure to count as real planning** — a bullet list, a cluster/web diagram, or a simple numbered list all accomplish outlining's real purpose (organizing ideas logically before drafting) just as well as a formal outline, as long as genuine logical organization is present; requiring the formal format specifically either discourages planning altogether or produces a rigid outline that doesn't actually help the writer's thinking. Second, **an outline is not a fixed, unchangeable contract once created** — it's completely normal, even a sign of good thinking, to revise an outline once drafting reveals a better structure, a logical gap, or a new idea; the plan should serve the writing, not force the writing into an outdated structure.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "an outline must be a formal Roman-numeral list, and once you've made one you have to stick to it exactly."**
The simulation: avoid outlining because the formal format feels burdensome, or force a draft to match an outline's original order even when drafting reveals a better structure.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a formal Roman-numeral outline compared against a simple bullet list capturing the identical logical organization, and a GPS route that reroutes around a road closure rather than forcing the driver down a closed road.
*Shelf-life warning*: "The formal format is just one tool, not a requirement. The GPS route reroutes when it hits a closure — so should your outline."

**Intermediate — "I can now use different outline formats and revise mid-draft, but I still hesitate to make big structural changes once I've committed to a plan."**
The simulation: accepts minor outline tweaks but resists more substantial reordering even when drafting clearly reveals it's needed.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct mid-draft revision practice, given a scenario where a genuinely better order emerges during drafting.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A GPS doesn't hesitate to reroute around a real closure — if drafting reveals a real problem with the plan, update the plan."

**Advanced — "I create and use an original outline in a format of my choice, revising it flexibly as drafting reveals improvements."**
The simulation: integrates flexible format choice, hierarchical organization, and willingness to revise into one coherent planning practice. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: `eng.writing.drafting`'s extension of this same flexible-plan-in-service-of-good-writing principle to the act of drafting itself.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you flexible outlining. Drafting itself extends this same flexible-plan principle next."

**Expert — "at fluency, outlining and revising the plan happen as an integrated part of ordinary writing, without a separate deliberate format check."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit multi-format-and-revision routine is a scaffold for a planning habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of composition.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **outlining is frequently taught and graded via one specific formal template** (the Roman-numeral/letter format), reinforcing an assumption that this ONE format is required rather than one option among several equally valid planning tools.

The second mechanism is that **an outline is presented as a completed assignment artifact submitted before drafting begins**, subtly implying it's a finished, fixed product rather than a living, revisable planning tool meant to evolve alongside the writer's developing understanding.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-AN-OUTLINE-MUST-BE-A-RIGID-FORMAL-ROMAN-NUMERAL-LIST
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from outlining typically being taught and assessed via one specific formal template, reinforcing that format as a requirement rather than one option among several.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **show multiple formats achieving the identical logical organization side by side**, since the format-independence is far more convincing demonstrated than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given the same content in two different outline formats, the learner confirms both achieve genuine logical organization.

### MC-ONCE-AN-OUTLINE-IS-MADE-IT-CANT-BE-CHANGED-DURING-DRAFTING
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from an outline being submitted as a completed artifact before drafting, implicitly framing it as fixed rather than a living planning tool.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **a concrete scenario where drafting reveals a genuinely better order**, since the flexible-revision norm is far more convincing demonstrated through a real improvement than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given a scenario where drafting reveals a better structure than the original outline, the learner revises the outline accordingly.

## Analogies

**Best — the GPS Route, Not a Locked Track** (Blueprint Component 3): a GPS that reroutes around a road closure rather than forcing the driver down a closed road — directly seeding the fix for MC-ONCE-AN-OUTLINE-IS-MADE-IT-CANT-BE-CHANGED-DURING-DRAFTING. Three different valid route-planning tools (a list of turns, a visual map, a formal itinerary) all legitimately planning the same trip — directly seeding the fix for MC-AN-OUTLINE-MUST-BE-A-RIGID-FORMAL-ROMAN-NUMERAL-LIST.
*Breaking point*: a GPS reroute is typically triggered by an external, unambiguous obstacle (a closure), while a writer's decision to revise an outline is a more subjective judgment call about whether a newly-discovered structure is genuinely better — a less clear-cut trigger than a physical road closure.

**Alternative — a recipe versus a chef's mise en place notes.** A formal recipe and a chef's own shorthand ingredient list both organize the same cooking plan; and any experienced cook adjusts the plan mid-cooking if something isn't working as expected.
*Breaking point*: a recipe is typically meant to be followed exactly for reliable results, while an outline is explicitly meant to be a flexible starting point — the recipe analogy could reinforce rigidity if pushed too far.

**Visual anchor — the GPS Route, Not a Locked Track**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"An outline has to use Roman numerals to count."** Directly installs the rigid-format misconception as a stated definition.
- **"Once you've made your outline, you have to follow it exactly."** Directly installs the fixed-contract misconception as a stated definition.

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by comparing multiple planning formats and by testing a real mid-draft revision scenario.

1. **Need**: "If a Roman-numeral outline feels burdensome, does that mean you shouldn't plan at all, or that you could plan a different way?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely organizes a set of ideas into a bullet list before being told the official multi-format-equivalence test.
3. **Invention**: "Once you start drafting, could you discover something that makes you want to change your plan?"
4. **Collision**: present a mid-draft scenario where a better order emerges, and ask whether the original outline should still be followed exactly.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — any format that genuinely organizes ideas counts as outlining, and outlines should be revised when drafting reveals improvements — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Any format that organizes your ideas works; revise the plan when drafting reveals something better."

- **Multi-format outlining practice (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the first misconception before hierarchical organization is practiced.
- **Identifying main points and sub-points (TA-2) comes second**, building the hierarchical organization skill outlining requires, independent of format.
- **Revising an outline mid-draft (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting the second misconception once the hierarchical-organization skill is established.
- **Choosing the right format for the task (TA-4) comes fourth**, extending format-flexibility into task-appropriate judgment.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reason for choosing an outline format, and for revising or not revising mid-draft**, since a correct-looking outline with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from rote compliance.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **Voluntary selection of a non-formal outline format** (bullet list, cluster diagram) with a stated organizational reason — the direct behavioral signature of the format-flexibility fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary revision of an outline mid-draft** when a better structure emerges, without treating this as a failure of the original plan — direct evidence the flexible-planning fix is internalized.
- **Forcing a draft to match an outdated outline order despite drafting revealing a clear improvement** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-ONCE-AN-OUTLINE-IS-MADE-IT-CANT-BE-CHANGED-DURING-DRAFTING still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Any format that organizes your ideas works — and reroute your plan when drafting reveals something better."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But my teacher said outlines have to use Roman numerals"** — take this as an accurate report of a specific instruction, not a mistake; respond by adding the broader test: "That's one valid format, that's true — but does a bullet list or a cluster diagram also organize your ideas logically? If so, it's also outlining."
- **"I already made my outline, I can't change it now"** — a genuine, reasonable assumption given how outlines are often submitted as fixed artifacts; respond by naming the additional layer: "You made a plan, yes — but if drafting just showed you a better order, updating the plan is good thinking, not a mistake."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full original outline creation down to **sorting one single jumbled list of 4 ideas into main points and sub-points, with format choice and revision set aside.** Isolates the core hierarchical-organization skill before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just write the Roman-numeral outline the way you were shown."** This directly licenses the rigid-format pattern, undermining the format-flexibility discipline this concept specifically builds.
