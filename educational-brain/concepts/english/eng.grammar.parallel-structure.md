# Parallel Structure — `eng.grammar.parallel-structure`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.parallel-structure` (canonical English
  KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Parallel Structure" —
  using matching grammatical forms for items joined in a series or
  comparison, avoiding mismatched structures.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.compound-sentences` — load-bearing
  because parallel structure is a related but distinct extension of
  the joining skill that concept established: where compound sentences
  join independent clauses with coordinating conjunctions, parallel
  structure governs how items WITHIN a series or comparison (often
  connected by the same coordinating conjunctions) must share
  grammatical form for the sentence to read as balanced.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none listed — this is a terminal node in the
  current KG's grammar domain. **Cross-links** (from KG):
  `eng.composition.style-voice-and-tone` — parallel structure is one of
  the specific grammatical patterns contributing to a writer's
  recognizable stylistic signature; this entry notes the connection
  without restating that concept's own content.
- **Difficulty**: advanced · **Bloom**: analyze · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify a parallel structure violation in a sentence containing a
   simple word-list series with mismatched grammatical forms.
2. Recognize that the same matching principle extends to series of
   phrases (gerund phrases, infinitive phrases) and full clauses, not
   just single words.
3. Distinguish a genuine, precise grammatical-form fix from a
   superficial "sounds better" adjustment that leaves the underlying
   mismatch intact.
4. Revise a non-parallel sentence by matching all items in the series
   or comparison to one exact, identified grammatical form.
5. Identify and correct parallel structure violations at any level of
   complexity — word, phrase, or clause — in a full synthesis task, and
   explain why parallel structure matters for readability and balance.

## Core Understanding

Parallel structure requires that items joined together in a SERIES
(three or more items) or a COMPARISON share the same grammatical form —
this creates a sentence that reads as balanced and clear rather than
lopsided. The principle is easiest to see in simple word lists ("I like
apples, oranges, and bananas" — all nouns), but this is the concept's
first, most load-bearing insight: the SAME principle applies at ANY
level of complexity, not just single words. A series of PHRASES needs
matching phrase forms ("She likes swimming, running, and biking" — all
gerunds; "She likes swimming, to run, and biking" mismatches a gerund,
an infinitive, and another gerund), and a series of full CLAUSES needs
the same discipline. A second, independent, equally load-bearing insight
concerns HOW a violation gets fixed: genuinely correcting a parallel-
structure error means identifying the EXACT grammatical form shared by
the matching items and revising the mismatched item(s) to that PRECISE
form (same part of speech, same phrase type, same verb form) — not
merely adjusting wording until the sentence "sounds a bit better."
Adding filler words or superficially smoothing a mismatched item without
checking its actual grammatical form leaves the underlying structural
problem unsolved, even if the sentence reads slightly less jarring.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "parallel structure is a rule for simple word
  lists, and fixing a mismatch just means making the items sound more
  similar"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists
  to correct — a natural default because single-word list examples
  (apples, oranges, bananas) are the most visually obvious, first-taught
  case, and because informal "smoothing" a sentence until it reads
  better is a reasonable, intuitive editing instinct absent explicit
  instruction on checking grammatical form precisely.
- **Intermediate model — "I identify parallel structure violations and
  matches in simple word lists"**: installed by TA-1, establishing the
  base principle with the clearest, most concrete case before any
  complication is introduced.
- **Advanced model — "I recognize that the same matching principle
  extends to gerund phrases, infinitive phrases, and full clauses in a
  series, not just single words"**: installed by TA-2, using the
  Component 3 matched-place-settings anchor, resolving
  MC-A-PARALLEL-STRUCTURE-ONLY-APPLIES-TO-SIMPLE-LISTS-OF-SINGLE-WORDS.
  Upgrade trigger: correctly identifying a fresh phrase-level or
  clause-level mismatch without hesitation, having already secured
  simple word-list detection.
- **Expert model — "I identify the exact matching grammatical form and
  revise mismatched items precisely to it, applying this at any level
  of complexity in a full synthesis task"**: installed by TA-3 and
  TA-4, the concept's genuine mastery bar, resolving
  MC-B-FIXING-A-PARALLEL-STRUCTURE-ERROR-JUST-MEANS-MAKING-THE-ITEMS-
  SOUND-SIMILAR by requiring precise, form-matched correction rather
  than superficial polishing.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing
  (S1), MC-A generally needs resolving BEFORE MC-B, since a learner who
  cannot even detect phrase-level violations has nothing yet to
  practice fixing precisely — the sequence matters, not just coverage
  of both misconceptions.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and the Blueprint
itself explicitly notes that resolving the first is a prerequisite for
meaningfully practicing the second. The simple-lists-only failure exists
because single-word list examples ARE the most visually obvious, first-
taught demonstration of the principle — a learner reasonably narrows
the rule to that concrete case, with nothing marking the extension to
phrases and clauses until a learner is directly shown a phrase-level
mismatch (swimming/to run/biking) and asked whether the identical
matching-forms principle applies. The sounds-similar-is-enough failure
exists because informally "smoothing" a sentence until it reads better
is a genuinely reasonable, intuitive editing habit — nothing marks the
gap between surface adjustment and true grammatical-form matching until
a learner directly compares a superficially-adjusted "fix" (still
structurally mismatched) against a precisely form-matched correction and
is asked which one actually achieves the same grammatical form
throughout.

- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — the simplest,
  first-taught single-word-list case genuinely IS a correct instance
  of the principle, so narrowing the rule's scope to that case is a
  reasonable, if incomplete, generalization from real, correctly-
  understood evidence; the misconception survives because word-list
  violations are the most visually salient and frequently drilled,
  delaying exposure to the equally-real phrase- and clause-level cases.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a fresh
  series of gerund/infinitive phrases, spontaneously labels each item's
  grammatical form before checking for a match (rather than only
  scanning for single-word mismatches) is showing the principle has
  generalized beyond the specific TA-1 practice items to the harder
  TA-2 case.

**MC-B-FIXING-A-PARALLEL-STRUCTURE-ERROR-JUST-MEANS-MAKING-THE-ITEMS-
SOUND-SIMILAR (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — informally
  polishing a sentence until it "reads better" is a genuinely
  reasonable, common editing instinct that often does improve prose in
  OTHER respects, so applying it here is a reasonable extension of a
  real, generally-useful editing habit; the misconception survives
  because a superficially-adjusted sentence can sound marginally
  smoother without actually resolving the underlying grammatical-form
  mismatch, delaying the moment a precise form-check directly exposes
  the remaining structural problem.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when given a
  "smoothed" but still-mismatched correction attempt, spontaneously
  checks whether every item is now the SAME exact part of speech or
  phrase type (rather than accepting it because it "sounds fine") is
  showing the precise-matching discipline has been genuinely
  internalized.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the combined Matched-Place-Settings-and-Instrument-
  Tuning anchor itself is the analogy, not a supplement to it**
  (Blueprint Component 3): matched place settings following the
  identical pattern at any level of complexity (simple or elaborate)
  directly seeds the fix for MC-A (the matching principle applies
  regardless of item complexity), while three instruments tuned to the
  EXACT same pitch — not merely adjusted until they sound roughly
  similar — directly seeds the fix for MC-B (true parallel structure
  requires precise, checked matching, not superficial similarity).
  Breaking point: the combined anchor demonstrates both insights
  clearly but doesn't on its own train the integrated, full-sentence
  synthesis task (TA-4), which needs its own separate, mixed-complexity
  practice set.
- **Alternative — a recipe listing ingredients in inconsistent units**:
  "2 cups flour, a pinch of salt, 3 eggs, some butter" mixes precise
  measurements with vague ones, making the list structurally
  inconsistent even though every item is still, technically, an
  ingredient — parallel to how "swimming, to run, and biking" mixes
  grammatical forms even though every item is still, technically, an
  activity. Breaking point: useful for the mismatched-category insight,
  but doesn't address the precise-vs-superficial-fix distinction, which
  the instrument-tuning anchor's own TA-3 practice handles directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe parallel structure as "just making
  a list sound nicer"**: this framing directly reinforces
  MC-B-FIXING-A-PARALLEL-STRUCTURE-ERROR-JUST-MEANS-MAKING-THE-ITEMS-
  SOUND-SIMILAR by reducing a precise, checkable grammatical
  requirement to a vague aesthetic preference; parallel structure is a
  structural correctness issue with an exact, verifiable test (does
  every item share the identical grammatical form?), not a matter of
  taste or smoothness alone.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions, appropriate
to this concept's `analyze`-level Bloom classification.** The extends-
beyond-single-words insight and the precise-matching-not-surface-
smoothing insight are both genuine, directly testable phenomena well-
suited to guided discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured TA-1's
word-list detection, is shown "She likes swimming, to run, and biking"
and asked whether the same matching-forms principle they just used
applies here too, discovering the mismatch is real once each item's
form is labeled); **playground/invention** (TA-1's word-list practice
builds initial confidence with the core principle before its extension
and precision requirements are introduced); **collision** (the
Blueprint's own swimming/to-run/biking probe breaks the simple-lists-
only assumption; the superficially-adjusted-vs-precisely-matched fix
comparison breaks the sounds-similar-is-enough assumption);
**formalization** (MP-5's full identification-and-correction task,
requiring articulation of both insights across mixed-complexity
sentences); **compression** (TA-4's fluent full-sentence synthesis,
applying both disciplines directly without each instance separately
re-derived). The specific terms "gerund phrase," "infinitive phrase,"
and "parallel structure" are convention-bound grammatical terminology
best introduced by direct instruction once the learner has already felt
the underlying distinctions through the anchor and TA-1–2 — per the
Blueprint's own explicit Adaptive Flags, a learner who struggles with
this abstract labeling may still successfully identify mismatches by
ear/intuition, which should be accepted as a valid entry point before
connecting it to the formal labels needed for TA-3/TA-4's precise
correction work.

## Teaching Sequence

MC-B-FIXING-A-PARALLEL-STRUCTURE-ERROR-JUST-MEANS-MAKING-THE-ITEMS-
SOUND-SIMILAR, addressing a SECOND, independent dimension (correction

Routing, this sequencing is deliberate: MC-A generally needs resolving
before MC-B, since a learner who cannot even detect phrase-level
violations has nothing yet to practice fixing precisely. TA-4 (full
application: identifying and correcting violations) is placed last as
the concept's integration capstone, requiring the learner to apply both
disciplines across a mixed-complexity, mixed-parallel/non-parallel
sentence set. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-
