## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Spell a phonetically regular word by applying syllable-type knowledge directly, using the same patterns already secured for reading.
2. Use visual memory ("does it look right?") to judge between two candidate spellings of an ambiguous word.
3. Spell a word built from recognisable prefixes, suffixes, or roots by assembling its known word-parts, rather than sounding it out as one undifferentiated unit.
4. Choose the correct spelling for a sound with multiple valid spelling options, applying visual memory or word-family knowledge on top of sounding-out.
5. Select and apply the most appropriate strategy (or combination of strategies) to spell a genuinely novel, dictated word.

## Core Understanding

Spelling is **not primarily an act of arbitrary memorisation** — it is, for the substantial majority of English words, the **direct, reverse application of pattern knowledge the learner already holds for reading**: the same syllable-type rules (closed, open, silent-e, vowel-team, r-controlled, consonant-le) that let a learner *decode* an unfamiliar written word into its sound also let that same learner *encode* a heard sound into its most probable written form, simply run in the opposite direction. This concept formalises spelling as a **three-tool strategic toolbox**, to be selected from rather than applied singly and universally: **sound-it-out** (applying syllable-type and phonics pattern knowledge directly — the default, most efficient first move for regular words); **visual memory** ("does this look right?" — judging a candidate spelling against a stored, familiar visual image of the word, necessary whenever sound alone is ambiguous); and **word-part assembly** (spelling by recognising and combining known morphological units — prefixes, suffixes, roots — rather than sounding out a whole complex word from scratch). The concept's second major structural fact directly limits how far the first tool alone can go: **many English sounds have more than one valid spelling** (the /eɪ/ sound alone can be spelled "a_e," "ai," "ay," or "eigh," depending on the specific word), meaning sounding-out reliably narrows the possibilities but frequently cannot, by itself, determine the single correct spelling among several sound-plausible candidates — this is precisely the gap the second and third tools exist to close, and recognising *when* sounding-out alone is insufficient, then reaching for a further tool, is the concept's actual, generalisable target skill, more than any specific spelling itself.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "every word's spelling is its own separate fact to be memorised, letter by letter."**
The runnable simulation: encountering any word to spell, treat it as an unrelated sequence of letters with no exploitable internal structure, and commit the sequence to memory through repetition. This is not tutor-installed — it is a reasonable, if inefficient, default for a learner who has not yet been explicitly shown that the reading-side pattern knowledge they already possess transfers directly to spelling, and it is reinforced by the genuine fact that *some* English spelling really is close to arbitrary (a small set of sight-word-style irregularities, already met at `eng.phonics.sight-words`).
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — attempting to spell "napkin" by memorising ten separate letters, then being shown that "nap" and "kin," both already-known closed-syllable patterns, produce the same spelling far more efficiently.
*Shelf-life warning at replacement*: "Some words really are arbitrary and need pure memorising — but most aren't. Check for a pattern you already know from reading before assuming you have to memorise from scratch."

**Intermediate — "I can apply syllable-type patterns to spell regular words, and I trust that sounding a word out will always tell me the one correct spelling."**
The simulation: correctly apply the sound-it-out tool to regular words, having moved past pure rote memorisation, while not yet recognising that some sounds have multiple valid spellings sounding-out alone cannot disambiguate. This model has resolved the first misconception and not yet met the second.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — correctly sounding out the /eɪ/ sound in a word and confidently guessing "ai" or another sound-plausible option, only to discover the actual word ("eight") uses a spelling sounding-out alone gave no reason to prefer.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Sounding out gets you the right neighbourhood, not always the exact address. When there's more than one plausible spelling for a sound, you need one more check."

**Advanced — "spelling is a three-tool strategic toolbox — sound-it-out, visual memory, and word-part assembly — and the actual skill is recognising which tool, or combination, a given word calls for, especially knowing when sounding-out alone is insufficient and a further check is needed."**
The simulation: given any novel word to spell, the learner selects an appropriate starting strategy, recognises if the result is ambiguous or uncertain, and reaches for a further tool (visual check, or word-part analysis) as needed. This is the target model, matching TA-4 and TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: `eng.writing.sentence-writing` (the direct unlock), where spelling strategy selection must now operate fluidly, in real time, as one component within the larger, more demanding task of composing original sentences, rather than as a dedicated, isolated focus.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This toolbox works well in a focused spelling task. The next step is using it smoothly while you're also busy composing whole sentences and ideas."

**Expert — "the specific set of valid spellings for a given sound, and which spelling a given word actually uses, largely reflects English's layered etymological history (native Germanic vocabulary, French/Latin borrowings, Greek scientific vocabulary, each contributing its own spelling conventions), meaning morphological/etymological awareness is, at the most advanced level, itself a fourth strategic tool beyond the three this concept introduces."**
Named here to mark the arc; this concept deliberately introduces word-part assembly (TA-3) as a *productive-morphology* tool (recognising prefixes/suffixes/roots already known) without requiring the fuller etymological account, which belongs to more advanced vocabulary and word-study work beyond this concept's foundational scope.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant failure mechanism is that **spelling instruction is frequently experienced, and sometimes genuinely delivered, as a fully separate skill from reading** — weekly spelling lists, memorisation drills, and spelling tests are a common, salient instructional format that, by their very structure, invite the inference that spelling is its own independent body of knowledge to be built from scratch, rather than the same pattern knowledge already secured for reading, simply run in reverse. This is not a reasoning failure so much as a natural, format-driven inference the Blueprint's own conflict evidence is specifically designed to disconfirm by demonstrating the transfer directly.

The second mechanism, once pattern-transfer is established, is that **sounding-out is genuinely, overwhelmingly reliable for determining a word's *sound*, and a learner who has just gained confidence in a powerful, mostly-correct strategy has good reason to extend it past its actual scope** — the specific limitation (multiple valid spellings for one sound) is a fact about English's *writing system*, not about the learner's phonics knowledge, and nothing about successfully sounding out a word signals, from the inside, that the resulting sound might correspond to more than one plausible spelling; the learner must be shown specific, concrete cases where this occurs before the limitation becomes visible.

The third mechanism, specific to learners from highly phonetic, transparent-orthography L1 backgrounds, concerns a genuine, well-documented instance of **orthographic transfer**: a learner whose first-language literacy experience has never required anything beyond reliable sound-to-letter mapping (because their L1 genuinely doesn't have English's kind of sound-to-multiple-spelling ambiguity) has no prior occasion to have developed the visual-memory or word-part strategies this concept's second and third tools represent, and will predictably default to over-relying on sound-it-out alone — not from any conceptual confusion, but because their entire prior literacy experience has never needed anything more, exactly the case the Blueprint's own S9 protocol routing anticipates.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns the trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-SPELLING-IS-PURE-MEMORIZATION
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-SPELLING-IS-PURE-MEMORIZATION — includes the napkin/syllable-pattern and unhappy/word-part discrimination pairs.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), and specifically induced by a common instructional *format* (spelling taught and drilled as its own separate subject, via memorised weekly lists) rather than by any single misleading term or symbol — a further instance of the format-induced contamination sub-pattern first identified at `eng.phonetics.connected-speech`'s unstructured-example-list case, here applying to an entire subject's conventional pedagogical delivery rather than to a single concept's example set.
- **Teaching consequence of the birth type**: because the source is a pervasive instructional format rather than a single misleading fact, the repair requires **explicit, repeated cross-referencing back to already-secured reading knowledge**, exactly as the Blueprint's own Component 8 flag specifies ("explicitly connect this concept to the reading-decoding skills already built... spelling is largely the same pattern-knowledge applied in reverse") — a single demonstration is less likely to fully dislodge a format-reinforced default than the sustained, structural connection-making this flag calls for throughout instruction.
- **Verification of death**: given a novel regular word to spell, the learner's first move is to check for a recognisable syllable-type or word-part pattern (audibly reasoning "this is a closed syllable" or "this has the prefix 're-'") rather than to attempt letter-by-letter recall or guessing — the *strategy-first move*, not merely a correct eventual spelling, is the actual target evidence, since a correct spelling could in principle result from lucky rote recall of a previously-drilled word.

### MC-SOUNDING-OUT-ALWAYS-GIVES-CORRECT-SPELLING
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-SOUNDING-OUT-ALWAYS-GIVES-CORRECT-SPELLING — includes the eight/ait and receive/recieve discrimination pairs.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of a genuinely powerful and mostly-correct strategy (sounding-out via syllable-type knowledge) past the specific, bounded class of cases (sounds with multiple valid spellings) where it structurally cannot, by itself, determine a unique answer.
- **Teaching consequence**: because the over-extended strategy remains correct and valuable for the large majority of words, the repair must **narrow sounding-out's claimed scope precisely — from "gives the correct spelling" to "narrows the options" — without diminishing its value as the necessary first step**, exactly as the Blueprint's own replacement text specifies ("use sounding-out as a first step to narrow down possible spellings, then apply visual memory... or morphology to choose the correct one among the options"), mirroring the now-familiar non-destructive rule-scope-narrowing repair pattern established at multiple concepts throughout this program.
- **Verification of death**: given a genuinely novel word containing a sound with multiple valid spellings, the learner correctly sounds it out, recognises the resulting ambiguity explicitly (stating or indicating that more than one spelling is plausible), and then applies a further strategy (visual memory or word-family knowledge) to select among the candidates — the explicit recognition of ambiguity, not merely arriving at a correct final spelling, is the evidence the scope-narrowing has genuinely landed, since a correct spelling could result from simple prior familiarity with that specific word.

## Analogies

**Best — a mechanic's toolbox, with a wrench, a screwdriver, and pliers, and the actual skill being which tool a given job calls for, not memorising the fix for every possible car problem individually.** The Blueprint's own Component 3 anchor is already an analogy in physical, concrete form — a magnifying glass (sound it out), a photograph (visual memory), and puzzle pieces (word parts) — and this framing extends it directly: the skill this concept teaches is tool *selection*, not an ever-growing list of memorised specific fixes.
*Breaking point*: none serious; this closely restates the concept's own designed core instrument rather than introducing a competing metaphor.

**Alternative — a detective narrowing down suspects using several different kinds of evidence (a description, an alibi check, a fingerprint match), where the description alone often leaves more than one plausible suspect.** Sounding-out is like an eyewitness's general description — genuinely useful, often enough to make real progress, but not always sufficient on its own to name exactly one suspect; a further check (visual memory, word-family knowledge) is what closes the gap between "narrowed it down" and "identified the one correct answer."
*Breaking point*: detective evidence-types are independent and can be combined additively; the three spelling strategies are more sequential (sound-it-out first, then a further check only if needed) than simultaneously combined — useful for the *narrows-but-doesn't-always-determine* insight specifically, less precise as a full structural mapping.

**Story analogy — recognising a word's spelling the way you'd recognise a familiar face versus reconstructing a stranger's likely appearance from a description alone.** For a well-known word (a familiar face), visual memory alone works instantly. For a genuinely novel word (a stranger), you have to reconstruct it — starting from a description (the sound), and refining with whatever further information is available (does a similar-sounding word you already know provide a clue?).
*Breaking point*: familiar-face recognition is instantaneous and holistic; spelling strategy application, even for familiar words, still benefits from the underlying pattern awareness this concept builds — useful for contrasting visual memory's role specifically against the other two tools, not as a complete account of the whole toolbox.

**Visual analogy — the spelling toolbox** (Blueprint Component 3): a magnifying glass, a photograph, and puzzle pieces, tried in sequence for a target word. Not a metaphor but the actual working instrument this concept's core teaching is organised around, and it should remain the standing, explicitly-named reference throughout instruction, per Component 8's own flag that the three tools should be "revisited as three coordinated tools throughout spelling instruction, not taught once and abandoned."

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Spelling is basically reading backwards."** True in an important structural sense (the same pattern knowledge, reversed direction), but stated this baldly it risks implying the transfer is total and automatic, obscuring the genuine, separate additional demands (visual memory, word-part assembly) this concept must also teach — the relationship is real but partial, and this phrasing oversells the "backwards" simplicity.
- **"If you sound it out carefully enough, you'll always get the spelling right."** Directly installs MC-SOUNDING-OUT-ALWAYS-GIVES-CORRECT-SPELLING as if it were an encouraging piece of advice rather than the misconception itself.
- **"English spelling doesn't make sense, so there's no point looking for patterns."** Overstates English spelling's genuine irregularity and would, if believed, directly reinforce MC-SPELLING-IS-PURE-MEMORIZATION by asserting that pattern-seeking is futile — exactly the opposite of this concept's central, evidence-backed claim.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The spelling toolbox (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there, walking through all three tools for one target word. *Predict first*, before trying each tool: "Which of these three do you think will get us closest to the right answer for this word?"
2. **The napkin syllable-pattern collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence. *Predict first*: "Do you think you have to memorise all ten letters of 'napkin' separately, or is there a shortcut using something you already know?" Then show the nap-kin syllable-pattern transfer.
3. **The eight/ait ambiguity reveal (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "If you sound out the /eɪ/ sound at the end of this word, is there only one way it could be spelled?" Then reveal "eight" as the actual, sound-plausible-but-not-uniquely-determined answer.
4. **The word-part assembly task (learner activity, TA-3).** *Predict first*: "Do you recognise any smaller, already-known pieces inside this longer word?" Then assemble "unhappy" from "un-" and "happy."

## Discovery Questions

This concept splits, following the pattern established at multiple concepts in this program: **the specific inventory of which sound corresponds to which possible spellings is largely surveyed directly** (a fact about English orthography, told and demonstrated), but **the core insight that reading-side pattern knowledge transfers directly to spelling, and the recognition that sounding-out has a genuine scope limit, are both well suited to guided discovery**.

1. **Need**: "You already know how to read 'napkin' using syllable types. Does any of that knowledge help you spell it too?"
2. **Playground**: the learner attempts to spell a familiar, already-decodable word freely, using whatever strategy comes naturally.
3. **Invention**: "What did you actually use to figure out the spelling — did you remember it letter by letter, or did something from reading help?" The learner identifies, from their own attempt, whether syllable-type knowledge was (or could have been) used.
4. **Collision**: present a word with a genuinely ambiguous sound-to-spelling mapping (the /eɪ/ example) and ask the learner to sound it out — confirming that sounding-out alone leaves more than one plausible answer, motivating the need for a further tool.
5. **Formalization**: name the toolbox — sound-it-out, visual memory, word-part assembly — as three coordinated strategies, with sound-it-out narrowing the options and the other two closing genuine gaps it leaves open.
6. **Compression**: "Sound it out first. If there's more than one way it could look, check what looks right or what parts you recognise."

## Teaching Sequence

The pedagogical logic behind this arc:

- **Sound-it-out via syllable-type transfer (TA-1) comes first**, directly and explicitly connecting to already-secured reading knowledge before either of the other two tools is introduced — establishing this transfer as the concept's opening move is the direct, structural repair for MC-SPELLING-IS-PURE-MEMORIZATION, and it must precede the other tools so the learner doesn't mistake visual memory or word-part assembly for the *primary* strategy when sound-it-out, applied correctly, remains the more efficient default for the majority of regular words.
- **Visual memory (TA-2) and word-part assembly (TA-3) are introduced as genuinely separate, additional tools, not as refinements of sound-it-out** — the Blueprint's own sequencing treats them as distinct TAs with their own dedicated practice, and TA-3 is explicitly the vehicle for confronting the first misconception's more advanced form (recognising word-part patterns, not just syllable-type patterns, as a further efficient alternative to rote memorisation).
- **Multiple-spelling sound discrimination (TA-4) is deliberately sequenced after all three tools are individually secure**, since resolving a genuine spelling ambiguity requires combining sound-it-out (to narrow the options) with either visual memory or word-family knowledge (to select among them) — this task cannot be meaningfully attempted until both halves of that combination are independently available.
- **Strategy selection for novel words (TA-5) comes last**, requiring the learner to judge, for an entirely new dictated word, which tool or combination the specific word calls for — matching the general principle established throughout this program that a concept's most integrative task (here, genuine strategic judgement rather than applying a single named tool on request) is reserved for its culminating stage.

Turn-by-turn scripting, protocol routing, and adaptive flags: Blueprint **Component 7 — Session Architecture** and **Component 8 — Adaptive Flags** (in particular the three-strategy-toolbox standing framework, the explicit reading-to-spelling transfer connection, the dedicated ambiguous-sound practice, and the L1-orthographic-transparency adjustment for S9 learners).

## Tutor Actions

In recommended order, from `../teaching-actions/`:

1. **Demonstration** (SHOW) — the spelling toolbox walkthrough and the napkin and eight/ait collisions, all benefiting from being modelled explicitly and narrated aloud, tool by tool.
2. **Matching** (DO) — sound to candidate spellings (for the multiple-spelling discrimination task), and word to its component word-parts (for the morphology-assembly task), both directly building the target strategy-application skills.
3. **Prediction** (TEST-THINKING) — predicting which tool will work best for a given word before attempting it, and predicting whether a given sound has one or several plausible spellings before checking, both directly exercising the target strategic-judgement skill.
4. **Concept Map** (ORGANIZE) — the spelling toolbox itself, functioning as a standing, revisitable reference artefact throughout instruction, per Component 8's own explicit flag that the three tools should remain a continuously-referenced framework rather than a one-time introduction.
5. **Error Analysis** (TEST-THINKING) — presenting two candidate spellings (one correct, one a plausible phonetic misspelling, mirroring TA-2's own design) and asking the learner to judge and justify which "looks right," directly practising the visual-memory strategy.

**Does not fit**: **Worked Example** in the multi-step-derivation sense — spelling attempts are better practised through direct application (Demonstration, Matching) than through an abstract worked derivation. **Game** before all three tools are individually secure — speed pressure risks reinforcing whichever single tool is currently most automatic (very likely sound-it-out) at the expense of genuine, deliberate tool selection, precisely the skill this concept's culminating stage requires.

## Voice Teaching Notes

This concept's core evidence is primarily **written** (the produced spelling itself), with voice functioning mainly as the verification and reasoning channel (saying a word aloud before attempting to spell it, and articulating which strategy was used or why a candidate spelling was chosen), making its evidence profile closer to `eng.phonetics.ipa-basics`' pattern (voice supports and verifies a mainly written task) than to the purely auditory phonetics concepts in this program.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A word spelled letter-by-letter, slowly, with no audible reference to syllables, sounds, or word-parts** — the direct behavioural signature of MC-SPELLING-IS-PURE-MEMORIZATION, distinguishable from genuine strategy application by the absence of any audible "chunking" or pattern-naming during the attempt.
- **Audible syllable- or word-part-naming immediately preceding a correct spelling attempt** ("nap... kin... nap-kin" or "un... happy"). Strong positive evidence the target transfer strategy is actively in use, and it should be named and reinforced explicitly when observed, exactly as equivalent audible-strategy-use behaviours have been praised at several phonics concepts throughout this program.
- **A confident, immediate spelling attempt on a word with a genuinely ambiguous sound-to-spelling mapping, with no audible hesitation or acknowledgment that more than one spelling was plausible.** The direct behavioural signature of MC-SOUNDING-OUT-ALWAYS-GIVES-CORRECT-SPELLING, and it should be checked against the actual, stated reasoning (or lack thereof) rather than judged on the correctness of the final answer alone, per the established verification-of-death criterion above.
- **A brief pause, an audible "hmm, that could be a few different ways," or an explicit visual-memory check ("that doesn't look right, let me try...") on a genuinely ambiguous word.** The target strategic-recognition behaviour, and it should be praised as the actual skill this concept teaches, independent of whether the very first candidate spelling attempted happens to be correct.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Sound it out first — then ask if there's more than one way that could look, and check what looks right or what parts you recognise."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads, which the Blueprint does not own:

- **Sound-it-out via syllable type (MP-1).** *Correct, with audible syllable-naming or chunking* → strong evidence of genuine strategy transfer, not rote recall. *Correct with no audible strategy reference, on a word that could plausibly have been previously drilled* → treat as weaker, potentially rote-recall-based evidence, and follow up with a genuinely unfamiliar regular word before certifying this tool.
- **Visual memory check (MP-2).** Carries somewhat limited diagnostic weight in isolation, since a learner could correctly select the familiar-looking option from prior exposure to that specific word rather than from a generalisable visual-memory *strategy* — this tool is most convincingly demonstrated in combination with MP-4's ambiguous-sound item, where it must be applied to genuinely uncertain material.
- **Word-part assembly (MP-3).** *Correct, with the specific word-parts named or audibly segmented* → strong evidence for this tool specifically, distinct from and not guaranteed by success on MP-1's syllable-type tool, since word-part assembly draws on morphological rather than purely phonological pattern knowledge.
- **Multiple-spelling discrimination (MP-4).** This is this concept's most diagnostic single item for the second misconception, per the same reasoning established under Misconceptions above — the *explicit acknowledgment* that sounding-out alone left genuine ambiguity, not merely the correct final choice, is the target evidence, and should be actively elicited ("how did you know it wasn't spelled the other way?") rather than assumed from a correct answer alone.
- **Explanation (MP-5).** Listen for whether the explanation names at least two of the three tools by their actual function (not merely "sounding out and something else") — a response that can only articulate the sound-it-out tool clearly has not yet secured the toolbox's full, three-part structure even if prior items were answered correctly by other means.

**Mastery certification trigger**: correct spelling of a novel regular word via syllable-type application, with the strategy audibly demonstrated; correct visual-memory selection between two candidate spellings of a genuinely novel word; correct word-part assembly of a novel morphologically-complex word, with the specific parts named; correct resolution of a novel multiple-spelling-sound word, with the initial ambiguity explicitly acknowledged before the correct choice is justified; and successful strategy selection (choosing and correctly applying the most appropriate tool or combination) for a genuinely novel dictated word not fitting neatly into any single previously-drilled category. The strategy-selection requirement on the final item is essential and matches the Blueprint's own explicit Component 6 design — this concept's actual mastery bar is judgement about *which* tool to use, not merely correct execution of any single tool in isolation.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"I already know how to read this word, why do I have to learn to spell it separately?"** — take this as evidence the learner has correctly, if only partially, grasped the reading-spelling connection this concept is built around; respond by extending rather than contradicting: "You're right that a lot of what you already know from reading applies directly — let's use it. The only new part is checking whether the sound you're spelling has more than one possible way to look, which reading doesn't always show you." This both validates the insight and names precisely what remains genuinely new.
- **"But it sounds exactly like 'ait,' why isn't that right?"** — a completely reasonable objection given how reliably sounding-out has worked so far; respond by validating the phonetic accuracy of the guess before introducing the further check, exactly as the Blueprint's own s6_path specifies: "Your ear is totally right — that is what it sounds like. English just has more than one way to spell that sound, and this particular word happens to use a less common one. That's exactly the kind of case where sounding out gets you close but not all the way there."
- **The smaller question to shrink to**: from strategy selection or ambiguous-sound resolution, down to **the sound-it-out tool alone, applied to a single, maximally regular, already-familiar closed-syllable word, with no ambiguity and no morphology involved.** This removes the visual-memory and word-part-assembly demands entirely, isolating the most basic, most reliable tool before returning to the harder, combined strategic-judgement tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just try to remember how the word looked the last time you saw it written."** For a genuinely novel word the learner has never seen written, this instruction is simply inapplicable and would produce pure guessing rather than genuine strategy application.

## Memory Hooks

- **Concept type**: strategic toolkit (three named tools, each itself drawing on prior pattern knowledge) plus a **judgement skill** (selecting the appropriate tool or combination for a given word) that must generalise to genuinely novel material, mirroring the concept-type structure already established at `eng.phonics.digraphs` (a discrimination procedure plus a calibrated-confidence judgement) and `eng.phonics.syllable-types` (a classification system plus a general procedural strategy).
- **Review form — sound-it-out via syllable-type**: this largely maintains itself through continued interleaving with `eng.phonics.syllable-types` review, since the two concepts share the identical underlying pattern knowledge, applied in opposite directions.
- **Review form — visual memory and word-part assembly**: dedicated, separate spaced review specifically on words this concept's other tool (sound-it-out) cannot fully resolve, since these two tools have no equivalent "free" maintenance from another concept's review cycle and would otherwise risk being under-practised relative to the more heavily-reinforced sound-it-out default.
- **Review form — multiple-spelling discrimination and strategy selection**: review using genuinely novel words on a rotating basis, always requiring the explicit acknowledgment-of-ambiguity or stated-strategy-choice alongside the final spelling, per the established diagnostic emphasis throughout this entry.
- **Interleaving partners**: `eng.phonics.syllable-types` items should continue to be interleaved here in both directions (reading-decoding and spelling-encoding of the same pattern types), reinforcing rather than duplicating the shared underlying knowledge, exactly the relationship already flagged as this concept's core structural fact.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: `eng.writing.sentence-writing` — the direct unlock, requiring spelling strategy selection to operate fluidly and in real time as one component within composing original sentences, rather than as a dedicated, isolated focus.
- **Near**: `eng.phonics.syllable-types` — the shared, bidirectional relationship already established throughout this entry; reading-decoding and spelling-encoding are, for the majority of regular words, the same pattern knowledge applied in opposite directions.
- **Far**: vocabulary growth and morphological awareness generally — the word-part assembly tool, once well-developed, gives a learner a genuine analytical foothold into unfamiliar but morphologically transparent vocabulary (recognising "un-," "re-," "-ing," "-ful" in new words), extending well beyond spelling into reading comprehension and word-meaning inference.
- **Real-world**: using a dictionary or spell-checker effectively — a learner who understands *why* a spell-checker sometimes offers several plausible corrections (because a misspelled attempt phonetically matches several valid English spellings) has genuine insight into how such tools work and why they occasionally fail on genuinely rare or unusual words.
- **Expert transfer**: the durable skill is **applying an already-mastered analytical framework in the reverse direction to solve a structurally related but practically distinct problem, while recognising the specific points where the reversed application requires an additional, non-obvious extra step** — the same transfer skill needed in applying a decoding/encoding relationship in any symbolic system (translating between two notations, converting between representations in mathematics or programming), where the forward and reverse directions share most, but not quite all, of their underlying logic.
