# Word Families — `eng.vocab.word-families`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.word-families` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Word Families" — groups of
  words that share a common base word or root and related meaning,
  taught as meaning-rooted groupings explicitly distinguished from mere
  spelling similarity.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.word-recognition` — load-bearing because
  recognizing a genuine word family requires already treating each
  member word as a meaning-bearing unit connected to known vocabulary;
  a learner still judging words by decoded sound alone, without a
  secure sense of word-meaning, has no reliable basis for testing
  whether two words share an actual meaning connection versus merely
  looking alike.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.vocab.compound-words`,
  `eng.vocab.prefixes`, `eng.vocab.suffixes`. This concept's core
  insight — that words are built from meaningful parts (a root plus
  modifying pieces) rather than being unanalyzable wholes — is the
  direct conceptual foundation for identifying prefixes and suffixes as
  specific, namable modifying pieces, and for recognizing compound
  words as a different but related way two meaningful units combine.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Confirm that a set of related word forms shares a genuine meaning
   connection through a common root.
2. Distinguish a true word family (shared root meaning) from words that
   merely share similar-looking spelling at the start with no actual
   meaning connection.
3. Identify that word-family members typically span different word
   classes (verb, noun, adjective, adverb) built from the same root,
   rather than assuming all members share one word class.
4. Generate multiple word-family members from a given root word.
5. Use knowledge of a known root's meaning to infer the meaning of an
   unfamiliar word that is itself a member of that same family.

## Core Understanding

A word family is a group of words that share a common base word or root
and a genuinely related meaning — "act," "action," "active," "actively,"
and "reaction" all connect back to the core meaning of "doing," despite
looking and functioning quite differently on the surface. The defining
test for a true word family is a shared MEANING connection through a
common root, not merely similar-looking spelling: "car," "care," and
"card" happen to share letters at the start but share no meaning
connection whatsoever, and are not a word family at all, however much
they might superficially resemble one. A second, equally load-bearing
fact is that genuine word-family members typically span MULTIPLE word
classes built from the same root — "act" (verb), "action" (noun),
"active" (adjective), and "actively" (adverb) are all members of one
family despite belonging to different grammatical categories, so
assuming every family member must share the first member's word class
is a mistaken constraint on what a word family actually is. Recognizing
these two facts together makes word-family knowledge genuinely useful
for vocabulary growth: once a learner knows a root's core meaning, they
can generate or infer the meaning of unfamiliar related forms across
several different grammatical roles, rather than needing to learn each
form as an entirely separate, unconnected word.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "words that look similar (share starting letters)
  belong together as a group"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model
  this concept exists to correct — a natural extension of basic visual/
  spelling pattern-recognition, which genuinely does work for identifying
  many TRUE word families (since related words often do share visible
  letter sequences), giving no built-in signal that spelling similarity
  alone is an unreliable test.
- **Intermediate model — "I can confirm whether a set of related word
  forms shares a genuine meaning connection through a common root"**:
  installed by TA-1's identification practice, using the Component 3
  word-family tree to make the shared-root-meaning relationship visually
  concrete. Upgrade trigger: correctly explaining WHY a set of words
  belongs together (citing the shared root meaning) rather than only
  confirming that they do.
- **Advanced model — "spelling similarity alone is not enough — I check
  for an actual shared meaning before grouping words as a family"**:
  installed by TA-2's true-family-vs-spelling-lookalike sorting task,
  resolving MC-SIMILAR-SPELLING-MEANS-SAME-FAMILY through the felt
  contrast between "act/action/reaction" and "car/care/card." Upgrade
  trigger: correctly rejecting a spelling-similar-but-unrelated pair
  without being told to check for a meaning connection.
- **Expert model — "word-family members can span different word classes
  built from the same root, and I can generate new family members and
  infer unfamiliar ones' meanings productively"**: installed by TA-3's
  word-class-variation task (resolving MC-WORD-FAMILY-MEMBERS-ARE-
  ALWAYS-SAME-WORD-CLASS), TA-4's generation task, and TA-5's meaning-
  inference task. This is the concept's genuine mastery bar — moving
  from passive recognition of a family to active, productive use of
  root-meaning knowledge across multiple grammatical forms.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 learners whose L1 uses a very different morphological system (an
  isolating language with little derivational morphology, or affixes
  that work very differently from English), the very idea of a
  "morphological family" built from a shared root plus modifying pieces
  may not map cleanly from their L1 structure — this concept should
  provide more foundational scaffolding on the underlying idea itself
  for these learners, rather than assuming the pattern-recognition skill
  transfers readily from their existing language intuitions.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of otherwise-useful pattern-recognition habits. The
spelling-as-family-test failure exists because shared starting letters
genuinely DO correlate with true word families most of the time (most
words that look alike at the start really are morphologically related),
so a learner reasonably relies on this fast, visual heuristic, with
nothing in ordinary exposure signaling the heuristic can mislead until a
specifically constructed counterexample (a spelling-similar-but-
meaning-unrelated set like "car/care/card") is directly presented. The
same-word-class-assumption failure exists because it is a natural
default to expect related words to behave the same grammatically —
nothing about encountering "act" as a verb inherently signals that its
family members will include nouns, adjectives, and adverbs, so a
learner reasonably extends "these are the same kind of word" from
"these are related words," until directly shown that "action" functions
as a noun in an actual sentence despite belonging to the same family as
the verb "act."

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.vocab.word-families.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions
this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-SIMILAR-SPELLING-MEANS-SAME-FAMILY (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — shared starting
  letters genuinely predict true word-family membership for the large
  majority of cases a learner encounters (most spelling-similar sets ARE
  related), so the learner extends this reliable correlation into an
  unconditional rule, with nothing in typical vocabulary exposure
  marking the coincidental-overlap exception until directly tested.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when given a new
  spelling-similar set, spontaneously asks "wait, do these actually mean
  anything related, or do they just look alike?" before being prompted
  to check is showing the meaning-check habit has generalized beyond
  the specific TA-2 practice pairs.

**MC-WORD-FAMILY-MEMBERS-ARE-ALWAYS-SAME-WORD-CLASS (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: also a Type 1 signature, distinct
  mechanism — a learner's default expectation that "related words behave
  alike" is a reasonable extrapolation from how vocabulary is often
  first encountered (a single word in a single grammatical role), with
  nothing in that initial encounter signaling that the SAME root can
  recombine into multiple distinct grammatical roles until a full family
  is presented together and each member's actual sentence role is
  examined.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when generating
  family members (TA-4), spontaneously produces forms across multiple
  word classes ("act, action, active, actively...") without being
  prompted to include different grammatical categories is showing the
  word-class-diversity pattern has been genuinely internalized as
  expected, not merely tolerated when pointed out.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the word-family tree itself is the analogy, not a
  supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): one root at the base with
  branches extending to each related word-class form, all connecting
  back to the same root meaning, directly embodies genuine word-family
  structure — with a separate, deliberately unconnected set of spelling-
  lookalikes drawn off to the side, explicitly excluded from the tree.
  Breaking point: a tree implies a single, tidy branching structure,
  which can understate that real word families sometimes have quite
  irregular or unevenly-developed branches (not every root produces a
  clean verb/noun/adjective/adverb set) — this entry treats the tree as
  the introductory frame for the shared-root-meaning principle, not a
  claim that every family branches identically.
- **Alternative — a family surname passed down with different first
  names**: family members (Alex Smith, Jordan Smith, Sam Smith) share
  the surname "Smith" (the root) while each having a distinct first name
  and role in the family — analogous to a root ("act") appearing across
  distinct word-class "identities" (action, active, actively). Breaking
  point: useful for the "shared root, different individual identity"
  structure, but should not be extended to suggest word-family members
  are otherwise unrelated in behavior the way different family members
  might have entirely separate lives — word-family members remain
  meaningfully connected in usage, not merely sharing a label.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe spelling-lookalike words (like "car,"
  "care," "card") as a "false" or "fake" word family**: framing them as
  a deceptive or invalid category risks implying word families are
  primarily about avoiding a trick, rather than about recognizing a
  genuine, positive pattern in how English builds vocabulary. These
  words should simply be described as sharing no root-meaning
  connection, full stop — not as a "trap" to be wary of.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (word-family tree)**: the Blueprint's Component 3
  anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: the tree can be drawn on any paper, described
  verbally, or built with simple word cards arranged in a branching
  layout; no special materials are required.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's generation task (producing as
  many family members as possible from a root) and TA-5's meaning-
  inference task are both hands-on, productive activities requiring the
  learner to generate or infer, not merely recognize a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (spelling-lookalike contrast)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — contrasting "act,
  action, reaction" (true family) with "car, care, card" (spelling
  overlap only) — making the meaning-vs-spelling distinction directly
  visible.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  family-membership judgment (TA-1/TA-2) or a word-class identification
  (TA-3), ask the learner to state their own judgment before the tutor
  confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
meaning-vs-spelling test and word-class diversity within a family are
both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured word recognition, is asked
whether "car," "care," and "card" belong together just because they
look similar); **playground/invention** (TA-1's root-and-family-members
practice across several roots lets the learner notice the shared-
meaning pattern); **collision** (the Blueprint's own car/care/card
conflict-evidence probe breaks the spelling-only assumption; the
"is 'action' a verb?" probe breaks the same-word-class assumption);
**formalization** (MP-5's own-words inference task, applying the root-
meaning principle to a genuinely unfamiliar family member); **compression**
(TA-4's fluent generation of multiple family members without needing
each one's meaning connection separately re-derived). There is no
convention-bound terminology here requiring separate direct instruction
beyond the label "word family" itself, best introduced once the learner
has already felt the underlying shared-meaning pattern through the
anchor and TA-1.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (identifying a genuine word family from a root) is placed first as
the concept's most basic, confirmatory task — presenting an already-
clear family and having the learner confirm the shared meaning
connection, before any contrast or complication is introduced. TA-2
(distinguishing true families from spelling lookalikes) follows
immediately, directly targeting MC-SIMILAR-SPELLING-MEANS-SAME-FAMILY —
per the Blueprint's own session architecture, this conflict-evidence
probe is placed right after TA-1, once the learner has a secure sense of
what a genuine family looks like to contrast against a lookalike set.
TA-3 (word class variation within a family) follows next, introducing
the second core insight only after the basic family concept (TA-1) and
its meaning-based test (TA-2) are both established — attempting the
word-class distinction earlier would risk conflating "these words are
grammatically different" with "these words aren't really related" at a
point when the learner's family concept is not yet secure enough to
hold both facts at once. TA-4 (generating word family members) is
placed after TA-3 specifically because productive generation benefits
from the learner already expecting multiple word classes to appear —
without TA-3's insight, a learner might artificially limit their
generation attempt to only the root's own word class. TA-5 (using word
family knowledge to infer meaning) is placed last as the concept's
integration and practical-payoff capstone, applying the full family
concept (shared meaning, spanning word classes) to the genuinely useful
task of inferring an unfamiliar word's meaning from a known root. This
entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script
(Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously cites the
  shared MEANING (not just shared letters) when explaining why a set of
  words belongs together — this is the primary diagnostic signal for
  whether TA-2's meaning-based test has genuinely replaced the surface
  spelling heuristic as the learner's default approach.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before confirming a
  borderline or unfamiliar word-family grouping, followed by a correct
  meaning-based judgment, suggests the learner is consciously checking
  for a genuine connection rather than pattern-matching on spelling
  alone — a good sign. Instant, confident grouping of a spelling-similar
  but unrelated set (with no meaning check at all) is the concerning
  pattern for MC-SIMILAR-SPELLING-MEANS-SAME-FAMILY.
- **Productive-generation stakes**: real and central to TA-4/MP-4 — a
  learner who generates only ONE word class (e.g., only verbs) from a
  root, without prompting, may still be under the influence of
  MC-WORD-FAMILY-MEMBERS-ARE-ALWAYS-SAME-WORD-CLASS even if they passed
  TA-3's more structured identification task, since spontaneous
  generation is a stronger test of internalized expectation than guided
  recognition.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does this actually share a meaning, or
  do they just look alike?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for
  MC-SIMILAR-SPELLING-MEANS-SAME-FAMILY, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh spelling-similar set is introduced, making the
  meaning-check a routinely-invited response rather than an afterthought.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a family-identification task shrinks to the
  Component 3 word-family tree using the simplest, most familiar root
  available (e.g., "happy"), with the tutor building the branches
  together before the learner attempts a fresh root independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: a confident grouping of
  spelling-similar-but-unrelated words is more likely than "I don't
  know" for MC-SIMILAR-SPELLING-MEANS-SAME-FAMILY — route directly to
  the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence probe (checking for an actual
  meaning connection) rather than treating it as a generic vocabulary
  gap. For MC-WORD-FAMILY-MEMBERS-ARE-ALWAYS-SAME-WORD-CLASS, a
  generation attempt limited to a single word class is more likely than
  outright confusion — prompt explicitly for "what about a word that
  names the ACTION as a thing, not a doing-word?" to elicit the noun
  form.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously generates family members across multiple word classes
  without being prompted to diversify is showing the word-class-
  diversity pattern has genuinely generalized — this should be named
  and praised explicitly, per the Blueprint's own regulation-tail
  guidance (praising checking for shared meaning over shared spelling).

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework — a morphological-relationship
framework (shared root meaning, spanning multiple word classes) applied
to genuinely novel roots and word sets, combined with a productive
generation skill (TA-4); mastery is demonstrated both by correctly
classifying unfamiliar word sets (MP-2/MP-3) and by fluently generating
and inferring from a known root (MP-4/MP-5). Interleaving partners: mix
receptive classification items (testing TA-1/TA-2/TA-3, confirming or
rejecting a given grouping) with productive generation and inference
items (testing TA-4/TA-5, requiring the learner to produce novel forms
or meanings) in ongoing review, so both the analytical and the
generative sides of word-family knowledge are separately verified.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: confirming or rejecting fresh word-family candidates,
  identifying word classes within a family, and generating or inferring
  from a fresh root.
- **Far**: this concept's shared-root-meaning principle is the direct
  foundation for `eng.vocab.prefixes` and `eng.vocab.suffixes` (specific,
  namable modifying pieces attached to a root) and for
  `eng.vocab.compound-words` (a related but distinct pattern of
  combining two whole meaningful words rather than a root plus affixes).
- **Real-world**: every unfamiliar word the learner encounters that
  shares a root with an already-known word is an immediate, ungated
  opportunity to apply root-meaning inference without needing a
  dictionary.
- **Expert-transfer**: efficient vocabulary growth throughout a reader's
  lifetime depends substantially on fluent root-based inference, since a
  single well-known root can unlock the approximate meaning of dozens of
  related but individually unencountered word forms.
