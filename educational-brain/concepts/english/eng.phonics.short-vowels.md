# Short Vowel Sounds — `eng.phonics.short-vowels`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.phonics.short-vowels` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / phonics / "Short Vowel Sounds" —
  sibling to `eng.phonics.consonants`, both branching directly from
  letter-sound correspondence; together they complete the two sound
  classes a learner needs before combining letters into full CVC words.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.phonics.letter-sound-correspondence` —
  specifically the symbol-to-sound mapping principle; load-bearing
  because this concept immediately layers a second, competing mapping
  onto each vowel letter (its alphabet NAME, already familiar from
  alphabet instruction, versus its short SOUND, which this concept
  installs) — the prerequisite's general principle is what makes "one
  letter, more than one thing to say" a coherent idea rather than a
  contradiction.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.phonics.consonant-blends`. Short vowels
  complete the CVC (consonant-vowel-consonant) syllable structure that
  consonant blends extend (e.g., CCVC "stop") — a learner cannot
  correctly read a blended word without already being secure on the
  vowel sound at its core.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.85 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Produce each of the five short vowel sounds in isolation, on sight
   of the letter, using a fixed keyword anchor.
2. Identify and produce the short vowel sound within a closed-syllable
   (CVC) word.
3. Distinguish a vowel letter's alphabet NAME from its short SOUND when
   asked for each separately.
4. Determine, from spelling alone (before pronouncing), whether a
   one-syllable word is closed (short vowel) or ends in a silent "e"
   (long vowel), on both familiar and novel minimal pairs.
5. Read a genuinely unseen CVC word correctly, using the short vowel
   sound, demonstrating generalization beyond the taught word set.

## Core Understanding

A vowel letter's short sound is not an arbitrary alternative to its
alphabet name — it is the sound that letter takes specifically inside a
CLOSED syllable: one vowel, immediately followed by one or more
consonants, with no silent "e" at the syllable's end. This structural
condition, not syllable count or word length, is what determines
whether a vowel is short. The single most consequential fact this
concept must install is that "one syllable" and "short vowel" are NOT
the same claim — a huge number of common one-syllable English words
(cake, pine, cute) have long, not short, vowels, distinguished from
their short-vowel counterparts (cap, pin, cut) by exactly one silent
letter at the end. Short-vowel identification is therefore inseparable
from a spelling check (is there a silent e?), not merely a sound the
learner produces once cued by seeing any vowel letter in isolation.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "each vowel letter has a special short sound, cued
  by a keyword picture"**: installed directly by the Component 3
  five-keyword anchor (apple, elephant, igloo, octopus, umbrella or
  equivalent). Sufficient for TA-1's isolated-sound production but not
  yet connected to reading real words, and vulnerable to the alphabet-
  name intrusion below.
- **Intermediate model — "the vowel letter's NAME (from the alphabet)
  and its short SOUND (in a real word) are two different things I must
  keep separate"**: the correct first discrimination, installed by
  TA-3's explicit name-vs-sound contrast. A learner who has not yet
  reached this model will substitute the letter's alphabet name for its
  sound when reading (MC-VOWEL-LETTER-NAME-IS-THE-SOUND). Upgrade
  trigger: TA-3's direct contrast items ("the letter 'i' says 'eye,' but
  in 'sit' it says /ɪ/").
- **Advanced model — "short vs. long vowel is determined by syllable
  STRUCTURE (closed vs. silent-e), which I check by looking at the
  spelling before I read"**: installed by TA-4's minimal-pair
  discrimination (cap/cape, pin/pine, cut/cute). This is the model that
  resolves MC-ONE-SYLLABLE-WORDS-ALWAYS-SHORT-VOWEL — the learner now
  checks structure, not merely counts syllables. Upgrade trigger: correct
  vowel-length prediction on a novel minimal pair, made from spelling
  BEFORE pronouncing the word aloud.
- **Expert model — fluent, automatic short/long discrimination requiring
  no conscious spelling check** (TA-5's target, and beyond): the
  structural check from the advanced model becomes instantaneous and
  unconscious during ordinary reading — this concept's mastery gate
  targets the advanced model made reliable, with full automaticity
  developing through the ongoing reading practice that follows this
  node, not required for mastery here.
- **Do not upgrade early**: a learner not yet secure on TA-2's basic CVC
  short-vowel reading should not be given TA-4's silent-e minimal pairs
  — the closed/silent-e distinction is a refinement on top of an already-
  secure short-vowel base, not a substitute path to it.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here. The alphabet-name
substitution failure exists because alphabet-song instruction
(universally taught before or alongside phonics) teaches each vowel
letter's NAME with total confidence and high repetition, and nothing in
that instruction marks a boundary where the name stops applying inside
words — a learner who has thoroughly learned "a says 'ay'" from the
alphabet song is not confused, they are correctly generalizing their
only prior instruction into a new context where it happens to be wrong.
The one-syllable-therefore-short-vowel failure exists because syllable
count is a much more perceptually available property of a word than
its internal structure (closed vs. silent-e) — a learner can count
syllables by ear almost immediately, while noticing a single silent
letter at a word's end requires a deliberate visual check that nothing
in ordinary spoken-language processing would prompt on its own.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.phonics.short-vowels.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions
this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added (`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-VOWEL-LETTER-NAME-IS-THE-SOUND (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 3,
language contamination**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: this is language contamination rather
  than overgeneralization from a phonics rule — the "wrong" sound the
  learner produces is not an error internal to the phonics system at
  all, it is an entirely different, correctly-learned piece of prior
  knowledge (the alphabet-song name) intruding into a domain (reading
  real words) where a different convention applies. The learner has not
  overextended a rule; they have applied the wrong one of two
  independently correct systems.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when asked to read
  "pin," produces something closer to "pine" and shows no hesitation is
  demonstrating the substitution is still fully active and unmonitored;
  a learner who produces the same error but then self-corrects
  ("pin... no wait, pin") is already partway to the intermediate model
  and needs less intervention, only confirmation.

**MC-ONE-SYLLABLE-WORDS-ALWAYS-SHORT-VOWEL (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: the short-vowel-in-one-syllable-words
  pattern is genuinely, overwhelmingly true across the FIRST word set a
  beginning reader typically meets (cat, hot, sit, cup — all CVC, all
  short) — Type 1's exact signature: a rule valid in its dominant early
  domain, extended into a domain (silent-e words) where it silently
  breaks, with nothing in the early word set marking the boundary.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner reading "cape" as though
  it rhymed with "cap" (rather than pausing at the unfamiliar silent
  letter) is showing this misconception is fully active; noticing and
  querying the extra "e" before attempting to read the word ("what's
  this e doing here?") is evidence the advanced model is already
  forming, even before it is formally taught.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the vowel's alphabet name is its "formal title";
  its short sound is its "everyday nickname" used inside real words**:
  both are legitimate and correctly learned; the skill is knowing which
  context calls for which. Breaking point: "nickname" may suggest the
  short sound is somehow less correct or less important than the name
  — both must be framed as equally legitimate, context-dependent uses.
- **Alternative — a silent "e" is a remote control that changes the
  vowel from across the room**: it does not touch the vowel directly
  (it sits at the word's end) but still changes what sound the vowel
  makes. Breaking point: "remote control" may suggest the effect is
  magical or unexplainable rather than a real, consistent spelling
  pattern — should be paired with the structural explanation (closed
  vs. silent-e syllable), not left as an unexplained trick.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT frame the silent "e" as simply "doing
  nothing"**: describing it as silent-and-therefore-irrelevant directly
  installs MC-ONE-SYLLABLE-WORDS-ALWAYS-SHORT-VOWEL's mechanism by
  suggesting the letter can be ignored entirely, when in fact it is
  doing significant structural work (changing the preceding vowel) even
  though it is not itself pronounced. Always frame it as "silent, but
  changes the vowel before it," never as "silent, so it doesn't matter."

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/auditory (five keyword pictures)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: the five keyword sounds can be rehearsed from
  memory once installed, with any household object substituting for a
  picture card (an apple in the kitchen, for instance).
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's minimal-pair sort (cap/cape,
  pin/pine, cut/cute) is itself a hands-on discrimination activity.
- **Teacher-demo (name vs. sound contrast)**: the tutor models saying
  the letter's alphabet name, then the same letter's short sound inside
  a real word, back to back and clearly separated — directly
  instantiating the Blueprint's own name-vs-sound worked example
  (Component 5, WE-3-adjacent).
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming whether
  a minimal pair (TA-4) is short or long, ask the learner to predict
  from the spelling alone, before pronouncing — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**Direct instruction wins here for the base short-vowel sounds; a
narrower discovery step suits the closed-vs-silent-e distinction.** The
five short vowel sounds themselves are pure convention (there is no
regularity to discover in why "a" sounds the way it does) and are
correctly taught by direct demonstration via the keyword anchors,
matching this concept's sibling `eng.phonics.consonants`'s reasoning for
its base 18-consonant set. The closed-vs-silent-e structural rule
(TA-4), however, is a genuine, statable regularity worth a brief guided
discovery: **need** (the learner meets "cape" immediately after
successfully reading "cap," producing a real surprise at the different
vowel sound); **playground** (several minimal pairs to examine
side-by-side); **invention** (the learner is guided to notice the
extra "e" is the only spelling difference and to propose it as the
cause); **collision/formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of the
rule); **compression** (TA-5's fluent reading of novel CVC words without
re-deriving the rule each time). This is a smaller-scope discovery
design than `eng.phonics.consonants`'s hard/soft rule, appropriately
scaled to the single structural fact being taught.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (isolated short-vowel production) must precede TA-2 (short vowel in
CVC words) because producing a sound on cue from a keyword anchor is
easier than extracting and applying it within the context of a full
word. TA-3 (name vs. sound contrast) is placed immediately after, before
TA-4's silent-e work, because the name/sound confusion is the MORE
fundamental and more universally-present failure (rooted in near-
universal alphabet-song instruction) and must be resolved before the
learner can even reliably identify what "the short vowel sound" refers
to, which the silent-e distinction (TA-4) then builds on top of. TA-4
(closed vs. silent-e discrimination) is deliberately scoped narrowly — 
per the Blueprint's own Component 8 Adaptive Flag, this concept
introduces the silent-e CONTRAST only to the extent needed to correctly
bound "short vowel," leaving the full long-vowel silent-e system to the
downstream `eng.phonics.long-vowels-silent-e` concept; over-teaching
silent-e mechanics here would duplicate that concept's own content. TA-5
(novel CVC reading) is placed last as the integration and generalization
check, requiring both the base short-vowel sounds and the closed-
syllable boundary check to operate together fluently. This entry does
not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script
(Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

only a felt sense of correctness.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner's short-vowel production
  matches the SOUND or drifts toward the letter's alphabet NAME — this
  is the single most diagnostic thing to listen for at this node, more
  informative than whether the word is ultimately read correctly (a
  learner can sometimes self-correct a name-for-sound slip mid-word).
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a pause specifically when meeting a
  silent-e word for the first time (rather than an ordinary CVC word)
  suggests the learner has noticed the extra letter and is genuinely
  processing the structural difference — a good sign, not a sign of
  struggle, distinct from a fast, confident WRONG reading of the same
  word as though the "e" were absent.
- **Pronunciation stakes**: real but bounded — short-vowel realization
  varies somewhat by dialect (the precise quality of /æ/ or /ɛ/ differs
  across English varieties), so judge against the learner's own natural
  pronunciation, not a single reference accent; the STRUCTURAL judgment
  (short vs. long, based on spelling) is what must be correct, not the
  exact phonetic quality.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "before you say the word, check — does
  it end in a silent e?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for the
  advanced model and should be modeled explicitly and repeated across
  sessions, per `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3`.

## Assessment Signals

- **Diagnostic (golden probe)**: the Blueprint's own name-vs-sound probe
  (MP-3-class: "what is the NAME of the letter 'o'? What SOUND does it
  make in 'dog'?") cleanly separates a learner still substituting the
  alphabet name from one who has already made the distinction, before
  deciding how much time TA-3 needs.
- **Guided practice → independent practice**: the Blueprint's WE-1 → WE-2
  → WE-3 → MP-1 through MP-5 fading ladder is reused directly
  (Components 5–6) — not re-authored here.
- **Mastery gate set** (`../../assessment/05-mastery-verification.md
  §3`): production (fresh isolated short-vowel sound, MP-1); new-surface
  (short vowel in a novel CVC word, MP-2); name/sound discrimination
  (MP-3); structural-boundary discrimination (a novel closed-vs-silent-e
  minimal pair, MP-4 — the strongest evidence the structural rule, not
  memorized word-pairs, is what generalizes); explanation (MP-5).
- **Interpretation of response pattern**: fast-and-correct on a fresh
  minimal pair confirms the structural check is automatic; slow-and-
  correct (a visible pause to check for a silent e) is an acceptable,
  even good, intermediate sign, distinct from `eng.phonics.rhyming`'s
  spelling-trap pattern in one respect — here the check IS spelling-
  based by design, so a deliberate spelling glance is correct process,
  not a shortcut to unlearn. Fast-and-WRONG on a silent-e word (reading
  it as though closed) is the dangerous quadrant — it signals the
  structural check is being skipped entirely, not merely applied
  carelessly.
- **Transfer items**: near (a fresh CVC word); far (a minimal pair using
  a short vowel not heavily rehearsed during teaching, e.g. /ʌ/, testing
  whether the structural rule generalizes beyond the most-practiced
  vowels); real-world (spotting a silent-e word on a sign or in a book
  the learner is already reading).

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a CVC reading task shrinks to the isolated-sound
  keyword anchor for that specific vowel (e.g., "what sound does 'a'
  make, like in apple?") rather than the whole word, rebuilding the
  single-sound base before returning to the word-level task.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: reading a word using
  the vowel's alphabet name rather than saying "I don't know" is more
  likely, per MC-VOWEL-LETTER-NAME-IS-THE-SOUND's mechanism — when this
  specific substitution appears, route directly to that misconception's
  recovery (the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence probe: "say the real
  word naturally — does the middle sound like the letter's name, or
  something shorter?") rather than treating it as a generic decoding
  error.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who visibly
  looks at the end of a word before reading it (checking for a silent
  e) is showing the correct structural-check behavior even on items
  where the final answer is wrong from an unrelated slip — this process
  should be praised explicitly, separate from correcting the outcome.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: mixed — the five isolated short-vowel sounds are
facts (fixed keyword-anchored pairings, standard spaced fact-review),
while the closed-vs-silent-e structural check is a procedure (a
checking habit applied to novel words, reviewed through continued
application rather than simple recall). Interleaving partners: mix
short-vowel and silent-e minimal pairs within the same practice set
(rather than blocking) so the learner cannot predict "short" or "long"
from position in the set rather than from the actual spelling check —
directly parallel to `eng.phonics.consonants`'s hard/soft interleaving
rationale.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: reading fresh, unpracticed CVC words using the correct short
  vowel sound.
- **Far**: the closed-vs-silent-e structural discipline transfers
  directly to `eng.phonics.long-vowels-silent-e`, which builds the full
  long-vowel system on top of the same boundary this concept first
  establishes narrowly.
- **Real-world**: any CVC word encountered in the learner's own reading
  or environment (signs, labels, simple books) is an immediate practice
  opportunity.
- **Expert-transfer**: fluent, automatic short/long vowel discrimination
  during silent reading, well past this node — the advanced model made
  unconscious.
