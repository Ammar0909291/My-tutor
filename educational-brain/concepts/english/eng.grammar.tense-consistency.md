# Tense Consistency — `eng.grammar.tense-consistency`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.tense-consistency` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Tense Consistency" —
  maintaining logically consistent verb tense across a sentence or
  passage, distinguishing genuine time-motivated tense shifts from
  arbitrary, unmotivated ones.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.past-tenses` AND
  `eng.grammar.future-tenses` — load-bearing because this is an
  analytical, proofreading-level skill (Bloom: analyze) built ENTIRELY
  on top of fluent tense-formation ability; a learner cannot judge
  whether a tense shift is motivated or unmotivated without already
  producing past and future forms fluently and effortlessly enough to
  free attention for the analytical judgment itself.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.active-and-passive-voice`,
  `eng.grammar.direct-and-indirect-speech`. Both downstream concepts
  require the learner to track and preserve correct tense while
  transforming a sentence's structure (voice or speech-reporting form) —
  a skill that presupposes the tense-awareness and consistency-checking
  discipline this concept installs.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: analyze · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Detect and correct unmotivated tense shifts — arbitrary tense
   switches within the same real-world time-frame with no justifying
   time-reference change.
2. Identify motivated tense shifts as correct — genuine tense changes
   that reflect a real change in the time being described.
3. Recognize historical-present narration (present tense used to narrate
   past or fictional events) as a legitimate, deliberate stylistic
   choice, not an automatic error.
4. Detect and correct inconsistent historical-present mixing — passages
   that randomly switch between historical present and past tense
   mid-narration without a time-reference reason.
5. Proofread a full, longer passage containing both motivated shifts
   (to be left alone) and unmotivated shifts (to be corrected),
   applying the timeline-check technique to distinguish them.

## Core Understanding

"Tense consistency" does NOT mean using one single tense throughout a
passage — it means using tenses that logically and consistently reflect
the ACTUAL time relationships among the events being described. A
MOTIVATED shift genuinely reflects a real change in time ("She graduated
last year; next year she will start medical school" — two genuinely
different times, so two different tenses are correct and necessary). An
UNMOTIVATED shift switches tense arbitrarily within the SAME real-world
time-frame, with no time-reference justification ("He picked up the
phone and calls his mother" — both actions are in the same past scene,
so "calls" is a genuine error and should be "called"). A second,
independent load-bearing fact is that past tense is not the only valid
narrative tense: historical present (narrating past or fictional events
in present tense, "So I walk into the room, and everyone stares at me")
is a legitimate, deliberate stylistic choice used in storytelling,
sports commentary, and some literary styles — the actual requirement is
CONSISTENCY once that choice is made, not that present-tense narration
is inherently wrong. The concept's core analytical skill is judging
"motivated vs. unmotivated," not counting whether words match.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "every verb in a passage must be the exact same
  tense; any shift is automatically an error, and stories must always
  be past tense"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept
  exists to correct — a natural, reasonable simplification of
  "consistency" as literal word-form matching, and of "narrative" as
  synonymous with "past tense" since past tense is by far the most
  commonly encountered narrative default in early instruction.
- **Intermediate model — "I distinguish motivated shifts (correct,
  reflecting real time changes) from unmotivated shifts (errors, same
  time-frame)"**: installed by TA-1 and TA-2, using the Component 3
  timeline-check anchor, resolving
  MC-ALL-VERBS-IN-A-PASSAGE-MUST-MATCH-EXACTLY. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly leaving a genuinely motivated shift uncorrected in a fresh
  passage, rather than "fixing" it out of habit.
- **Advanced model — "I recognize historical present as a valid,
  deliberate stylistic narrative choice"**: installed by TA-3, resolving
  MC-NARRATIVE-TENSE-CANT-BE-PRESENT by directly validating present-
  tense narration as a real option, not a lesser or incorrect one.
- **Expert model — "I detect inconsistent historical-present mixing, and
  I proofread full passages combining both misconception-relevant
  judgments"**: installed by TA-4 and TA-5, the concept's genuine
  mastery bar — applying both the motivated/unmotivated judgment and the
  historical-present-consistency judgment together across a realistic,
  longer passage.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 has different or looser tense-consistency
  conventions, or doesn't require the same past/present narrative
  distinctions, this analytical, metalinguistic skill should not be
  introduced until past/future tense FORMATION is genuinely fluent and
  effortless — attempting the consistency judgment on top of still-
  effortful tense production risks failure attributable to formation
  gaps, not analytical weakness.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
simplifications given typical early instruction. The all-verbs-must-
match failure exists because "consistency" is, quite reasonably, first
understood as literal sameness — nothing in early tense instruction
marks the distinction between a genuine time-reference change (which
correctly demands a tense shift) and an arbitrary switch within the same
scene (which doesn't), until a learner is directly shown a passage where
"fixing" a motivated shift would actually break the sentence's correct
meaning. The narrative-must-be-past failure exists because past tense
genuinely IS the overwhelmingly dominant narrative default a learner
meets in early reading and writing instruction — nothing marks present-
tense narration as a real, accepted stylistic register until a learner
is directly shown a natural example (a storyteller's "so I walk into the
room...", or sports commentary's "he shoots, he scores!") and asked
whether it's actually ungrammatical or simply a different, valid choice.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.tense-consistency.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full
trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content — reused by
reference below, with birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-ALL-VERBS-IN-A-PASSAGE-MUST-MATCH-EXACTLY (Blueprint Component 1)
— Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — "consistency"
  is reasonably first understood as literal word-form sameness, and
  early tense instruction gives no explicit signal that a genuine time-
  reference change licenses, and requires, a tense shift; the
  misconception survives because most early sentences a learner
  practices with genuinely DO stay in one tense throughout, giving no
  counter-evidence until a passage spanning multiple real times is
  directly presented.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a
  passage with a genuinely motivated tense shift, spontaneously says
  something like "that's fine, because it's talking about a different
  time" before being told is showing the motivated/unmotivated
  distinction has been genuinely internalized, not merely applied to
  the specific TA-1/TA-2 practice items.

**MC-NARRATIVE-TENSE-CANT-BE-PRESENT (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — past tense is
  overwhelmingly the dominant narrative default a learner meets in early
  reading and writing instruction, so extending "narratives are past
  tense" to "narratives MUST be past tense" is a reasonable
  generalization until directly shown a natural, accepted counter-
  example (storytelling, sports commentary) using present tense
  consistently and successfully.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when asked whether
  a fresh historical-present passage is grammatically wrong, correctly
  identifies it as a valid stylistic choice (checking only for internal
  consistency, not tense choice itself) is showing the historical-
  present legitimacy has generalized beyond the specific TA-3 examples.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the timeline-check proofread itself is the analogy,
  not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): plotting each verb's
  action on a simple timeline and asking "do these two actions happen at
  the same real-world time, or different times?" directly converts the
  abstract motivated/unmotivated judgment into a concrete, checkable
  visual process, contrasted directly against a genuinely motivated-
  shift example. Breaking point: the timeline plot handles the
  motivated/unmotivated judgment well but doesn't on its own address the
  historical-present legitimacy question (TA-3), which requires its own
  separate demonstration and validation.
- **Alternative — a courtroom timeline exhibit vs. a rambling,
  contradictory witness**: a well-constructed courtroom timeline
  correctly places events in their true chronological relationship, even
  when multiple different times are involved (parallels motivated
  shifts); a witness who randomly jumps between describing an event as
  happening and having happened, with no logical reason, sounds
  unreliable and confusing (parallels unmotivated shifts). Breaking
  point: useful for the motivated/unmotivated contrast, but doesn't
  address the historical-present-as-valid-choice insight, which needs
  its own separate case.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe historical-present narration as
  "informal" or "less correct" writing than past-tense narration**: this
  framing risks reinforcing MC-NARRATIVE-TENSE-CANT-BE-PRESENT by
  implying present-tense narration is a lesser, degraded register rather
  than a genuine, deliberate stylistic choice used in serious literary
  fiction and professional sports commentary alike; both narrative
  tenses are equally legitimate, differing in effect (immediacy vs.
  retrospection), not in correctness.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the timeline-check proofread)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any piece of the learner's own past writing
  supplies real TA-1/TA-5 proofreading material; any spoken or written
  story the learner has heard or read supplies real TA-3 historical-
  present recognition material (a friend's informally-told anecdote, a
  sports broadcast).
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-5's full-passage proofreading is
  inherently hands-on, requiring the learner to apply both judgments
  (motivated/unmotivated AND historical-present consistency)
  simultaneously across continuous text, not merely recognize an
  isolated stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the same-scene test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether two
  actions in a sentence like "He picked up the phone and calls his
  mother" genuinely happen at different real-world times, making the
  unmotivated-shift judgment directly testable rather than an abstract
  rule.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  tense-consistency judgment (TA-1 through TA-5), ask the learner to
  state their own attempt (error or no error, and why) before the tutor
  confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
motivated/unmotivated distinction and the historical-present legitimacy
are both genuine, directly testable analytical judgments well-suited to
guided discovery: **need** (the learner, presented with a passage
spanning both a past graduation and a future exam, is asked whether
both actions should use identical tense, discovering that forcing
sameness would actually distort the meaning); **playground/invention**
(TA-1's unmotivated-shift detection and TA-2's motivated-shift
confirmation build initial confidence with both directions of the
judgment); **collision** (the Blueprint's own "picked up the phone and
calls" probe breaks the all-verbs-must-match assumption; a natural
storytelling or sports-commentary example breaks the narrative-must-be-
past assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of both
the motivated/unmotivated distinction and the historical-present
legitimacy); **compression** (TA-5's fluent full-passage proofreading,
applying both judgments together without each instance separately
re-derived from first principles). The specific terms "motivated shift,"
"unmotivated shift," and "historical present" are convention-bound
grammatical terminology best introduced by direct instruction once the
learner has already felt the underlying distinctions through the
timeline-check anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (detecting unmotivated tense shifts) is placed first as the
concept's clearest, most unambiguous entry point, per the Blueprint's
own timeline-check anchor — directly targeting
MC-ALL-VERBS-IN-A-PASSAGE-MUST-MATCH-EXACTLY by first confirming the
learner understands WHY a shift is wrong (unmotivated), not merely that
it "looks different." TA-2 (identifying motivated, correct shifts)
follows immediately, completing the same analytical dimension from its
other direction — confirming a genuine time-reference change correctly
licenses a tense shift. TA-3 (historical present as a valid stylistic
choice) introduces a SECOND, independent dimension (narrative-tense
legitimacy, not motivated/unmotivated judgment), directly targeting
MC-NARRATIVE-TENSE-CANT-BE-PRESENT, once the base consistency judgment
is secure. TA-4 (detecting inconsistent historical-present mixing)
extends TA-3 by applying the now-secure consistency-checking skill from
TA-1/TA-2 to the historical-present register specifically. TA-5
(full-passage tense-consistency proofreading) is placed last as the
concept's integration capstone, combining both analytical dimensions
(motivated/unmotivated AND historical-present consistency) across a
single, realistic, longer passage. This entry does not restate the
Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see
Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously justifies a
  tense-consistency judgment by reference to actual time relationships
  ("that's fine because it's a different time" / "that's wrong because
  it's the same scene") rather than by surface word-form matching alone
  — this is the primary diagnostic signal for whether TA-1/TA-2's
  analytical distinction has genuinely generalized.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before judging a
  borderline shift, followed by a correct, time-reference-grounded
  justification, suggests the learner is consciously applying the
  timeline-check process rather than pattern-matching on surface form —
  a good sign. Instant, confident "fixing" of a genuinely motivated
  shift (without checking the actual time relationship) is the
  concerning pattern for
  MC-ALL-VERBS-IN-A-PASSAGE-MUST-MATCH-EXACTLY.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags — for S9 learners, this analytical
  consistency-checking skill should not be introduced until past/future
  tense FORMATION is genuinely fluent, since the analytical judgment
  depends heavily on effortless tense production as a foundation;
  attempting it too early risks misattributing formation gaps to
  analytical weakness.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "do these two actions happen at the
  same real time, or different times?" is this concept's load-bearing
  sentence for
  MC-ALL-VERBS-IN-A-PASSAGE-MUST-MATCH-EXACTLY, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh tense-shift judgment is required, making the
  timeline-check the routinely-invited first response rather than an
  afterthought.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Concept-specific application of `../../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`:
- **"I don't know"** on a tense-consistency judgment task shrinks to the
  Component 3 timeline-check anchor using the simplest, most clearly
  unmotivated example available, with the tutor modeling the timeline-
  plotting process before the learner attempts a fresh, more ambiguous
  passage independently.
- **Likeliest utterance/behavior at this node**: for
  MC-ALL-VERBS-IN-A-PASSAGE-MUST-MATCH-EXACTLY, "correcting" a
  genuinely motivated shift is more likely than "I don't know" — route
  directly to the Blueprint's own conflict-evidence probe rather than
  treating it as a generic tense-formation gap. For
  MC-NARRATIVE-TENSE-CANT-BE-PRESENT, flagging a valid historical-
  present passage as an error is more likely than confusion — route
  directly to TA-3's stylistic-legitimacy case.
- **Non-verbal signal specific to this node**: a learner who
  spontaneously pauses before "fixing" an ambiguous shift and instead
  asks "wait, is this actually a different time?" before being told is
  showing the motivated/unmotivated discipline has generalized — this
  should be named and praised explicitly, per the Blueprint's own
  regulation-tail guidance (praising checking for motivated vs.
  unmotivated shifts over assuming all shifts are errors).

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedural/analytical — a judgment-application skill
(motivated vs. unmotivated tense shift; historical-present consistency)
applied to genuinely novel passages, not a fixed fact set; mastery is
demonstrated by correctly judging fresh, previously-unseen passages
(MP-1 through MP-4) rather than by recognizing a fixed, previously-
taught example list. Interleaving partners: mix motivated-shift items
(testing TA-2, where "no error" is the correct answer) with unmotivated-
shift items (testing TA-1/TA-4, where correction is required) in ongoing
review, since a learner who only ever practices finding errors risks
over-correcting genuinely valid shifts.

- **Near**: proofreading fresh passages for unmotivated tense shifts and
  inconsistent historical-present mixing; correctly leaving fresh
  motivated shifts and consistent historical-present narration
  uncorrected.
- **Far**: this concept's tense-tracking-through-transformation
  discipline is the direct prerequisite for
  `eng.grammar.active-and-passive-voice`'s and
  `eng.grammar.direct-and-indirect-speech`'s shared requirement to
  preserve the correct tense while restructuring a sentence's voice or
  speech-reporting form.
- **Real-world**: every piece of extended writing the learner produces —
  essays, stories, reports, emails describing events across multiple
  times — offers immediate, ungated opportunities to apply correct
  tense-consistency proofreading.
- **Expert-transfer**: polished, professional-quality writing at any
  advanced level depends on the ability to proofread one's own extended
  text for genuine tense errors without over-correcting legitimate,
  meaning-carrying tense shifts — a discrimination skill that remains
  relevant at every proficiency level, including native-speaker
  academic and professional writing.
