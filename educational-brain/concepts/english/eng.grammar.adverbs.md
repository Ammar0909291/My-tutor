# Adverbs — `eng.grammar.adverbs`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.adverbs` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Adverbs" — words that
  modify verbs, adjectives, or other adverbs, typically expressing
  manner, time, place, or degree.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.verbs` AND `eng.grammar.adjectives` —
  load-bearing on two independent fronts: verbs because adverbs of
  manner directly modify the verb, requiring a learner who can already
  identify the verb being modified; adjectives because two of this
  concept's five teaching actions (TA-3, TA-4) require the learner to
  distinguish adverbs from "-ly" adjectives and to recognize adverbs
  modifying adjectives, both of which depend on already having a
  reliable, independent sense of what an adjective is.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.comparatives-and-superlatives`.
  Adverbs and adjectives share the general modification-word structure
  and both take comparative/superlative degree forms (more quickly,
  most carefully vs. taller, tallest); the downstream concept formally
  extends the degree-modification pattern this concept's TA-4/TA-5
  introduce to both word classes together.
- **Difficulty**: foundational · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.80 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify adverbs of manner modifying verbs (quickly, quietly,
   carefully) and name the verb each one modifies.
2. Identify adverbs that do not end in "-ly" (fast, hard, well, soon,
   often, never) by function rather than by ending.
3. Distinguish "-ly" words that are actually adjectives (friendly,
   lovely, lonely, silly) from true adverbs, using the function test.
4. Identify degree adverbs modifying adjectives (very tall, extremely
   difficult) and name the adjective each one modifies.
5. Identify degree adverbs modifying other adverbs (quite quickly, too
   slowly), completing the full three-target modification pattern.

## Core Understanding

An adverb is defined by what it MODIFIES, not by how it's spelled — this
is the single most load-bearing fact in the whole concept. The "-ly"
ending is a common, useful first clue, but it is neither necessary nor
sufficient: many genuine adverbs don't end in "-ly" at all (fast, hard,
well, soon, often, never, very), and several "-ly" words are actually
ADJECTIVES describing nouns, not verbs (friendly, lovely, lonely, silly
— "a FRIENDLY dog" describes the dog, a noun, making "friendly" an
adjective despite its ending). The reliable test is always: what does
this word actually modify? A second, equally load-bearing fact is that
adverbs modify THREE different kinds of words, not just one: verbs
("she runs quickly" — manner), adjectives ("very tall" — degree), and
other adverbs ("quite quickly" — degree). When an adverb modifies an
adjective or another adverb, it is almost always showing DEGREE or
INTENSITY (very, extremely, quite, too, so, rather), a genuinely
different job from the manner/time/place adverbs a learner typically
meets first.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "adverbs end in '-ly' and only describe verbs"**:
  the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct
  — a natural default because "-ly" IS the most commonly taught,
  visually salient adverb marker, and because verb-modifying (manner)
  adverbs are typically the first, most concrete examples a learner
  meets, giving no signal that either assumption has real exceptions.
- **Intermediate model — "I identify adverbs by checking what they
  modify, not by their ending, and I recognize '-ly' adjectives as a
  named exception category"**: installed by TA-1 through TA-3, using
  the Component 3 three-target modifier-toss anchor, resolving
  MC-ALL-ADVERBS-END-IN-LY. Upgrade trigger: correctly identifying a
  fresh non-"-ly" adverb and a fresh "-ly" adjective without hesitation,
  in the same practice set.
- **Advanced model — "I recognize adverbs modifying adjectives, showing
  degree"**: installed by TA-4, extending the concept beyond verb-
  modification, beginning to resolve
  MC-ADVERBS-ONLY-MODIFY-VERBS.
- **Expert model — "I recognize adverbs modifying other adverbs,
  completing the full three-target modification system"**: installed by
  TA-5, the concept's genuine mastery bar — fully resolving
  MC-ADVERBS-ONLY-MODIFY-VERBS by demonstrating all three modification
  targets (verb, adjective, adverb) as equally legitimate, commonly-
  occurring adverb functions.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 doesn't morphologically distinguish adverbs from
  adjectives, or has a very different degree-modification system,
  expect the three-way modification pattern and the adjective-vs-
  adverb-FORM distinction (e.g., "real happy" vs. "really happy") to
  require dedicated, explicit, contrastive practice — this is a well-
  documented, persistent error area for many L2 English learners, and
  often for native speakers in casual registers too.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here. The all-adverbs-end-
in-ly failure exists because "-ly" genuinely IS the most commonly
taught, most visually salient adverb marker a learner meets — over-
relying on it is a reasonable extension of a real, useful pattern, with
nothing marking either direction of exception (non-"-ly" adverbs like
"fast," or "-ly" adjectives like "friendly") until both are directly
contrasted against the ending-based rule. The adverbs-only-modify-verbs
failure exists because manner adverbs modifying verbs are typically the
first, most concrete, most visually demonstrable examples a learner
meets ("she ran quickly" is easy to act out; "very tall" describing an
adjective's intensity is more abstract) — nothing marks degree-adverb
modification of adjectives and other adverbs as an equally legitimate,
equally common adverb function until a learner is directly shown a
sentence like "she is very tall" and asked what "very" is actually
describing.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/

(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-ALL-ADVERBS-END-IN-LY (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — "-ly" genuinely
  IS the most common, most commonly taught adverb marker, so over-
  relying on it is a reasonable extension of real, useful partial
  evidence rather than a wholesale error; the misconception survives
  because the "-ly" pattern genuinely works correctly for the majority
  of adverbs a learner first encounters.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when meeting a
  fresh word like "friendly," spontaneously checks what it modifies
  before classifying it (rather than classifying it as an adverb purely
  because of its ending) is showing the function-over-ending test has
  been genuinely internalized.

**MC-ADVERBS-ONLY-MODIFY-VERBS (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — manner adverbs
  modifying verbs are typically the first, most concrete, most easily
  demonstrated examples a learner meets, so extending "adverbs describe
  actions" to "adverbs ONLY describe actions" is a reasonable
  generalization until directly shown a degree-adverb example
  modifying an adjective or another adverb.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when meeting a
  fresh degree-adverb sentence like "she is extremely talented,"
  spontaneously identifies "extremely" as modifying the adjective
  "talented" (not the verb "is") before being told is showing the
  three-target modification pattern has generalized beyond the specific
  TA-4/TA-5 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the three-target modifier toss itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically
  tossing a beanbag at the "Verb," "Adjective," or "Adverb" target
  corresponding to what a given modifying word actually modifies
  directly embodies the three-way modification pattern as a concrete,
  checkable physical action rather than an abstract classification
  rule. Breaking point: the toss anchor demonstrates the three-target
  pattern well but doesn't on its own address the "-ly"-ending trap
  (TA-2/TA-3), which needs its own separate ending-vs-function
  demonstration.
- **Alternative — a job title vs. a job description**: "-ly" is like a
  common uniform many adverbs happen to wear, but the uniform doesn't
  determine the job — what the word actually DOES (modifies a verb,
  adjective, or adverb) is the real job description, and some workers
  wearing the "-ly" uniform (friendly, lovely) actually have a
  different job (describing nouns, i.e., being adjectives). Breaking
  point: useful for the ending-vs-function distinction, but doesn't
  address the three-target modification pattern, which the toss anchor
  handles directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe the "-ly" ending as "the adverb
  rule" even as a simplified teaching shortcut**: framing "-ly" as THE
  rule, even informally, directly reinforces
  MC-ALL-ADVERBS-END-IN-LY and sets up a later, harder-to-unlearn
  correction; from the very first example, frame "-ly" as "a common
  clue, not the test."

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the three-target modifier toss)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any action the learner performs (walking,
  speaking, working) supplies real TA-1/TA-2 manner-adverb material by
  simply asking "how are you doing that?"; any description of an
  object's quality supplies real TA-4 degree-adverb material by asking
  "how tall/difficult/good?"
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-3's "-ly"-adjective sorting task
  (correctly classifying friendly, lovely, lonely, silly as adjectives
  despite their ending) is inherently hands-on discrimination practice,
  requiring active function-checking, not mere recognition of a stated
  fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the modifies-what test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking, for "a
  friendly dog," whether "friendly" describes an action or the noun
  "dog," making the function-over-ending test directly audible and
  testable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming an
  adverb identification or modification-target attempt (TA-1 through
  TA-5), ask the learner to state their own attempt before the tutor
  confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
function-over-ending test and the three-target modification pattern are
both genuine, directly testable phenomena well-suited to guided
discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured TA-1's manner-adverb
practice, is asked whether "friendly" in "a friendly dog" is an adverb
since it ends in "-ly," discovering the answer feels wrong once tested
against what it modifies); **playground/invention** (TA-1's manner-
adverb practice and TA-2's non-"-ly"-adverb practice build initial
confidence with both directions of the ending-independence insight);
**collision** (the Blueprint's own "friendly dog" probe breaks the
all-adverbs-end-in-ly assumption; the "she is VERY tall" probe breaks
the adverbs-only-modify-verbs assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's
own-words statement of both the function-over-ending test and the
three-target modification pattern); **compression** (TA-5's fluent
identification of adverb-modifying-adverb relationships, completing the
full three-target system without each instance separately re-derived).
The specific terms "adverb of manner," "degree adverb," and "modifies"
are convention-bound grammatical terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying
distinctions through the modifier-toss anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (adverbs modifying verbs, manner) is placed first as the concept's
most concrete, most easily demonstrated entry point, per the Blueprint's
own modifier-toss anchor — establishing the clearest adverb function
before any ending-based or multi-target complication is introduced.
TA-2 (non-"-ly" adverbs) follows immediately, directly targeting
MC-ALL-ADVERBS-END-IN-LY from one direction (adverbs that lack the
expected ending). TA-3 ("-ly" adjectives) completes the same
misconception target from the other direction (words with the ending
that aren't adverbs at all) — per the Blueprint's own session
architecture, this conflict-evidence probe is placed right after TA-2,
once the learner has secure non-"-ly"-adverb practice to contrast the
adjective exceptions against. TA-4 (adverbs modifying adjectives)
introduces a SECOND, independent dimension (multi-target modification,
not ending reliability), directly targeting
MC-ADVERBS-ONLY-MODIFY-VERBS, once the base function-over-ending test is
secure. TA-5 (adverbs modifying other adverbs) is placed last as the
concept's integration capstone, completing the full three-target
modification pattern (verb, adjective, adverb). This entry does not
restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) —
see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously checks what
  a word modifies (rather than its ending) when classifying a fresh
  "-ly" word, and whether the learner spontaneously identifies a degree
  adverb as modifying an adjective or another adverb (rather than
  defaulting to "modifies the verb") — these are the primary diagnostic
  signals for whether TA-2/TA-3 and TA-4/TA-5's disciplines have
  genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before classifying a
  fresh "-ly" word, followed by a correct, function-based
  classification, suggests the learner is consciously checking what the
  word modifies rather than pattern-matching on the ending — a good
  sign. Instant, confident classification of any "-ly" word as an
  adverb (without checking function) is the concerning pattern for
  MC-ALL-ADVERBS-END-IN-LY.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Protocol Routing — for S9 learners whose L1 doesn't
  morphologically distinguish adverbs from adjectives, reassure that
  the three-way modification pattern and the "real happy" vs. "really
  happy" form distinction are well-documented, persistent challenge
  areas warranting dedicated contrastive practice, not a sign of weak
  general English ability.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "what is that word actually
  describing?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for
  MC-ALL-ADVERBS-END-IN-LY, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh potentially-ambiguous "-ly" word is introduced,
  making the function check a routinely-invited response rather than an
  afterthought.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework — a function-based classification
system (three modification targets: verb, adjective, adverb) combined
with an explicit exception category ("-ly" adjectives), applied to
genuinely novel words in context; mastery is demonstrated by correctly
classifying fresh, previously-unseen words by function (MP-2 through
MP-4) rather than by recognizing a fixed, previously-taught example
list. Interleaving partners: mix manner-adverb items (testing TA-1)
with ending-exception items (testing TA-2/TA-3) and degree-adverb items
(testing TA-4/TA-5) in ongoing review, so the learner continues
exercising all three distinct dimensions as separately-verified
competencies.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: identifying fresh adverbs across all three modification
  targets (verb, adjective, adverb); correctly discriminating fresh
  "-ly" adjectives from true adverbs by function.
- **Far**: this concept's modification-word framework is the direct
  foundation for `eng.grammar.comparatives-and-superlatives`'s formal
  extension of degree-modification to comparative and superlative
  forms, applying to both adjectives and the adverbs this concept
  installs.
- **Real-world**: every description of manner, time, place, or degree
  the learner produces — in speech or writing — offers immediate,
  ungated opportunities to apply correct adverb identification and use.
- **Expert-transfer**: precise, vivid descriptive and narrative writing
  at any advanced level depends on fluent command of all three
  modification targets, since restricting adverb use to verb-modifying
  manner alone (or misusing adjective forms where adverbs are required,
  e.g. "she sings good") remains a noticeable marker of less
  sophisticated production even at otherwise advanced proficiency.
