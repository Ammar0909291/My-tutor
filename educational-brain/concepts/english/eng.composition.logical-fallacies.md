## Learning Objective
The learner will name and detect three common reasoning flaws — ad hominem, straw man, and false dilemma — in real arguments, and distinguish a genuine fallacy from a legitimate criticism that merely resembles one on the surface.

## Core Understanding
A logical fallacy is a pattern of reasoning that fails regardless of whether its conclusion happens to be true — the flaw is in the STRUCTURE of the argument's support, not in the popularity or correctness of its final claim. This is the single hardest thing to internalize: a true conclusion reached by fallacious reasoning is still a fallacious argument, and a false conclusion reached by valid reasoning from false premises is a different kind of problem entirely. The three named fallacies here are: (1) ad hominem — attacking the arguer's character/circumstances instead of their argument's content; (2) straw man — misrepresenting an opponent's position to make it easier to attack; (3) false dilemma — presenting only two options when more exist. Each has a legitimate look-alike that is NOT fallacious, and distinguishing them is the actual skill.

## Mental Models
- **Lottery-Ticket-and-Relevant-Witness Anchor**: A lottery ticket's winning depends on the actual numbers drawn, not on how confident or well-dressed the person holding it is — likewise, an argument's validity depends on its structure, not on who's making it or how the conclusion "feels." A witness's credibility (e.g., a convicted perjurer testifying about a crime) IS relevant to whether to trust their testimony — this is the anchor for the ad-hominem look-alike: attacking credibility about a fact-claim is legitimate; attacking character as if it disproves an argument's logic is not.
- **Two-Option Trap**: false dilemma is visualized as a fork with only two branches painted on, when the real terrain has a third (or fourth) path the arguer didn't draw.

## Why Students Fail
Learners conflate "this conclusion is true/popular" with "this reasoning is valid," so a fallacious argument that reaches an agreeable conclusion doesn't register as fallacious. They also over-apply "ad hominem" to any personal criticism, missing that legitimate credibility challenges (a source's documented bias, a witness's criminal record on a related charge) are not automatically fallacious.

## Misconceptions
- **MC-A (Type 1, overgeneralization from "fallacies produce wrong conclusions")**: "If the conclusion is true or something I agree with, the argument can't be fallacious." Diagnostic: present a fallacious argument with a true conclusion (e.g., ad hominem attack on a scientist whose finding is nonetheless correct) and ask if the ARGUMENT is fallacious. Repair: separate "is this conclusion true" from "does this argument's structure support its conclusion" as two independent questions asked in sequence, every time.
- **MC-B (Type 1, overgeneralization from "criticizing a person = ad hominem")**: "Any criticism of a person mentioned in an argument is an ad hominem fallacy." Diagnostic: present a case where a source's documented, relevant bias or credibility issue is raised (e.g., "this study was funded by the company whose product it tested") and ask if that's a fallacy. Repair: the Lottery-Ticket-and-Relevant-Witness anchor — ask "is this attack aimed at whether the ARGUMENT'S LOGIC holds, or at a genuinely relevant fact about trustworthiness on this specific claim?"

## Analogies
- The lottery-ticket/relevant-witness anchor (above).
- A courtroom: attacking a witness's known perjury conviction on a related matter is legitimate impeachment; attacking their haircut is not — the line is relevance to the claim at hand, not "any personal comment is off-limits."
- Anti-analogy: fallacy-spotting is not "any argument I disagree with is fallacious" — that itself is close to a fallacy (motivated reasoning), and the tutor should flag this if a learner starts labeling opposing views "fallacies" reflexively.

## Demonstrations
- Present a real or constructed argument with an ad hominem attack that happens to reach a true conclusion; ask the learner to evaluate the ARGUMENT separately from the CONCLUSION.
- Present two personal-criticism examples side by side — one a legitimate relevance-based credibility challenge, one a true ad hominem — and have the learner sort them with reasons.
- Present a false dilemma ("either we ban it completely or allow total chaos") and have the learner name the missing middle options.

## Discovery Questions
- "Is this argument fallacious because of its LOGIC, or because you disagree with where it ends up?"
- "Is this criticism attacking whether the argument holds together, or a fact that's actually relevant to trusting the source on this specific claim?"
- "How many options does this argument admit exist? How many actually exist?"

## Teaching Sequence
1. Anchor: lottery-ticket-and-relevant-witness framing — validity is structural, not about the person or the popularity of the conclusion.
2. Introduce ad hominem with both a fallacious example and a legitimate-credibility-challenge look-alike side by side.
3. Introduce straw man, connecting explicitly to the prerequisite concept's counterargument-fairness discipline.
4. Introduce false dilemma with the two-option-trap visual.
5. Guided: learner sorts a mixed set of examples (fallacy vs. legitimate look-alike) for each of the three types.
6. Independent: learner finds one real example of any of the three fallacies in a source of their choosing and explains the structural flaw.
7. Confirm: learner explains why "true conclusion" doesn't rescue a fallacious argument.

## Tutor Actions
- SHOW: paired fallacy/legitimate-look-alike examples for each of the three named fallacies.
- TEST-THINKING: ask the learner to evaluate argument structure and conclusion truth as two separate questions.
- ERROR ANALYSIS: give a passage with an embedded fallacy and have the learner locate and name it.
- SOCIAL/DEBATE: have the learner attempt to construct a legitimate credibility challenge that is NOT ad hominem, to test the boundary from the inside.

## Voice Teaching Notes
When a learner says "that's just a fallacy" too quickly about an argument they simply disagree with, slow down and ask them to name which of the three structural patterns is present — reflexive fallacy-labeling of any disagreeable argument is itself a red flag worth naming gently.

## Assessment Signals
- Probe: "This argument attacks the funding source of a study. Is that always ad hominem?" — correct answer notes relevance is the deciding factor.
- Probe: given an argument with a true conclusion reached via straw-manning the opposition, ask "is this argument valid?" — correct answer: no, regardless of the conclusion.
- Confident-wrong signature: quick, confident "yes that's a fallacy" labeling with no structural reasoning given — flag as reflexive labeling, distinct from either MC-A or MC-B.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy
If a learner keeps merging "I disagree" with "this is fallacious," don't argue the object-level claim — return to the lottery-ticket anchor and make them state the STRUCTURAL flaw (or lack of one) as a separate step before any conclusion-level judgment.

## Memory Hooks
"Lottery ticket wins on the numbers, not the holder" for structural validity vs. conclusion truth; "relevant witness vs. haircut" for the ad-hominem/legitimate-challenge boundary; "how many branches does the fork really have?" for false dilemma.

## Cross-Subject Connections
The structural-validity-vs-truth distinction mirrors formal logic's soundness-vs-validity split (a valid argument can have a false conclusion if a premise is false; a fallacious argument can have a true conclusion by accident) — useful bridge if the learner has any exposure to formal logic or debate.
