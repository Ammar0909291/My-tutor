# Run-On Sentences and Comma Splices — `eng.grammar.run-on-sentences-and-comma-splices`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.run-on-sentences-and-comma-splices`
  (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Run-On Sentences and
  Comma Splices" — errors where two independent clauses are incorrectly
  joined without proper punctuation or a conjunction, and how to
  correct them.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.compound-sentences` AND
  `eng.grammar.comma-usage` — load-bearing on two fronts: compound
  sentences because this concept is explicitly built as the ERROR-
  RECOGNITION counterpart to that concept's own CORRECT clause-joining
  methods (comma+conjunction, semicolon alone, period) — a learner
  cannot recognize a joining error without first securing what correct
  joining looks like; comma usage because the comma splice specifically
  IS the clause-joiner comma rule's comma used WITHOUT its required
  conjunction, a connection this concept's own Blueprint names as the
  direct reason it depends on comma usage specifically.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): none listed — this is a terminal node in the
  current KG's grammar domain, though it is a foundational error-
  recognition skill for clear writing.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: analyze · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify a true run-on sentence — two independent clauses joined
   with absolutely no punctuation or conjunction at all.
2. Identify a comma splice — two independent clauses joined with only a
   comma, no conjunction — correctly distinguishing it from a properly
   formed compound sentence.
3. Generate all three valid fixes for a given run-on or comma splice
   (adding a coordinating conjunction after a comma, using a semicolon,
   splitting into two sentences) and explain the different effect of
   each.
4. Select and justify the most contextually appropriate fix among the
   three valid options, based on the relationship between the two ideas
   and the desired rhythm.
5. Identify and correct a mix of true run-ons, comma splices, and
   correctly formed sentences within a full passage.

## Core Understanding

A run-on sentence and a comma splice are both errors where two
independent clauses are incorrectly joined — but they are distinct
errors, each defined precisely against the CORRECT joining methods
already secured in the prerequisite compound-sentences concept. A TRUE
RUN-ON joins two independent clauses with absolutely NO punctuation or
conjunction at all ("The test was hard I still passed it"). A COMMA
SPLICE joins two independent clauses with only a comma, no conjunction
— and this is the concept's single most load-bearing insight: a comma
ALONE is never sufficient to correctly join two independent clauses,
even though it FEELS like it's doing real connecting work. A comma
alone is like a single link of chain with no connecting clasp — it
cannot securely hold two independent clauses together without the
"clasp" of a coordinating conjunction (and, but, so, or, nor, for, yet)
following it. "The rain stopped, we went outside" is a comma splice — an
error — genuinely distinct from "The rain stopped, and we went outside,"
a correctly formed compound sentence. A second, independent, equally
load-bearing insight concerns FIXING these errors: there is no single
"correct" fix. THREE equally valid strategies exist — adding a
coordinating conjunction after the comma, replacing the comma with a
semicolon, or splitting into two separate sentences — each producing a
genuinely different rhythm and emphasis, the way sewing, fabric glue,
and a patch can all genuinely repair a torn seam, each with a different
look and durability. The best fix depends on the specific relationship
between the two ideas and the rhythm the writer wants, not a single
default strategy applied mechanically every time.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "a comma alone is enough to correctly join two
  independent clauses, and the only way to fix a run-on or splice is to
  split it into two separate sentences"**: the arriving, pre-
  instructional model this concept exists to correct — a natural
  default because a comma genuinely DOES appear to be doing some
  connecting work between the two clauses, making it feel sufficient on
  its own, and because splitting into two sentences is the most
  visually direct, immediately obvious repair strategy.
- **Intermediate model — "I identify true run-ons with no punctuation at
  all"**: installed by TA-1, establishing the most basic, unambiguous
  error type as the entry point.
- **Advanced model — "I identify comma splices as a comma alone with no
  conjunction, distinct from a correctly formed compound sentence"**:
  installed by TA-2, using the Component 3 chain-clasp anchor,
  resolving
  MC-A-A-COMMA-SPLICE-IS-JUST-AS-CORRECT-AS-A-COMPOUND-SENTENCE-SINCE-
  BOTH-USE-A-COMMA.
- **Expert model — "I generate and choose among all three valid fix
  strategies, and I identify and correct a mix of both error types
  across a full passage"**: installed by TA-3 and TA-4, the concept's
  genuine mastery bar, resolving
  MC-B-THE-ONLY-WAY-TO-FIX-A-RUN-ON-OR-COMMA-SPLICE-IS-TO-SPLIT-IT-INTO-
  TWO-SEPARATE-SENTENCES by requiring the learner to actively consider
  and compare multiple valid options rather than defaulting to one.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing
  (S1), MC-A generally needs resolving BEFORE MC-B, since a learner who
  cannot recognize a comma splice as an error has nothing yet to
  practice fixing correctly — the sequence matters, not just coverage
  of both misconceptions.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and the Blueprint
itself explicitly notes that resolving the first is a prerequisite for
meaningfully practicing the second. The comma-alone-is-enough failure
exists because a comma genuinely DOES appear to perform real connecting
work visually and rhythmically between two ideas — treating it as
sufficient on its own is a reasonable default absent explicit
instruction on the required coordinating-conjunction "clasp," with
nothing marking the error until a learner directly compares "The rain
stopped, we went outside" against "The rain stopped, and we went
outside" and is asked whether the comma alone is genuinely enough. The
only-fix-is-splitting failure exists because splitting into two
sentences is the single most visually direct, immediately obvious
repair a learner reaches for absent awareness of the alternatives —
nothing marks the semicolon and comma-plus-conjunction options as
equally valid, sometimes-superior fixes until a learner directly
generates and compares all three for the same sentence pair.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.run-on-sentences-and-comma-splices.md`, Component 1)
documents both misconceptions this concept produces, with full
trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content — reused by
reference below, with birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-A-A-COMMA-SPLICE-IS-JUST-AS-CORRECT-AS-A-COMPOUND-SENTENCE-SINCE-
BOTH-USE-A-COMMA (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature, and directly
  parallel to `eng.grammar.compound-sentences`'s own
  MC-A-SEMICOLON-AND-A-COMMA-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE-JOINERS — a comma
  genuinely does appear in the correct compound-sentence pattern
  (comma+conjunction), so extending "comma present" to "correctly
  joined" is a reasonable, incomplete generalization from real, partial
  evidence, omitting the required conjunction component.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a fresh
  comma-only join between two independent clauses, spontaneously
  identifies the missing conjunction that would need to be added is
  showing the comma-alone-is-insufficient discipline has been genuinely
  internalized.

**MC-B-THE-ONLY-WAY-TO-FIX-A-RUN-ON-OR-COMMA-SPLICE-IS-TO-SPLIT-IT-
INTO-TWO-SEPARATE-SENTENCES (Blueprint Component 1) — Type 1,
overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — splitting into
  two sentences genuinely IS one valid, always-correct fix, so
  defaulting to it exclusively is a reasonable extension of real,
  correct partial knowledge, until a learner directly generates and
  compares the semicolon and comma-plus-conjunction alternatives for
  the same sentence pair and notices each produces a genuinely
  different, equally valid effect.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when correcting a
  fresh run-on or splice, spontaneously generates more than one valid
  fix option and compares their rhythm before selecting one is showing
  the multiple-strategies discipline has generalized beyond the
  specific TA-3 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the combined Chain-Clasp and Torn-Seam-Repair anchor
  itself is the analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint
  Component 3): a chain link with no connecting clasp, unable to
  securely hold two heavy objects together on its own, directly seeds
  the comma-alone-is-insufficient fix; a torn fabric seam repairable by
  sewing, fabric glue, or a patch — each a valid fix with a different
  look — directly seeds the multiple-valid-fixes insight. Breaking
  point: the combined anchor demonstrates both core insights clearly
  but doesn't on its own train the full-passage synthesis task (TA-4),
  which needs its own separate, mixed-error-type practice set.
- **Alternative — a doorway with no door vs. a doorway with a door but
  no lock**: a true run-on is like a doorway with no door at all
  (nothing marking the boundary between the two spaces); a comma splice
  is like a doorway with a door but no lock (some marking present, but
  not securely closed). Breaking point: useful for distinguishing the
  two error types by degree, but doesn't address the multiple-fix-
  strategies insight, which the torn-seam anchor's own TA-3 practice
  handles directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe splitting into two sentences as "the
  safest fix" or "the default choice when unsure"**: this framing
  directly reinforces
  MC-B-THE-ONLY-WAY-TO-FIX-A-RUN-ON-OR-COMMA-SPLICE-IS-TO-SPLIT-IT-INTO-
  TWO-SEPARATE-SENTENCES by implying the other two fixes are riskier or
  secondary; per the Blueprint's own explicit framing, all three fixes
  are equally valid and correct, differing only in the rhythm and
  relationship-emphasis they convey, not in correctness or safety.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions, appropriate
to this concept's `analyze`-level Bloom classification.** The comma-
alone-is-insufficient insight and the multiple-valid-fixes insight are
both genuine, directly testable phenomena well-suited to guided
discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured TA-1's true-run-on
detection, is shown "The rain stopped, we went outside" and asked
whether the comma alone correctly joins the two ideas, discovering
their instinct feels wrong once compared against the conjunction-
containing version); **playground/invention** (TA-1's true-run-on
practice and TA-2's comma-splice practice build initial confidence with
error-type recognition before the fixing-strategy complication is
introduced); **collision** (the Blueprint's own comma-splice-vs-
compound-sentence pair breaks the comma-alone-is-enough assumption; the
three-fixes-for-one-run-on comparison breaks the only-splitting-works
assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's full passage correction task,
requiring articulation of both error types and multiple fix
strategies); **compression** (TA-4's fluent full-passage synthesis,
applying both disciplines directly without each instance separately
re-derived). The specific terms "run-on" and "comma splice" are
convention-bound grammatical terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying
distinctions through the anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (identifying true run-ons) is placed first as the concept's most
basic, unambiguous entry point, per the Blueprint's own session
architecture — establishing the no-punctuation-at-all error before the
subtler comma-splice case is introduced. TA-2 (identifying comma
splices) directly targets
MC-A-A-COMMA-SPLICE-IS-JUST-AS-CORRECT-AS-A-COMPOUND-SENTENCE-SINCE-
BOTH-USE-A-COMMA, using the chain-clasp anchor. TA-3 (the three valid
fix strategies) directly targets
MC-B-THE-ONLY-WAY-TO-FIX-A-RUN-ON-OR-COMMA-SPLICE-IS-TO-SPLIT-IT-INTO-
TWO-SEPARATE-SENTENCES, addressing a SECOND, independent dimension
(fixing strategy, not error-type identification) — per the Blueprint's
own Protocol Routing, this sequencing is deliberate: MC-A generally
needs resolving before MC-B, since a learner who cannot recognize a
comma splice as an error has nothing yet to practice fixing correctly.
TA-4 (full application: identifying and correcting errors in context)
is placed last as the concept's integration capstone, requiring the
learner to apply both error-type recognition and contextual fix
selection across a realistic mixed passage. This entry does not restate
the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see
Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously identifies
  the missing conjunction in a fresh comma-only join (rather than
  accepting the comma alone as sufficient), and whether the learner
  spontaneously generates more than one valid fix for a fresh run-on or
  splice before selecting one — these are the primary diagnostic
  signals for whether TA-2 and TA-3's disciplines have genuinely
  generalized beyond the specific practice items.

  BOTH-USE-A-COMMA.

  S9-specific routing beyond standard practice depth is called for. The
  Blueprint instead flags a persistent real-world pattern worth noting:
  per its own Adaptive Flags, comma splices remain a very common error
  even among otherwise skilled writers, warranting targeted, ongoing

- **The load-bearing sentence**: "is the comma alone actually enough,
