# Suffixes — `eng.vocab.suffixes`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.vocab.suffixes` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / vocab / "Suffixes" — word parts
  added to the end of a base word or root that change its meaning or
  grammatical category, taught with explicit attention to suffixes'
  primary grammatical-conversion function and the systematic spelling
  rules governing their attachment.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.vocab.word-families` — load-bearing because
  that concept's insight that word-family members span different word
  classes built from a shared root (act/action/active/actively) is
  precisely the phenomenon suffixes are responsible for producing —
  this concept examines the specific mechanism (the suffix itself)
  behind that family-spanning pattern already observed.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.vocab.roots-and-origins`, jointly with
  `eng.vocab.prefixes` as co-prerequisites. Having examined both ends of
  a word (prefix at the front, suffix at the end), the learner is ready
  to examine the base word or root itself — its historical origin and
  deeper meaning — as the next layer of morphological analysis.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify noun-forming suffixes (-tion, -ment, -ness, -ity) and the
   resulting word-class change from the base word.
2. Identify adjective-forming (-ful, -ous, -ive, -able) and adverb-
   forming (-ly) suffixes and the resulting word-class change.
3. Identify verb-forming suffixes (-ize, -ify, -en) and the resulting
   word-class change, completing the four-word-class pattern.
4. Correctly apply the three core suffix spelling rules — dropping a
   silent final "e," changing "y" to "i," doubling a final consonant —
   based on the base word's specific ending pattern.
5. Combine word-class identification with correct spelling application
   for a word requiring a spelling adjustment.

## Core Understanding

A suffix is a word part added to the end of a base word or root that
changes its meaning or grammatical category — but this concept's
genuine teaching challenge is recognizing that suffixes' PRIMARY
function is often grammatical, not merely semantic: many common
suffixes exist specifically to change a word's CLASS (part of speech),
often more predictably than they add any subtle shade of meaning —
"-tion/-ment/-ness/-ity" typically create nouns, "-ful/-ous/-ive/-able"
typically create adjectives, "-ly" typically creates adverbs, and
"-ize/-ify/-en" typically create verbs, so "create" (verb) plus "-tion"
becomes "creation" (noun) with the suffix's essential job being that
grammatical conversion. A second, equally load-bearing fact is that the
spelling changes accompanying suffix attachment — dropping a silent
final "e" before a vowel-starting suffix ("hope" + "-ing" = "hoping"),
changing a final "y" to "i" before most suffixes when preceded by a
consonant ("happy" + "-ness" = "happiness"), and doubling a final
consonant in short, stressed, single-vowel-ending words ("run" + "-ing"
= "running") — are systematic, checkable rules tied to the base word's
specific ending, not arbitrary or random exceptions to memorize
case-by-case.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "suffixes just add a bit of extra meaning to a
  word, and any spelling changes when adding them are unpredictable
  quirks"**: the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists
  to correct — a natural focus on meaning first (since meaning feels
  like "the main point" of a word part), with spelling changes
  experienced as isolated exceptions encountered one at a time without
  an underlying rule ever being made explicit.
- **Intermediate model — "I can identify noun-forming suffixes and the
  resulting word class"**: installed by TA-1's noun-forming-suffix
  practice, using the Component 3 costume-change anchor to make the
  grammatical-conversion process visually concrete. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly naming BOTH the suffix and the resulting word class for a
  fresh noun-forming example, not merely producing the derived word.
- **Advanced model — "suffixes primarily convert a word's grammatical
  CLASS — I can identify this across adjective/adverb and verb-forming
  suffixes too, completing the full pattern"**: installed by TA-2 and
  TA-3's adjective/adverb and verb-forming suffix practice, resolving
  MC-SUFFIXES-ONLY-CHANGE-MEANING-NOT-WORD-CLASS through the felt
  realization that "creation" is fundamentally a different KIND of word
  than "create," not merely a variant with added meaning. Upgrade
  trigger: spontaneously stating a fresh suffixed word's word class
  before being asked, rather than only describing a meaning change.
- **Expert model — "spelling changes when adding suffixes follow
  systematic, checkable rules based on the base word's ending, and I
  can combine word-class identification with correct spelling
  together"**: installed by TA-4's spelling-rule application (resolving
  MC-SUFFIX-SPELLING-CHANGES-ARE-RANDOM) and TA-5's integration task.
  This is the concept's genuine mastery bar — treating spelling rule
  application as a checkable procedure applied to the base word's
  ending, not a case-by-case memorization task.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Adaptive Flags, for
  S9 learners whose L1 doesn't mark word-class changes through
  suffixation (many isolating languages) or has very different
  spelling-adjustment conventions, both the word-class-conversion
  concept itself and the English-specific spelling rules may have no
  direct L1 parallel and warrant dedicated, explicit teaching rather
  than assumed transfer.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, both reasonable
extensions of how suffixes are typically first encountered. The
meaning-only failure exists because meaning is the most immediately
salient aspect of a new suffixed word — a learner encountering
"creation" for the first time naturally focuses on what it MEANS,
with the grammatical fact that it now functions as a noun (rather than
the verb "create") going unremarked unless directly pointed out, since
ordinary reading and vocabulary exposure rarely explicitly labels word
classes. The random-spelling-changes failure exists because spelling
adjustments are typically encountered piecemeal, one specific word at a
time, across years of writing instruction — a learner memorizes
"hoping" and "running" as individually correct spellings long before
any single lesson makes the underlying, shared RULE explicit, so each
new case can feel like a fresh exception to learn rather than an
instance of an already-known, checkable pattern.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.vocab.suffixes.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions this
concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-SUFFIXES-ONLY-CHANGE-MEANING-NOT-WORD-CLASS (Blueprint Component
1) — Type 2, perceptual intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a genuine perceptual default rather than
  a wrongly-taught rule — meaning is simply the more salient, more
  immediately noticed aspect of any new word, and grammatical word-class
  membership is a more abstract, structural property that ordinary
  reading and speaking rarely require a speaker to consciously label,
  so the learner's attention naturally settles on meaning first with
  nothing perceptually foregrounding the word-class change until it is
  explicitly pointed out.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, upon meeting a
  fresh suffixed word, spontaneously says something like "so that turns
  it into a noun now" before being asked about meaning at all is showing
  the grammatical-conversion focus has become the learner's default
  lens, not merely a fact they can produce when prompted.

**MC-SUFFIX-SPELLING-CHANGES-ARE-RANDOM (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization inverted — genuinely a Type 2, perceptual
intuition**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: a Type 2 signature — spelling
  adjustments are typically encountered as individually memorized,
  correctly-spelled instances across years of ordinary writing
  instruction, with the underlying shared rule never made explicit
  in that piecemeal exposure, so the learner has no perceptual or
  instructional basis for recognizing "hoping" and "running" as
  instances of the SAME rule-governed pattern rather than unrelated,
  individually-memorized spellings.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when given a
  genuinely novel base word requiring a spelling adjustment, correctly
  predicts the needed change by naming the applicable rule ("that ends
  in a short vowel and a single consonant, so I need to double it")
  rather than simply guessing or asking is showing the systematic-rule
  model has been genuinely internalized.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the suffix job-change costume anchor itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): attaching a
  suffix card to a base-word card and flipping its grammatical-role
  label (VERB → NOUN, or → ADJECTIVE) directly embodies the
  grammatical-conversion function as a "costume change" for a different
  sentence "role." Breaking point: a costume change implies the
  underlying "actor" (the base word's core meaning) stays essentially
  unchanged beneath the costume, which is largely true for grammatical
  conversion but can understate that some suffixes DO also shift shade
  of meaning alongside the word-class change — this entry treats the
  costume-change anchor as the introductory frame for the grammatical-
  conversion insight specifically, not a claim that suffixes never
  affect meaning at all.
- **Alternative — a checklist before mailing a letter, not guesswork**:
  spelling rule application is like running through a short, fixed
  checklist (does the base word end in a silent e? does it end in y
  after a consonant? does it end in a single stressed vowel + consonant?)
  before "sending" the suffixed word, rather than guessing at the
  spelling. Breaking point: useful for framing spelling-rule application
  as a checkable procedure, but should not be extended to suggest every
  suffix attachment requires a spelling adjustment — many base words
  (e.g., "walk" + "-ing") need no adjustment at all, and the checklist's
  purpose is determining WHETHER a rule applies, not assuming one
  always does.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe spelling-rule exceptions as "just
  memorize these, there's no real pattern"**: framing spelling rules as
  arbitrary memorization directly reinforces MC-SUFFIX-SPELLING-
  CHANGES-ARE-RANDOM; even genuinely irregular cases should be
  distinguished from the three core systematic rules (drop-e, y-to-i,
  double-consonant), which reliably apply based on a checkable base-word
  ending pattern.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
grammatical-conversion function and the systematic spelling rules are
both genuine, directly felt phenomena well-suited to guided discovery:
**need** (the learner, having secured word-family knowledge, is asked
what word class "creation" is compared to "create"); **playground/
invention** (TA-1 through TA-3's suffix-by-word-class practice across
several examples lets the learner notice the pattern applies broadly);
**collision** (the Blueprint's own "is 'creation' still a verb?" probe
breaks the meaning-only assumption; the "hopeing" vs. "hoping" contrast
breaks the random-spelling assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's
own-words statement of both the grammatical-conversion function and the
systematic spelling rules); **compression** (TA-5's fluent combination
of word-class identification and correct spelling without needing each
rule separately re-derived). The specific RULE NAMES (drop-e, y-to-i,
double-consonant) and word-class category labels are convention-bound
terminology best introduced by direct instruction once the learner has
already felt the underlying patterns through TA-1–3's examples.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (noun-forming suffixes) is placed first as the concept's most
familiar entry point, since noun-forming suffixes are common and
readily demonstrated using the Component 3 anchor's own "create →
creation" example. TA-2 (adjective- and adverb-forming suffixes)
follows, directly targeting MC-SUFFIXES-ONLY-CHANGE-MEANING-NOT-WORD-
CLASS by requiring explicit word-class identification across a second,
distinct pair of categories — per the Blueprint's own session
architecture, the conflict-evidence probe for this misconception is
placed after TA-2, once the learner has multiple word-class examples in
hand. TA-3 (verb-forming suffixes) completes the four-word-class
pattern (noun, adjective, adverb, verb), placed third so the learner
builds the full category framework incrementally rather than all at
once. TA-4 (spelling rule application) is placed after all three
word-class categories are established, addressing the SECOND,
independent skill dimension (correct spelling, not word class) once the
learner already has a secure sense of what genuine suffix attachment
looks like across multiple categories — introducing spelling
complexity simultaneously with word-class variety would risk
overloading the learner with two new dimensions at once. TA-5
(combining word-class change with correct spelling) is placed last as
the concept's integration capstone, requiring both skill strands (TA-1–
3's categorization and TA-4's spelling rules) to operate together on
the same word. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own
turn-by-turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References
below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously states a
  fresh suffixed word's word class (not just its meaning) when
  introduced to it — this is the primary diagnostic signal for whether
  TA-1–3's grammatical-conversion focus has genuinely become the
  learner's default lens rather than an occasionally-recalled fact.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before spelling a
  genuinely novel suffixed word, followed by a correctly-applied
  spelling rule (stated or silently applied), suggests the learner is
  consciously checking the base word's ending against the relevant rule
  rather than guessing — a good sign. Instant, confident but incorrect
  spelling (e.g., "hopeing") with no hesitation at all is the concerning
  pattern for MC-SUFFIX-SPELLING-CHANGES-ARE-RANDOM.
- **Word-class stakes**: real and central to TA-1–3/MP-1–3 — a learner
  who correctly identifies a suffix but describes only a vague meaning
  shift, without naming the resulting word class when asked directly,
  may still be under MC-SUFFIXES-ONLY-CHANGE-MEANING-NOT-WORD-CLASS's
  influence even after apparent identification success.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "did the suffix change more than just
  the meaning — what word class is this now?" is this concept's
  load-bearing sentence for MC-SUFFIXES-ONLY-CHANGE-MEANING-NOT-WORD-
  CLASS, per `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` —
  asked whenever a fresh suffixed word is introduced, making word-class
  identification a routinely-invited response rather than an
  afterthought.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: procedure — a structural word-analysis routine
(identify a suffix, determine the resulting word class, check the base
word's ending against the relevant spelling rule, apply it correctly)
applied to genuinely novel words, following a skill-fluency pattern:
correctness first (reliably identifying word class and applying
spelling rules, even slowly, one at a time), then fluent, integrated
application (TA-5) combining both dimensions on the same word without
needing them addressed separately. Interleaving partners: mix
word-class-only identification items (testing TA-1/TA-2/TA-3) with
spelling-rule-only items (testing TA-4) and fully combined items
(testing TA-5) in ongoing review, so the learner continues exercising
both dimensions as distinct, separately-verified skills as well as their
integration.

  or professional level depends substantially on correct suffix spelling
  becoming automatic, and grammatical fluency in complex sentence
  construction depends on reliably recognizing which word class a given
  form belongs to.
