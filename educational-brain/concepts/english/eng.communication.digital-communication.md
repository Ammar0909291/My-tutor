## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Produce appropriately different register versions of the same content for different digital platforms and relationships.
2. Assess the risk of tone misinterpretation in a text-based digital message, especially for sarcasm, dry humor, or subtle criticism.
3. Revise a tone-risky message to reduce misinterpretation risk.
4. Apply platform-specific conventions (email structure, chat brevity, social media's public/permanent nature).
5. Compose appropriately adapted versions of one message purpose across multiple digital platforms.

## Core Understanding

Different digital platforms carry **different register expectations, just as spoken contexts do** — this is the concept's first load-bearing fact: a message perfectly appropriate for a text to a close friend ("hey can u send the report? thx") is a genuine register mismatch when sent via professional email to a supervisor, even though both are "digital communication." Recognizing this means the writer must actively match register to the specific platform and relationship each time, rather than applying one uniform digital style everywhere. The concept's second, equally load-bearing fact is that **text-based digital communication strips away vocal tone, facial expression, and immediate clarifying feedback**, making intended tone — especially sarcasm, subtle humor, or gentle criticism — genuinely easy to misread; what feels obviously playful to the sender can land as cold, confusing, or hostile to a reader who lacks the vocal and facial cues that would disambiguate the same words spoken aloud. Because this risk is real and not merely hypothetical, a careful digital communicator assesses tone-misread risk deliberately and adds explicit clarity (a clarifying phrase, appropriate rephrasing) when the stakes of misinterpretation are genuinely high.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "I write digital messages the same way regardless of platform, and I trust that my intended tone will come through in text the way it would if I said it out loud."**
The runnable simulation: apply one uniform casual (or uniform formal) style everywhere, and assume sarcasm or humor will read as clearly in text as in speech. This is a reasonable default, since a learner's most frequent digital communication (texting friends) genuinely doesn't require register-switching, giving little occasion to notice the mismatch until a different platform or relationship is involved.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — comparing the same casual message sent to a friend (fine) versus a supervisor (a mismatch); and seeing a sarcastic text with no vocal cue, asking how a reader who doesn't know the sender well might interpret it.
*Shelf-life warning at replacement*: "Different platforms and relationships need different registers — and text strips away the tone cues speech has, so sarcasm and subtle humor are genuinely easy to misread."

**Intermediate — "I match register to platform, but I still don't always think to check whether my tone might be misread in a specific message."**
The simulation: correctly adjusts register across platforms, but doesn't yet systematically assess tone-misread risk before sending, especially for messages that feel obviously playful or clear to the sender.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — the sarcastic "Oh great, ANOTHER meeting" message, and recognizing both a genuinely-annoyed and a playfully-sarcastic reading are equally plausible without vocal cues.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Feeling clearly playful to you doesn't guarantee it reads that way. Check tone-risk deliberately, especially for sarcasm or criticism."

**Advanced — "I match register to platform and relationship, assess tone-misread risk before sending, revise risky messages for clarity, and apply platform-specific conventions."**
The simulation: composes platform-appropriate messages as a matter of course, actively checking tone risk and applying the right conventions (subject lines, greetings, brevity, public-permanence awareness) for each context. This is the target model, matching TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the direct unlock `eng.communication.cross-cultural-communication`, where this concept's tone-fragility awareness extends to contexts where misinterpretation risk is compounded by differing cultural norms.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the platform-and-tone judgment. The next step adds a further layer — cultural norms that can shift what counts as appropriate register or clear tone in the first place."

**Expert — "at full digital-communication fluency, platform-register matching and tone-risk assessment happen largely automatically, with deliberate revision reserved for genuinely high-stakes or unfamiliar-audience messages."**
Named here to mark the arc; this concept's explicit platform-dial and tone-risk-checking routine is a scaffold for a judgment that, at true fluency, operates largely below conscious awareness for familiar platforms and audiences — named here so the tutor understands the routine's eventual goal is fluent, automatic register-and-tone judgment, not a permanently maintained conscious checklist.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant failure mechanism is that **a learner's most frequent, most practiced digital communication is casual texting with close contacts**, where register-matching genuinely doesn't need to vary — this heavily-reinforced pattern generalizes reasonably, if incorrectly, to the assumption that digital communication in general doesn't require register-switching, since the learner's own dominant experience never required it.

The second mechanism is that **the sender always has full access to their own intended tone** while composing a message, making that tone feel self-evidently obvious — the learner has no direct experience of how the message reads to someone who lacks that inside knowledge, since they cannot un-know their own intent while re-reading their own words.

The third mechanism, specific to sarcasm and dry humor, is that **these tones specifically depend on vocal inflection or facial expression to signal "this isn't literal"** in spoken communication, and text-based digital communication removes exactly that signaling channel — meaning sarcasm is disproportionately, not just generally, vulnerable to misreading compared to more literal statements.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns the trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-THE-SAME-REGISTER-AND-STYLE-WORKS-ACROSS-ALL-DIGITAL-PLATFORMS
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-THE-SAME-REGISTER-AND-STYLE-WORKS-ACROSS-ALL-DIGITAL-PLATFORMS — includes the friend-text/supervisor-email discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of a genuinely reasonable pattern from the learner's own dominant digital-communication experience (casual texting with close contacts), extended to all digital communication regardless of platform or relationship.
- **Teaching consequence of the birth type**: because the casual register isn't wrong for its actual dominant context, the repair must **add platform/relationship-checking as a new step, not discard the casual register as generally bad** — the Blueprint's own bridge text does this correctly by framing register as something to match to context, not to abandon.
- **Verification of death**: given the same content and two different platforms, the learner produces appropriately different register versions for each, rather than one uniform style.

### MC-TONE-IS-EASY-TO-CONVEY-IN-TEXT-BASED-DIGITAL-COMMUNICATION
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-TONE-IS-EASY-TO-CONVEY-IN-TEXT-BASED-DIGITAL-COMMUNICATION — includes the sarcastic-meeting-message discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), reinforced by the sender's own privileged, complete access to their intended tone while composing — a genuine asymmetry between sender and reader experience, not an overextended rule.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct exposure to how the same message could plausibly be read two different ways by someone without the sender's inside knowledge**, since simply being told "tone is easy to misread" is less convincing than seeing the specific alternative, plausible reading.
- **Verification of death**: given a genuinely tone-ambiguous message, the learner identifies the misinterpretation risk and revises for clarity, rather than assuming the intended tone is self-evident.

## Analogies

**Best — the Platform Dial and the Muted Instrument** (Blueprint Component 3): turning a dial between Text-to-Friend, Professional Email, Social Media Post, and Work Chat settings directly seeds the platform-matching fix; a musical instrument playing through a thick mute (representing text) versus playing openly (representing face-to-face speech) directly seeds the tone-fragility fix, since subtlety and expression genuinely get muffled through the "muted" text channel.
*Breaking point*: a physical mute affects volume and timbre uniformly, while text-based communication doesn't uniformly muffle all tone types equally — literal, factual statements transfer to text with little distortion, while sarcasm and subtle emotional content are disproportionately affected — useful for the general muffling intuition, less precise for implying every tone type is equally at risk.

**Alternative — a phone call with a bad connection versus a clear one.** On a bad connection (text-based communication), you lose some of the vocal cues (tone, emphasis, timing) that a clear connection (face-to-face speech) carries, and a joke or subtle comment can land wrong simply because those cues didn't come through, not because anything was actually said incorrectly.
*Breaking point*: a bad phone connection is a passive degradation outside anyone's control, while a writer composing a digital message has active choices (adding a clarifying phrase, rephrasing) available to compensate for the missing channel — useful for the lost-cues intuition, less precise for implying the writer is a passive victim of the medium rather than an active compensator.

**Visual/physical anchor — the Platform Dial and the Muted Instrument** as already described above, doubling as both the primary teaching metaphor and the actual working instrument this concept's core teaching is organized around.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Just write digital messages the way you'd talk — it'll come across fine."** Directly installs both misconceptions at once: ignoring platform-register differences and assuming tone transfers cleanly to text.
- **"If you meant it as a joke, people will get that it's a joke."** Directly installs MC-TONE-IS-EASY-TO-CONVEY-IN-TEXT-BASED-DIGITAL-COMMUNICATION as a stated assumption, exactly the tone-transfers-easily error this concept's second misconception's repair must counter.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Platform Dial and the Muted Instrument (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Do you think this same message works on every platform?" Then turn the dial across platforms and confirm register needs differ.
2. **The friend-text/supervisor-email collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Would this exact message work sent to a supervisor?" Then compare and resolve.
3. **The sarcastic-meeting-message collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "How do you think someone who doesn't know you well would read this?" Then reveal both plausible readings and resolve.
4. **The multi-platform composition task (learner activity, TA-5).** *Predict first*: "How will your wording change across these 3 platforms for the same purpose?" Then compose and confirm together.

## Discovery Questions

This concept is well suited to **guided discovery for both structural facts**, since the evidence for each (platform-register mismatch; tone-misread risk) is directly checkable through the learner's own comparison of real messages across contexts.

1. **Need**: "If you sent the exact same casual text to a close friend and to your boss, would both reactions be the same?"
2. **Playground**: the learner drafts the same content for two different platforms freely, before being told which register fits which.
3. **Invention**: "Which version actually fits its platform, and why?" The learner justifies their own drafts.
4. **Collision**: present the sarcastic meeting message and ask how a stranger, versus a close friend, might read it differently.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — register must match platform/relationship, and tone is fragile in text — from the learner's own evidence.
6. **Compression**: "Match the platform, and don't trust text to carry your tone for you."

## Teaching Sequence

The pedagogical logic behind this arc:

- **Platform-register matching (TA-1) is established first**, directly confronting the first misconception before tone content is introduced, since platform-awareness is the more foundational, structural judgment this concept builds on.
- **Tone-risk assessment (TA-2) comes second**, introducing the awareness that tone can be misread before the harder generative step (TA-3) of actually revising for clarity.
- **Adding tone clarity (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting the second misconception by teaching the concrete repair (rephrasing, clarifying phrases) once the risk-assessment judgment (TA-2) is secure.
- **Platform-specific conventions (TA-4) and the multi-platform composition capstone (TA-5) come last**, with TA-4 adding concrete stylistic conventions per platform and TA-5 requiring the learner to integrate register-matching, tone-clarity, and platform conventions simultaneously across three genuinely different contexts — matching this program's established sequencing principle that a concept's most integrative task is reserved for its culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

This concept's core evidence is **written composition with stated platform/audience awareness** — the learner's drafted messages plus, critically, their stated reasoning about why a given register or tone-clarity choice fits the specific platform, since a plausible-looking message with no stated context-awareness cannot be distinguished from a lucky match.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A spontaneous register adjustment when a platform or audience changes mid-task, without being prompted** ("wait, if this is going to my boss I should rephrase that") — the direct behavioral signature of the first misconception's repair.
- **A stated tone-risk flag on a message the learner themselves drafted, followed by a revision** ("this could sound sarcastic if someone doesn't know me, let me clarify") — the direct target evidence for the second misconception's repair.
- **Identical wording produced for a friend-text and a supervisor-email version of the same content, with no adjustment** — the direct behavioral signature of the first misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Match the platform and the relationship — and don't assume text will carry your tone the way your voice would."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads, which the Blueprint does not own:

- **Register-to-platform matching (MP-1) and tone-risky revision (MP-2).** Per the Blueprint's own S9 protocol routing, mastery must NOT be certified unless both pass — a learner who applies platform conventions well (MP-4) but still uses a uniform register or ignores tone risk has not learned the core adaptive judgment this concept requires.
- **Tone-risk assessment (MP-3).** Listen for a stated reason naming the specific ambiguous element (sarcasm, brevity that could read as curt), not just a correct final risk ranking.
- **Platform-specific conventions (MP-4).** Watch for conventions genuinely specific to the named platform (a subject line for email, brevity-but-respectful for work chat, public/permanent awareness for social media), not a generic "be professional" answer.
- **Multi-platform composition (MP-5).** This concept's synthesis item; a strong performance here with weak MP-1/MP-2 scores suggests scripted-practice success without full internalization.

**Mastery certification trigger**: correctly differentiated register versions for a novel content-across-two-platforms item; a genuine, risk-reducing revision of a tone-risky message; correct tone-risk identification with stated reasoning across a set of messages; correct application of platform-specific conventions; and a full multi-platform composition for one purpose across three distinct platforms.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But I always write the same way no matter who I'm messaging, and it's never been a problem"** — take this as an accurate report of the learner's actual, limited experience, not a mistake to dismiss; respond by naming the boundary directly: "That's worked because the people you usually message already know you well. The register-switching skill matters most exactly when you're messaging someone who doesn't have that same context — a new contact, a supervisor, a stranger."
- **"I don't get why my joke would sound mean, it was obviously a joke to me"** — a genuine, understandable gap given the sender's privileged access to their own intent; respond by making the reader's missing information concrete: "You know it's a joke because you know your own tone of voice in your head as you write it — but the reader only has the words on the screen, with none of that. That's exactly the gap this concept is about."
- **The smaller question to shrink to**: from a full three-platform composition task, down to **a single content item drafted for just two clearly, maximally different platforms (a close-friend text and a formal email), with no tone-ambiguity complication at all.** This removes the tone-risk-assessment demand entirely, isolating the most basic register-matching skill before returning to harder, tone-ambiguous cases.
- **Never shrink to "just add a smiley emoji to anything that might sound sarcastic."** This treats a single surface fix as a universal solution rather than building the actual risk-assessment judgment this concept requires.
