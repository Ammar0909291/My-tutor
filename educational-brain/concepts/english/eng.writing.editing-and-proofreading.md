## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Demonstrate that reading aloud or using a disrupted-order technique catches errors a normal silent read missed.
2. Identify a homophone or context-dependent error that a spell-checker would miss.
3. Apply a single-error-type proofreading pass and a common-error checklist systematically.
4. Fully proofread a near-final draft using multiple techniques, correcting all found errors.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **reading a piece silently once, at normal speed, does not reliably catch all remaining errors** — the brain automatically "auto-corrects" familiar text while reading silently and quickly, meaning typos and small omissions are genuinely easy to miss this way; multiple passes and different techniques (reading aloud, reading backward sentence-by-sentence, checking one error type at a time) catch errors a single silent read misses. Second, **spell-check and grammar-check tools do not catch every error** — they check whether a word is spelled correctly, not whether it's the RIGHT word for the context, so homophone errors ("their/there/they're," "its/it's") are perfectly spelled but often wrong, and these tools frequently miss them entirely or flag correct constructions as errors; human proofreading remains necessary even after running these tools, not redundant with them.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "reading through my piece once catches everything, and spell-check catches all my errors."**
The simulation: proofread with a single quick silent read and trust a clean spell-check pass as proof the piece is error-free.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a paragraph with planted errors caught far more reliably reading aloud slowly than reading silently once, and a homophone error ("Their going to the store") that a spell-checker genuinely cannot flag.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A rearview mirror has a real blind spot no amount of staring fixes — you have to turn your head. And one flashlight sweep misses what three targeted sweeps catch."

**Intermediate — "I can now use multiple techniques and spot some spell-check blind spots, but I still sometimes trust a clean spell-check run as the final word."**
The simulation: applies read-aloud and disrupted-order techniques but still treats a spell-check pass with no flagged issues as sufficient confirmation.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-2's direct spell-check blind-spot identification practice, given a passage with homophone errors a spell-checker genuinely cannot catch.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Spell-check is a useful first pass, not the final word — always proofread yourself afterward, especially for homophones."

**Advanced — "I fully proofread a near-final draft using multiple techniques and a common-error checklist, correcting all found errors."**
The simulation: integrates multi-technique proofreading and spell-check-blind-spot awareness into one coherent, systematic proofreading pass. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: `eng.writing.essay-structure`'s final polish stage, which depends directly on this concept's proofreading discipline.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you a clean, error-free draft. Essay structure's final polish stage builds directly on this."

**Expert — "at fluency, using multiple proofreading techniques and recognizing spell-check's limits happens as an integrated part of ordinary finishing work, without a separate deliberate checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit multi-technique-and-blind-spot routine is a scaffold for a proofreading habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of composition.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **the brain's automatic reading fluency (the same skill that makes silent reading efficient) actively works against error-detection**, since familiar-looking text gets processed for meaning rather than for exact letter-by-letter accuracy — a genuinely counterintuitive cost of being a fluent reader that isn't obvious until directly measured.

The second mechanism is that **spell-check tools are visually reassuring (a clean run with no red underlines) and widely available**, creating a strong, easy-to-adopt habit of treating their silence as a positive confirmation of correctness, rather than recognizing their narrow, letter-level scope.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-PROOFREADING-BY-READING-SILENTLY-ONCE-CATCHES-ALL-ERRORS
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from silent, fluent reading feeling thorough and attentive, masking the brain's genuine tendency to auto-correct familiar text past small errors.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **directly compare error-catch rates between a silent read and a read-aloud or disrupted-order technique**, since the auto-correction effect is far more convincing demonstrated through a measured miss than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given a passage, the learner demonstrates that reading aloud or a disrupted-order technique catches errors a normal silent read missed.

### MC-SPELL-CHECK-AND-GRAMMAR-CHECK-TOOLS-CATCH-EVERY-ERROR
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from spell-check's visually reassuring "clean run" interface encouraging a habit of treating its silence as complete confirmation of correctness.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **confirming directly that a spell-checker would not flag a specific homophone error**, since the blind spot is far more convincing demonstrated through a concrete failed check than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given a passage with a homophone or context-dependent error a spell-checker would miss, the learner identifies and corrects it.

## Analogies

**Best — the Blind Spot Mirror and the Multi-Pass Flashlight** (Blueprint Component 3): a car's rearview mirror with a labeled blind spot area it simply cannot show no matter how carefully you look — directly seeding the fix for MC-SPELL-CHECK-AND-GRAMMAR-CHECK-TOOLS-CATCH-EVERY-ERROR. A single flashlight sweep across a dark room versus three separate targeted sweeps (shape, texture, color) — directly seeding the fix for MC-PROOFREADING-BY-READING-SILENTLY-ONCE-CATCHES-ALL-ERRORS.
*Breaking point*: a car's blind spot is a fixed, physically-defined region that a head-turn reliably resolves every time, while a spell-checker's blind spots (context-dependent word errors) are more varied and less predictably located than a single, consistent physical zone.

**Alternative — a metal detector at airport security versus a full pat-down.** A metal detector catches metal objects reliably but misses non-metal contraband entirely; a full manual check catches what the detector's specific technology cannot, just as human proofreading catches what spell-check's specific, narrow technology (letter-sequence matching) cannot.
*Breaking point*: a metal detector's limitation is a single, well-defined category (non-metal objects), while spell-check's blind spots span a more varied, harder-to-enumerate category (any correctly-spelled wrong word), a difference in the predictability of what's missed.

**Visual anchor — the Blind Spot Mirror and the Multi-Pass Flashlight**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Read through it once and you'll catch anything wrong."** Directly installs the single-silent-read misconception as a stated definition.
- **"If spell-check doesn't flag anything, your writing is error-free."** Directly installs the spell-check-sufficiency misconception as a stated definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Blind Spot Mirror and the Multi-Pass Flashlight (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Can staring harder at the mirror ever show you what's in its blind spot?"
2. **The technique-comparison collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Will a single silent read at normal speed catch all three planted errors?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by comparing error-catch rates across techniques and by testing a real spell-checker against a homophone error.

1. **Need**: "If your brain reads text quickly and smoothly, does that mean it's checking every single letter carefully?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely proofreads a passage silently once before being told the official multi-technique comparison test.
3. **Invention**: "Can you think of a word that's spelled correctly but might be the WRONG word for its sentence?"
4. **Collision**: present a homophone error and ask whether a spell-checker would catch it.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — a single silent read misses errors a fluent brain auto-corrects past, and spell-check can't catch correctly-spelled wrong words — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "One silent read isn't enough; spell-check can't hear context."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Multi-technique proofreading practice (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the first misconception before spell-check's limits are introduced.
- **Spell-check's blind spots (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting the second misconception once multi-technique awareness is established.
- **Single-error-type passes (TA-3) comes third**, extending the multi-technique discipline into a more systematic, targeted practice.
- **Common error pattern recognition (TA-4) comes fourth**, formalizing a checklist of frequently-missed error types.
- **Full proofreading of an original draft (TA-5) comes last**, the generative capstone integrating both misconception fixes — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated technique choice and their explicit check for spell-check blind spots**, since a clean-looking final draft with no stated process cannot be distinguished from an unusually error-free first attempt.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **Voluntary use of a technique beyond a single silent read** (reading aloud, disrupted order) when proofreading a new passage — the direct behavioral signature of the multi-technique fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary checking for homophone or context-dependent errors after a spell-check pass**, without treating the clean spell-check result as sufficient — direct evidence the blind-spot-awareness fix is internalized.
- **Relying on a single silent read-through, or treating a clean spell-check run as proof of an error-free draft** — the direct behavioral signature of either misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"One silent read isn't enough — and spell-check can't hear context."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"I already read through it, it looked fine"** — take this as an accurate report of genuine effort, not a mistake; respond by adding the technique test: "You did read it, that's true — but did you read it aloud, or just silently at normal speed? Try reading it aloud slowly and see what you notice."
- **"Spell-check didn't flag anything, so it must be correct"** — a genuine, reasonable trust given how reliable these tools generally feel; respond by naming the additional layer: "No flags is a good sign for spelling, yes — but would it catch a correctly-spelled WRONG word, like 'their' instead of 'there'? Check those specifically yourself."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full proofreading pass down to **finding one single planted error in a short passage using a read-aloud technique, with checklist application and spell-check-blind-spot checking set aside.** Isolates the most basic multi-technique benefit before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just run spell-check and call it done."** This directly licenses the spell-check-sufficiency pattern, undermining the human-proofreading discipline this concept specifically builds.
