## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Distinguish a genuine, arguable claim from a bare topic announcement using the "disagree test."
2. Diagnose whether a thesis is too broad, too narrow, or properly sized using the "list your body paragraphs" test.
3. Build a properly scoped thesis from a set of supporting observations, tying them together as one claim.
4. Diagnose an unnamed flaw in a given thesis (announcement, too broad, too narrow, or missing a preview) and revise it into a fully qualifying thesis statement.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **a thesis is not just an announcement that a topic exists** — a genuine thesis makes a CLAIM, something a reasonable person could disagree with or that the essay will specifically prove or explain; "this essay is about school lunch programs" tells a reader nothing they could push back on, while a specific claim gives them something to hold onto and lets them predict the essay's content. Second, **having "an opinion" alone does not make a thesis correct** — a thesis must be properly SIZED to the essay being written: too broad (like "social media is bad") sprawls into an unmanageable list of unrelated paragraphs, while too narrow (a single tiny fact) leaves nothing to develop after the first sentence; a thesis has to be sized to exactly fit the essay's actual length, tested by listing the 2-4 body paragraphs it would require.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "my thesis just needs to announce what my essay is about, and having an opinion is enough."**
The simulation: write "In this essay I will talk about the causes of the American Revolution" as a thesis, or assert "Social media is bad" without checking whether that claim can actually be developed within the essay's length.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a topic-announcement sentence tested against a specific, defensible claim for whether a reader can predict the essay's argument, and "Social media is bad" tested by listing its unmanageable sprawl of possible body paragraphs.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Announcing a topic is like saying 'I'm going to talk about weather' before a forecast — the forecast itself is the actual claim. And a thesis has to be sized like a container for the water you actually have."

**Intermediate — "I can now write a genuine, properly-sized claim, but I sometimes forget to preview the essay's supporting reasons within the thesis itself."**
The simulation: correctly avoids announcements and sizing errors but writes a bare claim with no "because" clause hinting at the body paragraphs to come.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-4's direct preview-clause practice, expanding a bare claim into a full previewing thesis by adding specific supporting reasons.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A strong thesis often signals, in miniature, the shape of the body paragraphs to come — add the 'because...' that names your reasons."

**Advanced — "I diagnose an unnamed flaw in a given thesis and revise it into a fully qualifying statement, integrating claim-testing, sizing, and preview together."**
The simulation: integrates the disagree test, the sizing test, and the preview mechanism into one coherent diagnosis-and-repair judgment. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: `eng.writing.citations-and-referencing`'s requirement that a citation only matters in service of a claim, building directly on this concept's thesis-writing foundation.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the load-bearing sentence for any essay. Supporting that claim with sourced evidence comes next."

**Expert — "at fluency, testing a thesis for claim-strength, size, and preview happens as an integrated part of ordinary essay composition, without a separate deliberate checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit claim-size-preview routine is a scaffold for a thesis-writing habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of composition.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **the essay-structure-level announcement-versus-thesis error, once resolved for a full introduction, doesn't automatically resolve at the single-sentence thesis level** — a learner who has learned an introduction needs a specific claim still may not apply the disagree test rigorously to the exact sentence functioning as the thesis itself.

The second mechanism is that **"having an opinion" is a genuinely necessary but not sufficient condition for a good thesis**, so once a learner clears the bar of having SOME opinion, there's no automatic prompt to check whether that opinion is sized appropriately for the essay's actual length — sizing only becomes a visible problem once someone actually tries to write the body paragraphs.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-THESIS-IS-JUST-THE-TOPIC-ANNOUNCEMENT
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the essay-level announcement-versus-thesis distinction not automatically transferring down to the specific single-sentence thesis itself.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **apply the "disagree test" mechanically to the learner's own sentence**, since the claim-versus-announcement distinction is far more convincing demonstrated through a direct rebuttal-writing attempt than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given four sentences, the learner identifies which are bare topic announcements and which are actual arguable claims, and rewrites one announcement into a claim.

### MC-B-THESIS-JUST-NEEDS-TO-BE-AN-OPINION
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of "having an opinion" being a genuinely necessary first step, extended into treating it as sufficient with no sizing check.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **the "list your body paragraphs" test applied directly**, since the sizing flaw only becomes visible once the learner actually attempts to enumerate supporting paragraphs.
- **Verification of death**: given three thesis statements on the same topic (one too broad, one too narrow, one well-sized), the learner labels each and explains the sizing flaw using the body-paragraph-listing test.

## Analogies

**Best — the Spine-and-Ribs Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a spine card labeled with the thesis, and rib cards branching off it labeled with body-paragraph points, where an unattached rib visibly dangles — directly seeding fixes for both misconceptions: a vague announcement spine gives ribs nothing precise to connect to (MC-A), and a spine sized wrong can't hold three ribs steady or has no room for more than one (MC-B).
*Breaking point*: a physical skeleton's rib attachment is a clean, binary connected-or-dangling state, while a real thesis's "fit" with a body paragraph is a more gradual, judgment-dependent quality than a rib simply attaching or not.

**Alternative — a recipe's headline claim versus its ingredient list.** "This is a quick dinner recipe" (an announcement) tells you nothing specific, while "This 20-minute one-pan chicken recipe uses only pantry staples" (a thesis-like claim) previews exactly what the recipe will deliver and lets you check whether it delivered.
*Breaking point*: a recipe's claim is checked against an objective, verifiable outcome (did it take 20 minutes, did it use pantry staples), while an essay's thesis is checked against the more interpretive standard of whether it's arguable and well-supported, a difference in verifiability the recipe analogy doesn't fully capture.

**Visual anchor — the Spine-and-Ribs Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Just tell the reader what your essay is going to be about."** Directly installs the announcement-as-thesis misconception as a stated definition.
- **"As long as you have an opinion, you have a thesis."** Directly installs the opinion-is-sufficient misconception as a stated definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Spine-and-Ribs Anchor (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "If the spine sentence is vague, can the rib cards actually attach to it?"
2. **The disagree-test collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "If I only read this one sentence, do I know what the essay is going to ARGUE?"
3. **The sizing collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Can you list a manageable, specific set of body paragraphs that 'Social media is bad' would require?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by testing candidate theses against the disagree test and the body-paragraph-listing test.

1. **Need**: "If your thesis just names a topic, could someone write a one-sentence rebuttal to it?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely sorts ten sentences into announcement versus claim piles before being told the official disagree test.
3. **Invention**: "Try listing every body paragraph 'Social media is bad' would need — how many do you get?"
4. **Collision**: present a too-broad thesis and a too-narrow thesis on the same topic, and ask which fails for which reason.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — a thesis must pass the disagree test, and it must be sized to the essay's actual length — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Make a claim someone could disagree with; size it to fit your paragraphs."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Claim vs. announcement (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the first misconception before sizing is introduced, since a claim must exist before it can be sized.
- **Sizing a thesis (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting the second misconception once genuine claims are being produced.
- **Building a thesis from evidence up (TA-3) comes third**, teaching the "ribs before spine" method as a primary production strategy.
- **Previewing structure within the thesis (TA-4) comes fourth**, adding the preview mechanism once claim and sizing are both secure.
- **Revising a weak thesis (TA-5) comes last**, the generative capstone integrating diagnosis and repair across all flaw types — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for whether a sentence passes the disagree test, and their explicit listing of body paragraphs when sizing a thesis**, since a correct-looking thesis with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from lucky imitation of an example.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated disagree-test check** ("could someone respond 'I disagree' or 'prove it' to this?") applied to a new draft sentence — the direct behavioral signature of the claim-versus-announcement fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary listing of intended body paragraphs** before finalizing thesis wording — direct evidence the sizing fix is internalized.
- **Writing "this essay is about X," or asserting a sweeping opinion with no check on how many paragraphs it would require** — the direct behavioral signature of either misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Make a claim someone could disagree with — and size it to fit your paragraphs."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads:

- **Claim vs. announcement (MP-1).** Failure alone indicates the claim/announcement distinction (TA-1) needs review.
- **Sizing diagnosis (MP-2) and building a thesis from evidence (MP-3).** MP-2/MP-3 failures indicate sizing (TA-2/TA-3) needs review.
- **Adding a preview clause (MP-4).** Failure alone indicates the preview mechanism (TA-4) specifically, not the underlying claim.
- **Full diagnosis and revision (MP-5).** The synthesis item; listen for correct flaw-type identification and repair across an unnamed defect, since MP-5 failure with MP-1 through MP-4 passing indicates a diagnosis-under-ambiguity gap requiring one more mixed-flaw worked example.

**Mastery certification trigger**: a correct claim-versus-announcement sort with one rewrite; a correct sizing diagnosis across three theses; a correct evidence-to-thesis synthesis; a correct preview-clause addition; a full flaw diagnosis and revision on an unnamed defect.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But my sentence does say what the essay covers"** — take this as an accurate observation about topic identification, not a mistake; respond by adding the disagree test: "It does name the subject, that's true — but could someone respond 'I disagree' or 'prove it' to this exact sentence?"
- **"I have a strong opinion, isn't that a thesis?"** — a genuine, reasonable first step toward argument; respond by naming the additional layer: "Having an opinion is a real start, yes — but can you list 2-4 body paragraphs this specific opinion would require? If not, it may need narrowing or broadening."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full diagnosis-and-revision down to **sorting one single set of 4 sentences into announcement versus claim, with sizing and preview set aside.** Isolates the most basic disagree-test discipline before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just write your opinion about the topic."** This directly licenses both misconceptions, undermining the claim-and-sizing discipline this concept specifically builds.
