## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify which of two slides supports spoken delivery versus which competes with it, and explain why.
2. Select 3-4 appropriate key points for a specific audience and purpose, justifying exclusions.
3. Outline a presentation's opening, body, and closing structure.
4. Design a slide using key phrases/images rather than full sentences.
5. Prepare and deliver a complete short presentation with selected content, clear structure, and supportive visual aids.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept, extending `eng.speaking.public-speaking-basics`' key-points delivery into a structured, visually-supported format. First, **visual aids should not contain everything the speaker will say** — a slide packed with full sentences duplicating the spoken content causes the audience to read the slide instead of listening to the speaker, turning the visual aid into a competitor rather than a support; good visual aids show key points, images, or data that reinforce spoken content, leaving explanation and detail to the speaker's actual words. Second, **a good presentation does not cover as much information as possible** — cramming in content doesn't demonstrate thoroughness, it makes a presentation harder to follow and less memorable; effective presentations are selective, choosing the most important points for the specific audience and purpose and developing those well, deliberately cutting or summarizing less critical information rather than superficially covering everything. Both corrections replace a "more is better" instinct — in slide density and in content coverage — with a disciplined, audience-focused selectivity.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "visual aids should contain everything I'm going to say; a good presentation covers as much information as possible."**
The simulation: pack slides with full sentences matching the spoken script, and try to include every fact known about the topic.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a dense, 6-sentence slide versus a 3-phrase-plus-image slide, asked which the audience is more likely to read instead of listening; and a 12-point superficial outline versus a 4-point in-depth one, asked which the audience will actually remember.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Slides support what you say, they don't replace it. Depth on fewer points beats superficial coverage of many."

**Intermediate — "I keep my slides lighter now, but I still tend to try to cover too much content in the actual presentation itself."**
The simulation: correctly designs supportive, key-phrase slides but hasn't yet reliably narrowed the spoken content itself to the most important points.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-2's direct practice selecting 3-4 points from a large information pool for a specified audience and purpose.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Ask: what does THIS audience need most? Cut or briefly mention the rest."

**Advanced — "I design supportive, non-duplicative slides, select audience-appropriate content, structure my presentation clearly, and deliver a full presentation integrating all three."**
The simulation: integrates selective content choice, structural organization, and supportive visual design into one coherent, generative presentation. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the three direct downstream unlocks — debate skills (structured argumentation), professional communication, and presentation design (deepening the visual-aid-support principle).
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the full presentation toolkit. Presentation design deepens the visual-support principle; debate and professional communication apply structured delivery in new contexts."

**Expert — "at fluency, selective content choice and supportive visual design happen as an integrated part of preparing any presentation."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit selection-and-design routine is a scaffold for a compositional judgment that, at true fluency, operates as an integrated part of presentation preparation.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **a slide functions, to an inexperienced presenter, as a safety net against forgetting what to say**, so writing out full sentences feels like responsible preparation rather than recognizing it converts the visual aid into a competing text the audience reads instead of listening to the speaker.

The second mechanism is that **more content can feel like more thorough preparation or more demonstrated knowledge**, especially under the anxiety of being evaluated, so a learner reasonably (but incorrectly) equates comprehensiveness with quality, rather than recognizing that audience retention and comprehension actually decrease as content volume increases beyond a manageable, focused set.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-VISUAL-AIDS-SHOULD-CONTAIN-EVERYTHING-THE-SPEAKER-WILL-SAY
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry — the dense-slide/supportive-slide discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from a slide functioning as a felt safety net against forgetting content, mistaken for good visual-aid design when it actually competes with the spoken delivery.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct side-by-side comparison of a dense slide against a supportive one on identical content**, since the fix requires the learner to experience the audience's actual reading-versus-listening behavior, not merely be told dense slides are ineffective.
- **Verification of death**: given two slides on the same content, the learner correctly identifies which supports spoken delivery and explains why.

### MC-A-GOOD-PRESENTATION-COVERS-AS-MUCH-INFORMATION-AS-POSSIBLE
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — the 12-point-superficial/4-point-in-depth discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the genuinely reasonable value placed on thorough preparation and knowledge, extended into treating raw content volume itself as evidence of quality, when audience comprehension and retention actually require selective focus.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct comparison of audience retention between comprehensive-but-shallow and selective-but-deep content**, since the counterintuitive claim (less content, better outcome) is more convincing demonstrated than asserted.
- **Verification of death**: given a large information pool and a specified audience/purpose, the learner selects 3-4 appropriate key points and justifies the exclusions.

## Analogies

**Best — the Suitcase for the Trip, and the Photo Album, Not the Novel** (Blueprint Component 3): an oversized suitcase stuffed with everything imaginable versus a carefully packed one with only what's needed for THIS trip directly seeds the selective-content fix; a photo album (brief, supportive captions) versus a novel (complete, replacing the need to listen) directly seeds the supportive-slide fix.
*Breaking point*: packing a suitcase has an objective, checkable success criterion (did you forget something you needed?), while selecting presentation content involves genuine audience-judgment calls with no single "correct" packing list — useful for the selective-not-comprehensive intuition, less precise for implying content selection is as mechanically verifiable as suitcase-packing.
*Breaking point (photo album)*: a photo album's captions are inherently brief by the medium's own physical constraints, while a slide has no such physical limit forcing brevity — useful for the brief-supportive-caption intuition, less precise for conveying that slide brevity is a deliberate choice, not a forced one.

**Alternative — a movie trailer versus watching the entire film in advance.** A trailer (supportive slide) gives just enough to engage interest and orient the viewer without replacing the actual film (the spoken delivery); watching the whole film beforehand (a full-sentence slide) removes any reason to experience the real thing.
*Breaking point*: a trailer deliberately withholds plot resolution to build anticipation, a goal not directly analogous to a presentation slide's supportive function.

**Visual anchor — the Suitcase for the Trip, and the Photo Album, Not the Novel**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Put everything you're going to say on the slide so you don't forget it."** Directly installs the visual-aid-as-full-script misconception as a stated strategy.
- **"Include as much information as you can to show you really know the topic."** Directly installs the comprehensive-coverage misconception as a stated value.

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both design facts are directly checkable by comparing slide density and content volume against actual audience comprehension.

1. **Need**: "If a slide has everything you're going to say written out, why would the audience bother listening to you?"
2. **Playground**: the learner drafts several candidate slides freely, testing dense versus sparse versions, before being told the official supportive-slide standard.
3. **Invention**: "Given this large pool of facts, can you pick just the 3-4 most important ones for THIS specific audience?"
4. **Collision**: present a 12-point superficial outline and a 4-point in-depth one; ask which the audience remembers better.
5. **Formalization**: name the two design facts — slides support, don't duplicate; selective depth beats comprehensive shallowness — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Support, don't duplicate — and go deep on a few, not shallow on many."

## Voice Teaching Notes

This concept is **inherently a performance-and-design skill** — its core evidence is the learner's actual or narrated presentation delivery alongside their designed slides, not written analysis alone, since selective content and supportive visual design are choices made in constructing and performing an actual presentation.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated design check** ("would the audience read this instead of listening to me?") applied to a new slide draft — the direct behavioral signature of the supportive-slide fix taking hold.
- **A stated exclusion justification** ("I'm leaving out the technical details because this audience needs the practical takeaways") when selecting content — direct evidence the selective-content fix is internalized.
- **A slide containing multiple full sentences, or a presentation attempting to cover far more points than the audience could absorb** — the direct behavioral signature of either misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Support your words, don't duplicate them — and go deep on a few points, not shallow on many."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"I want to put everything on the slide so I don't forget what to say"** — take this as a reasonable safety instinct; respond by naming the actual cost: "If it's all on the slide, the audience reads it instead of listening to you. Keep the slide brief — trust your own key-points outline for the rest."
- **"But I know so much about this topic, shouldn't I share it all?"** — a reasonable, knowledge-signaling instinct; respond by naming the retention cost: "More points means the audience remembers less of each one. What are the 3-4 things THIS audience actually needs?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full structured presentation down to **designing one supportive slide for a single, already-selected key point, with no full-structure or delivery task demanded.** Isolates the most basic visual-design skill before returning to fuller presentation preparation.
- **Never shrink to "just write down everything you want to say on the slide."** This directly licenses the visual-aid-as-script pattern, undermining the supportive-design discipline this concept specifically builds.
