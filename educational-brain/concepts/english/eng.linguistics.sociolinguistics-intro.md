## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify which social factor (class, age, gender, identity) likely explains a documented language variation pattern, distinct from geography.
2. Identify the specific social cue that likely triggered a code-switching example and explain why this demonstrates skill rather than confusion.
3. Identify a register-shifting pattern across two social contexts and the social factor driving it.
4. Explain what identity or group-belonging function a deliberate in-group language use example likely serves beyond pure communication.
5. Fully analyze a new scenario involving language variation: social factor(s), skilled-competence pattern, and any identity/belonging function.

## Core Understanding

This concept synthesizes `eng.phonetics.accents-and-dialects`' non-judgmental framing of regional variation and `eng.linguistics.pragmatics-intro`'s context-dependent meaning into a broader study of social language variation. Two corrections define it. First, **language variation is not only about geographic region** — social factors like class, gender, age, and identity ALSO produce systematic, documented patterns of variation independent of geography; speakers from the same city can systematically differ in language use due to social class or age cohort, not just regional accent. Second, **code-switching and register-shifting do not mean a speaker doesn't fully know either variety** — a bilingual speaker fluidly alternating languages, or a speaker adjusting formality by context, demonstrates full competence in BOTH varieties plus the additional social skill of choosing the right one for the right context; code-switching requires MORE linguistic skill, not less.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "language variation is only about geographic region; code-switching or shifting formality means a speaker doesn't fully know either variety."**
The simulation: assume two speakers from the same place must speak identically, and interpret a speaker alternating languages or formality as confused or incompletely fluent.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — documented social-class-based variation within the same city, asked what's driving the systematic difference if not geography; and a fluidly code-switching bilingual speaker versus a monolingual speaker using one register regardless of context, asked which demonstrates more linguistic and social skill.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Social factors, not just geography, drive systematic variation. Code-switching demonstrates MORE skill, not confusion."

**Intermediate — "I recognize social factors beyond geography now, but I still sometimes assume language mixing reflects incomplete mastery rather than checking for a deliberate, skilled pattern."**
The simulation: correctly attributes variation to social factors but hasn't yet reliably distinguished skilled code-switching from genuine incomplete acquisition.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-2's direct practice identifying the specific social cue triggering a code-switch, confirming it follows a systematic, purposeful pattern.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Check for a triggering social cue — audience, topic, setting. A systematic pattern reveals skill, not confusion."

**Advanced — "I identify social factors beyond geography, recognize code-switching and register-shifting as skilled competence, connect variation to identity/belonging functions, and analyze a new scenario integrating all of this."**
The simulation: integrates social-factor identification, skilled-competence recognition, and identity-function analysis into one coherent sociolinguistic analysis. Matches TA-5.
*Upgrade trigger*: the three direct unlocks — applied linguistics, dialectology (deeper dialect study), and cross-cultural communication (applying these social-variation principles across cultures).
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the social-variation framework. Applied linguistics, dialectology, and cross-cultural communication each extend it in a different practical direction."

**Expert — "at fluency, social-factor recognition and skilled-competence framing happen as an integrated part of any sociolinguistic reasoning."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit factor-competence-identity routine is a scaffold for a habit that, at true fluency, is inseparable from doing sociolinguistics or informed cross-cultural communication.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **`eng.phonetics.accents-and-dialects`' own established framing (regional variation is systematic) can be overgeneralized into "ALL systematic variation is regional,"** since geography is the most concretely visible, easily identifiable variable, while social factors like class or age require more inferential, less immediately observable analysis.

The second mechanism is that **monolingual, single-register speech is the most common everyday experience for many speakers**, so alternating languages or registers looks unusual and, by a "different from the norm" heuristic, gets misread as a deficiency rather than an additional skill layered on top of full competence in both varieties.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-LANGUAGE-VARIATION-IS-ONLY-ABOUT-GEOGRAPHIC-REGION-NOT-SOCIAL-FACTORS
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-A — includes the same-city-social-class discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the prerequisite concept's own genuinely correct finding (regional variation is systematic), extended into assuming region is the ONLY systematic source of variation, when social factors independently produce documented patterns too.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **documented same-region, differing-social-factor evidence**, since the fix requires isolating social factors from geography empirically, not merely asserting other factors exist.
- **Verification of death**: given three documented language variation patterns, the learner identifies which social factor (class, age, gender, identity) likely explains each, distinct from geography.

### MC-B-CODE-SWITCHING-AND-REGISTER-SHIFTING-MEAN-A-SPEAKER-DOESNT-FULLY-KNOW-EITHER-VARIETY
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — includes the fluid-bilingual/rigid-monolingual discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 1 (overgeneralization), of the reasonable observation that struggling with vocabulary in one language reflects incomplete acquisition, over-extended to ALL instances of language alternation, including fluent, deliberate, context-appropriate switching.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct comparison of a genuinely skilled code-switcher against a monolingual, single-register speaker**, since the counterintuitive claim (switching requires MORE skill) is more convincing demonstrated than asserted.
- **Verification of death**: given a code-switching example, the learner identifies the specific social cue that likely triggered the switch and explains why this demonstrates skill rather than confusion.

## Analogies

**Best — the Home-Decoration-Age-Cohort and Bilingual-Musician Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): neighbors in the same city with systematically different home decoration styles correlated with age cohort, not geography, directly seeds the social-factor fix; a bilingual musician fluidly switching between jazz and classical depending on venue, demonstrating skill in both plus contextual judgment, directly seeds the skilled-competence fix.
*Breaking point*: home decoration style is a matter of personal taste with looser social-patterning rigor than documented linguistic variation, while sociolinguistic patterns are empirically measured across large speaker populations — useful for the social-factor-not-just-geography intuition, less precise for conveying the actual rigor of sociolinguistic data collection.
*Breaking point (musician)*: a musician's genre-switching is a deliberate professional skill developed through training, while a bilingual speaker's code-switching is often acquired naturally through bilingual upbringing rather than formal study — useful for the both-varieties-mastered-plus-judgment intuition, less precise for the acquisition pathway.

**Alternative — an athlete who competes in both sprinting and long-distance running, adjusting technique for each.** Excelling at both requires genuine mastery of two different skill sets plus the judgment to apply the right one at the right time — not confusion about how to run.
*Breaking point*: sprinting and distance running are more clearly, physically distinct skill sets than two dialects or registers of the same broader language, which share more underlying structure.

**Visual anchor — the Home-Decoration-Age-Cohort and Bilingual-Musician Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"People who live in the same place should all talk the same way."** Directly installs MC-A as a stated expectation.
- **"If someone switches between languages or ways of talking, they probably don't fully know either one."** Directly installs MC-B as a stated judgment.

Then connect to social-factor-driven language variation; separately, examine the bilingual musician's skill.

3. **The fluid-bilingual/rigid-monolingual collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for MC-B. *Predict first*: "Which speaker is actually demonstrating more linguistic and social skill?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both social facts are directly checkable by isolating social factors from geography and by testing code-switching against a skill-versus-deficiency standard.

1. **Need**: "If two people live in the same city, does that guarantee they speak exactly the same way?"
2. **Playground**: the learner examines several documented variation patterns freely, guessing whether geography or another factor explains each, before being told the official social-factor framework.
3. **Invention**: "Can you find the specific social cue — who they're talking to, what the topic is — that triggers a code-switch in this example?"
4. **Collision**: present a fluidly code-switching bilingual speaker and a rigid monolingual one; ask which shows more skill.
5. **Formalization**: name the two social facts — social factors beyond geography drive systematic variation; code-switching is skilled competence, not confusion — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Social factors shape language too — and switching between varieties takes MORE skill, not less."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Social factors beyond geography (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting MC-A before code-switching content is introduced.
- **Code-switching as skilled competence (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting MC-B once the social-factor framework from TA-1 is established.
- **Register-shifting within one language (TA-3) comes third**, extending TA-2's skilled-competence principle to an analogous, related phenomenon.
- **Language variation and identity/group-belonging (TA-4) and full application (TA-5) come last**, adding the functional, identity-signaling dimension and integrating everything into one synthesis task — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for identifying a social factor or a code-switching trigger**, since a correct-looking attribution with no stated social-factor or trigger reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated non-geographic factor named** ("this pattern looks like it's about age cohort, not where they're from") applied to a new variation example — the direct behavioral signature of the MC-A fix taking hold.
- **A stated skill-recognition framing** ("switching here shows they know both languages well AND when to use each") applied to a new code-switching example — direct evidence the MC-B fix is internalized.
- **A variation pattern attributed purely to region, or a code-switching speaker described as "mixing up" their languages** — the direct behavioral signature of either misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Social factors shape language too, not just geography — and switching between varieties takes real skill."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"They're from the same place, shouldn't they talk the same way?"** — take this as a reasonable extension of the prerequisite's own geographic-variation lesson; respond by widening the lens: "Same place, sure — but could something else, like age or social group, also be shaping how they talk?"
- **"If they keep switching languages, don't they just not know either one well?"** — a reasonable-sounding default given monolingual norms; respond by testing skill: "Let's check — does the switch happen at a specific, meaningful moment (a new audience, a new topic), or randomly? A pattern reveals skill."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full new-scenario analysis down to **identifying a single social factor for one variation pattern, with no code-switching or identity-function task demanded.** Isolates the most basic social-factor recognition before returning to fuller analysis.
- **Never shrink to "just say if this is a regional accent or not."** This directly re-licenses the geography-only pattern this concept specifically works to dismantle.

- **Near**: the three direct unlocks — applied linguistics, dialectology (deeper dialect study), and cross-cultural communication (applying social-variation principles across cultures).
