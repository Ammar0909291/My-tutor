# Compound Sentences — `eng.grammar.compound-sentences`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.compound-sentences` (canonical English
  KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Compound Sentences" —
  sentences formed by joining two or more independent clauses with a
  coordinating conjunction, semicolon, or conjunctive adverb.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.simple-sentences` AND
  `eng.grammar.conjunctions` — load-bearing on two independent fronts:
  simple sentences because compound sentences are formally defined as
  the direct extension of the simple-sentence clause-counting test
  (two independent clauses joined, instead of one standing alone), per
  that concept's own explicit Adaptive Flags mandate; conjunctions
  because two of the three correct joining methods this concept teaches
  (comma + coordinating conjunction; semicolon + conjunctive adverb)
  depend on already knowing which words function as coordinating
  conjunctions or conjunctive adverbs.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.complex-sentences`,
  `eng.grammar.parallel-structure`, `eng.grammar.colons-semicolons-
  dashes`, `eng.grammar.run-on-sentences-and-comma-splices`. Complex
  sentences extend this concept's clause-joining framework to
  independent-plus-dependent combinations; parallel structure applies
  to the grammatically balanced elements this concept's coordinating
  conjunctions join; colons-semicolons-dashes formally extends this
  concept's semicolon-joining method to additional punctuation options,
  per the Blueprint's own explicit "three bridges" reuse mandate;
  run-on-sentences-and-comma-splices is built entirely on recognizing
  the exact comma-splice error pattern this concept's TA-4 establishes.
- **Difficulty**: proficient · **Bloom**: apply · **Mastery threshold**:
  0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Apply the both-sides independent-clause check to "and"/"or"/"but"-
   containing sentences, correctly distinguishing compound sentences
   from simple sentences with compound elements.
2. Correctly join two independent clauses using the comma + coordinating
   conjunction method.
3. Correctly join two independent clauses using a semicolon alone, and
   using a semicolon + conjunctive adverb + comma.
4. Detect and repair comma splices (two independent clauses incorrectly
   joined by a bare comma with no conjunction).
5. Choose the most appropriate joining method for a given pair of
   related clauses and construct original compound sentences using each
   of the three correct methods.

## Core Understanding

A compound sentence requires TWO complete independent clauses joined
together — not merely a sentence that happens to contain "and," "or,"
or "but." This is the exact same both-sides-of-the-conjunction check
already established in the prerequisite simple-sentences concept, now
applied at a harder level where THREE distinct joining methods must all
be checked for correctness, not just conjunction presence. There are
exactly three correct ways to join two independent clauses: (1) a comma
immediately before one of the seven coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS:
for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so); (2) a semicolon ALONE, with no
conjunction added — the semicolon is already strong enough on its own;
(3) a semicolon, followed by a conjunctive adverb ("however,"
"therefore," "moreover," "consequently"), followed by a comma. A second,
equally load-bearing insight concerns what these three methods are NOT:
a bare comma with no conjunction is NEVER sufficient to join two
independent clauses — this specific error is called a comma splice
("The lights flickered, the power went out completely" is incorrect).
Equally, a semicolon never needs a conjunction added right after it —
"; and" stacking a semicolon and a coordinating conjunction together is
also incorrect. Each of the three methods is complete and correct
entirely on its own; they are never combined or substituted for each
other.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "any sentence with 'and'/'or'/'but' is compound,
  and commas and semicolons are interchangeable joining marks"**: the
  arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct — a
  direct repetition, at a harder level, of the exact clause-vs-
  conjunction confusion already addressed in the prerequisite simple-
  sentences concept, now compounded by a second, genuinely new
  confusion between three structurally distinct punctuation methods.
- **Intermediate model — "I check both sides of a conjunction for a
  complete, standalone subject-verb pair before classifying a sentence
  as compound"**: installed by TA-1, using the Component 3 Three
  Bridges anchor, directly extending PD-1's clause-counting skill and
  resolving MC-ANY-CONJUNCTION-CREATES-A-COMPOUND-SENTENCE. Upgrade
  trigger: correctly classifying a fresh compound-verb sentence as
  simple despite containing "and," without hesitation.
- **Advanced model — "I correctly apply the comma+conjunction method
  and the two semicolon methods, treating all three as structurally
  distinct and each complete on its own"**: installed by TA-2 and TA-3,
  beginning to resolve
  MC-A-SEMICOLON-AND-A-COMMA-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE-JOINERS by
  establishing each method's exact, non-interchangeable requirements.
- **Expert model — "I detect and repair comma splices, and I choose the
  best joining method for a given clause pair while constructing
  original compound sentences"**: installed by TA-4 and TA-5, the
  concept's genuine mastery bar, fully resolving
  MC-A-SEMICOLON-AND-A-COMMA-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE-JOINERS by requiring
  the learner to actively identify the specific failure case (a bare
  comma) and actively construct, not merely recognize, correct joins.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing
  (S9), do not certify mastery unless MP-1 (both-sides check) and MP-2
  (comma-splice detection) both pass independently — a learner who can
  construct compound sentences correctly (MP-3) but cannot detect a
  comma splice has not yet learned the full boundary of correctness
  this concept requires, since the immediately downstream
  `eng.grammar.run-on-sentences-and-comma-splices` concept is built
  entirely on this exact detection skill.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here, and the Blueprint
itself explicitly frames the first as a direct repetition of an
already-known challenge at a harder level. The any-conjunction-creates-
compound failure exists for exactly the same reason as its prerequisite-
concept counterpart: "and"/"or"/"but" genuinely IS the most visible
signal of clause-joining, and over-relying on its mere presence is a
reasonable extension of real, partial evidence — now compounded by
three joining METHODS instead of one classification question, raising
the stakes of the same underlying shortcut. The comma-and-semicolon-
are-interchangeable failure exists because both marks genuinely DO
appear in similar clause-joining contexts, and without explicit
instruction on their precise, non-overlapping requirements, treating
them as freely swappable is a reasonable default — nothing marks the
comma's specific weakness (too weak alone) or the semicolon's specific
strength (sufficient alone, never needing a conjunction added) until a
learner directly compares all three correct methods against the broken
comma-splice case.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.compound-sentences.md`, Component 1) documents both
misconceptions this concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/
replacement/discrimination content — reused by reference below, with
birth-type classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-ANY-CONJUNCTION-CREATES-A-COMPOUND-SENTENCE (Blueprint Component
1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature, and explicitly
  identified by the Blueprint itself as "the exact clause-vs-
  conjunction confusion from `eng.grammar.simple-sentences` at a new,
  more advanced level" — the same real, partial evidence (conjunctions
  often DO join independent clauses) drives the same over-generalization,
  now at a harder level where three joining methods must all be
  checked, not just conjunction presence.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown "Ben
  and Sara studied hard and passed the exam," spontaneously marks the
  single compound subject and compound verb before confirming "one
  clause, so simple" is showing the both-sides check has generalized
  from the prerequisite concept to this harder context.

**MC-A-SEMICOLON-AND-A-COMMA-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE-JOINERS (Blueprint
Component 1) — Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — commas and
  semicolons genuinely do both appear in clause-joining contexts, so
  treating them as freely swappable (or assuming a semicolon still
  needs a conjunction the way a comma does) is a reasonable
  generalization absent explicit instruction on their precise,
  distinct requirements; the misconception survives because both
  errors (comma splice and semicolon+conjunction stacking) produce
  sentences that "look approximately right" without a direct, explicit
  three-method comparison.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown "The
  rain stopped, the sun came out," spontaneously identifies the missing
  conjunction (or proposes a semicolon instead) before being told is
  showing the three-method rule has been genuinely internalized.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the Three Bridges itself is the analogy, not a
  supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): two "island" cards, each
  one complete independent clause, connected by three distinct, fully-
  built bridge pieces (comma+conjunction; semicolon alone; semicolon+
  conjunctive-adverb+comma) versus a fourth, deliberately BROKEN bridge
  piece (a bare comma with no conjunction) that visibly collapses,
  directly embodies both the three-methods-are-each-complete insight
  AND the comma-splice failure case as one unified, concrete
  demonstration. Breaking point: the bridges anchor demonstrates the
  three joining methods and the comma-splice error well but doesn't on
  its own address the both-sides independent-clause check (TA-1), which
  needs its own separate, dedicated demonstration extending the
  prerequisite concept's own engine-counting test.
- **Alternative — three different, equally valid ways to lock a door**:
  a deadbolt alone (semicolon alone), a keyed lock plus a chain
  (comma+conjunction), or a keyed lock plus a chain plus a padlock
  (semicolon+conjunctive-adverb+comma) — each is a complete, sufficient
  locking method on its own; using only the chain with nothing else (a
  bare comma) leaves the door insecure. Breaking point: useful for the
  three-complete-methods-plus-one-insufficient-method insight, but
  doesn't address the both-sides check, which the bridges anchor's own
  TA-1 practice handles directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe the semicolon as "a stronger comma"
  or "a weaker period"**: this vague relative framing risks reinforcing
  MC-A-SEMICOLON-AND-A-COMMA-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE-JOINERS by implying a
  sliding scale of strength rather than each mark having its own
  precise, non-overlapping requirements; instead, teach the semicolon
  as its own complete, sufficient method — never needing anything added
  after it for this specific job.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the Three Bridges)**: the Blueprint's Component 3
  anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any two genuinely related, real events or
  facts the learner can state as two separate simple sentences supply
  real TA-2/TA-3 material for joining practice across all three
  methods; any everyday sequence of cause-and-effect supplies real
  material for choosing the appropriate conjunctive adverb (therefore,
  consequently) in TA-3.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-4's comma-splice detection-and-
  repair task is inherently hands-on diagnostic practice, requiring the
  learner to identify a specific failure case and apply one of three
  correct repairs, not merely recognize a stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the collapsing-bridge test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — presenting "The
  rain stopped, the sun came out" (comma splice) alongside "The rain
  stopped; the sun came out" (correct) and "The rain stopped, and the
  sun came out" (correct), asking which are grammatically complete
  joins, making the comma's specific weakness directly audible and
  testable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  classification, joining-method application, or comma-splice-repair
  attempt (TA-1 through TA-5), ask the learner to state their own
  attempt before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
both-sides independent-clause check and the three-methods-are-each-
complete insight are both genuine, directly testable phenomena well-
suited to guided discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured TA-1's
both-sides practice, is shown "The rain stopped, the sun came out" and
asked whether this is a correct compound sentence, discovering their
instinct to accept it feels wrong once each method's precise
requirement is checked); **playground/invention** (TA-1's both-sides
practice and TA-2's comma+conjunction method build initial confidence
with the base classification and one correct joining method before the
semicolon methods and the comma-splice failure case are introduced);
**collision** (the Blueprint's own compound-verb-vs-two-clauses pair
breaks the any-conjunction-creates-compound assumption; the three-
version comma-splice-vs-semicolon-vs-comma+conjunction comparison
breaks the interchangeable-joiners assumption); **formalization**
(MP-5's original-construction task with explicit method justification,
requiring articulation of both insights); **compression** (TA-5's
fluent method selection and original construction, applying both
insights directly without each instance separately re-derived). The
specific terms "coordinating conjunction," "conjunctive adverb," and
"comma splice" are convention-bound grammatical terminology best
introduced by direct instruction once the learner has already felt the
underlying distinctions through the bridges anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (both-sides independent-clause check) is placed first as the
concept's direct extension of the prerequisite simple-sentences
concept's own core test, per the Blueprint's own session architecture —
re-establishing the clause-counting discipline at this harder level
before any joining-method instruction begins. TA-2 (comma +
coordinating conjunction method) teaches the first of three correct
methods in depth. TA-3 (semicolon-alone and semicolon + conjunctive-
adverb methods) teaches the remaining two methods, directly targeting
MC-A-SEMICOLON-AND-A-COMMA-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE-JOINERS by treating all
three methods (now fully taught) as structurally distinct and complete
on their own. TA-4 (detecting and fixing comma splices) is placed next,
addressing the SPECIFIC failure case (a bare comma) once the learner
already knows what a CORRECT join looks like across all three methods —
per the Blueprint's own session architecture, recognizing an error
requires first knowing what correctness looks like. TA-5 (choosing the
best method and constructing original compound sentences) is placed
last as the concept's integration capstone, requiring the learner to
actively select and construct, not merely classify or repair. This
entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script
(Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously checks both
  sides of a fresh conjunction for a complete subject-verb pair before
  classifying (reusing the prerequisite concept's discipline at this
  harder level), and whether the learner spontaneously identifies a
  fresh comma splice as specifically missing a conjunction (rather than
  vaguely "sounding wrong") — these are the primary diagnostic signals
  for whether TA-1 and TA-4's disciplines have genuinely generalized
  beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before classifying a
  fresh "and"-containing sentence, followed by a correct, function-
  based classification, suggests the learner is consciously checking
  both sides — a good sign, directly inherited from the prerequisite
  concept. Instant, confident acceptance of a bare-comma join as
  correct (without checking for a conjunction) is the concerning
  pattern for MC-A-SEMICOLON-AND-A-COMMA-ARE-INTERCHANGEABLE-JOINERS.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: not specifically flagged for L1-
  transfer in this Blueprint — both misconceptions here stem from
  genuine surface-pattern overgeneralization (Type 1) within English's
  own punctuation system rather than L1-specific structural transfer,
  so no S9-specific routing beyond standard practice depth is called
  for.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "is there a complete subject-verb pair
  on both sides, and is the joining method one of the three correct
  ones?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for both
  misconceptions together, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh potentially compound sentence is introduced, making
  the two-part check (clause count, then method correctness) a
  routinely-invited response rather than an afterthought.
