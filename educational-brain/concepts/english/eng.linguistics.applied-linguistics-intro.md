## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Explain that effective language-teaching methods should be grounded in acquisition research findings rather than tradition or intuition alone.
2. Identify at least one way a language test can be unfair if it fails to account for legitimate dialect variation, distinguishing testing a skill from penalizing a dialect.
3. Explain that language policy decisions involve real social trade-offs, not purely neutral technical decisions.
4. Evaluate a new applied-linguistics scenario against the appropriate principle and identify any policy trade-offs involved.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **a language-teaching method's traditional or familiar status does not certify its effectiveness** — methods should be evaluated against actual acquisition-research findings (meaningful input, communicative practice) rather than assumed effective because they're long-standing or match how the learner themselves was taught. Second, **a standardized language test using only one dialect's norms is not automatically a neutral, purely technical measure of skill** — such a test can conflate dialect conformity with the actual skill being measured, unfairly penalizing speakers of other, equally legitimate and systematic dialects for features that reflect dialect difference rather than any genuine deficiency in the underlying ability.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "a teaching method must work if it's traditional, and a standardized test using one dialect must be objective."**
The simulation: endorse rote memorization or grammar-drilling because "that's how everyone learns it," and accept a one-dialect test's results as a neutral, unbiased measure.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a traditional-medicine-testing comparison contrasting evidence-based teaching evaluation with tradition-by-default, and a miscalibrated-height-device comparison showing a one-dialect test's systematic distortion.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Evaluate teaching methods against acquisition research, not tradition — and check whether a test measures the real skill or just dialect conformity."

**Intermediate — "I can now evaluate teaching methods against research and spot dialect-conflating tests, but I still treat language policy as a neutral technical decision."**
The simulation: correctly applies both evidence-based tests but hasn't yet recognized policy's genuine social trade-offs.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct policy-trade-off identification practice, given a hypothetical language-policy scenario.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Language policy isn't purely technical — it involves real competing values and resource trade-offs. What's actually at stake here?"

**Advanced — "I evaluate a new scenario against acquisition research or dialect-fairness principles, and identify genuine policy trade-offs, all together."**
The simulation: integrates evidence-based teaching evaluation, dialect-fairness testing analysis, and policy trade-off recognition into one coherent applied-linguistics judgment. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: `eng.linguistics.bilingualism-and-multilingualism`'s application of these same teaching/testing/policy principles to bilingual individuals and communities specifically.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the general applied-linguistics toolkit. Bilingual and multilingual contexts get their own deeper application next."

**Expert — "at fluency, evaluating teaching, testing, and policy questions against evidence happens as an integrated professional habit, without a separate deliberate checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit evaluate-identify-propose routine is a scaffold for a professional judgment that, at fluency, operates as an integrated habit.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **a teaching method's familiarity (having been taught that way oneself) is a powerful, emotionally-anchored substitute for actual evidence**, so tradition-by-default goes unchallenged until directly confronted with a specific acquisition-research finding it contradicts.

The second mechanism is that **standardized tests carry an aura of objectivity by virtue of being standardized and quantified**, obscuring the fact that a test's CONTENT choices (which dialect's norms to treat as correct) can embed a real, unexamined bias behind that neutral-seeming numerical output.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-ANY-LANGUAGE-TEACHING-METHOD-THAT-FEELS-TRADITIONAL-OR-FAMILIAR-MUST-BE-EFFECTIVE
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from personal experience of having been taught a certain way, which anchors that method as "the normal way" rather than a hypothesis to be tested against research.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **anchor evaluation to a specific acquisition-research principle from the prerequisite concept**, since abstract appeals to "evidence" are less convincing than concrete callback to a finding the learner already accepted.
- **Verification of death**: given two teaching approaches, the learner evaluates each against a specific acquisition-research principle rather than defaulting to familiarity.

### MC-B-A-STANDARDIZED-LANGUAGE-TEST-THAT-USES-ONE-DIALECT-IS-A-NEUTRAL-FAIR-MEASURE-OF-LANGUAGE-SKILL
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from standardized tests' quantified, official-seeming format conferring an unearned appearance of neutrality regardless of the dialect assumptions embedded in their content.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **directly identifying a specific test item that conflates dialect with skill**, since the bias is invisible until a concrete instance makes the conflation observable.
- **Verification of death**: given a test item, the learner identifies whether it risks penalizing a legitimate dialect feature rather than genuinely measuring the intended skill.

## Analogies

**Best — Traditional-Medicine-Testing and Miscalibrated-Height-Device** (Blueprint Component 3): medicine tests traditional remedies against real evidence rather than assuming tradition equals effectiveness — directly seeding the fix for MC-A. A height-measuring device calibrated to only one unit system produces systematically distorted results for anyone using a different, equally valid convention — directly seeding the fix for MC-B.
*Breaking point*: medical evidence-testing has a relatively clean gold-standard (patient outcomes), while "effective teaching" involves multiple, sometimes-competing measures (retention, engagement, transfer) that don't reduce as cleanly to one metric.

**Alternative — a scale that's been zeroed incorrectly.** A bathroom scale reading 5 pounds high for everyone still LOOKS precise and objective, but its calibration error means it's consistently unfair in one direction — just as a one-dialect test's calibration embeds a consistent, hidden bias.
*Breaking point*: a miscalibrated scale's error is uniform and correctable by a single offset, while dialect-conflation in a test is uneven — it affects some test-takers and not others, unlike a scale's flat, universal error.

**Visual anchor — Traditional-Medicine-Testing and Miscalibrated-Height-Device**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"If a teaching method is old and everyone uses it, it must be effective."** Directly installs the tradition-equals-effectiveness misconception as a stated definition.
- **"A standardized test is objective because it's the same test for everyone."** Directly installs the neutral-test misconception as a stated definition.

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by testing candidate methods and test items against acquisition-research principles and dialect-fairness criteria.

1. **Need**: "If a teaching method has been used for a hundred years, does that alone prove it's effective?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely evaluates two teaching approaches before being told the official acquisition-research test.
3. **Invention**: "Can you design a test item that measures a skill without penalizing any dialect feature?"
4. **Collision**: present a test item that marks a legitimate dialect feature as "incorrect" and ask whether it's testing the intended skill.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — teaching methods need evidence, not just tradition; tests must not conflate dialect with skill — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Check teaching methods against research; check tests for dialect conflation."

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for evaluating a method or test, and for identifying a policy trade-off**, since a correct-looking judgment with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated acquisition-research citation** ("this matches/contradicts what we know about meaningful input") applied to a new teaching method — the direct behavioral signature of the evidence-over-tradition fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary identification of a dialect-conflation risk** in a new test item — direct evidence the fairness discipline is internalized.
- **Endorsing a teaching method purely because it's traditional or familiar** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Check teaching methods against research, and check tests for dialect conflation — not just tradition or format."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But that's how I was taught, and I turned out fine"** — take this as an accurate personal observation, not a mistake; respond by adding the stricter test: "That's a real experience, that's true — but does it match what acquisition research shows works best, or could something else have worked even better?"
- **"It's a standardized test, so it must be fair"** — a genuine, reasonable assumption given how standardized tests are presented; respond by naming the additional layer: "Standardized means everyone takes the same test, yes — but does the test's content accidentally penalize a dialect feature instead of measuring the real skill?"
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full applied-linguistics evaluation down to **evaluating one teaching method against one specific research principle, with testing and policy set aside.** Isolates the core evidence-over-tradition discipline before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just go with whatever feels traditional or standard."** This directly licenses both misconceptions, undermining the evidence-based discipline this concept specifically builds.
