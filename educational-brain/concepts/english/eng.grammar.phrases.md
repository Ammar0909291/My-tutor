# Phrases — `eng.grammar.phrases`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.phrases` (canonical English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Phrases" — groups of
  related words functioning as a single grammatical unit without a
  subject-verb pair: noun, verb, prepositional, adjectival, and
  adverbial phrases.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.word-order` — load-bearing because
  phrases are the intermediate structural unit BETWEEN individual words
  and the full SVO sentence frame; a learner cannot recognize a
  multi-word group as functioning like a single noun or verb within a
  sentence without already having a secure sense of what role a single
  noun or verb plays in that sentence's word order.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.clauses`. Clauses are formally
  defined in direct contrast to phrases (presence vs. absence of a
  subject-verb pair) — a learner cannot reliably identify a clause
  without first having phrases secure as the contrasting category.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 3

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify noun phrases and their head noun.
2. Identify verb phrases and their head verb, including phrases built
   from multiple helping verbs.
3. Identify prepositional, adjectival, and adverbial phrases and the
   function each performs.
4. Apply the subject-verb-pair test to correctly distinguish phrases
   from clauses in a mixed set.
5. Identify multiple phrase types within an extended, realistic passage
   of continuous text.

## Core Understanding

A grammatical phrase is not just any string of consecutive words — it
is a group of related words that function TOGETHER as a single unit
(acting as a noun, verb, adjective, or adverb within the larger
sentence), built around a HEAD word that the rest of the phrase
modifies or complements. "The tall building" is a noun phrase because
it functions as one unit doing a noun's job, built around the head
noun "building." A random scrambled word sequence isn't a phrase at
all, regardless of how many consecutive words it contains. A second,
equally load-bearing fact is the defining structural boundary between
phrases and their close relative, clauses: phrases NEVER contain a
subject-verb pair ("in the morning" — no subject, no verb), while
clauses ALWAYS do ("when the sun rises" — "sun" is the subject, "rises"
is the verb). This presence-or-absence-of-a-subject-verb-pair test is
the single, reliable way to distinguish the two categories, since both
are genuinely "groups of words smaller than a full sentence" and easily
conflated on that surface similarity alone.

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "a phrase is any group of a few words, and phrases
  and clauses are basically the same thing"**: the arriving, pre-
  instructional model this concept exists to correct — a natural
  default because "phrase" in everyday, non-grammatical language often
  just means "a few words" (as in "a nice turn of phrase"), and because
  phrases and clauses genuinely do share the surface property of being
  smaller than a full sentence, giving no signal that a stricter,
  structural definition applies in grammar.
- **Intermediate model — "I identify noun and verb phrases by their
  head word and unified function"**: installed by TA-1 and TA-2, using
  the Component 3 phrase-unit-blocks anchor, resolving
  MC-A-PHRASE-IS-JUST-ANY-GROUP-OF-WORDS. Upgrade trigger: correctly
  identifying the head word of a fresh noun or verb phrase without
  hesitation.
- **Advanced model — "I identify prepositional, adjectival, and
  adverbial phrases by their function"**: installed by TA-3, extending
  the head-word-unity test to the remaining three phrase types,
  completing the full five-type functional inventory.
- **Expert model — "I apply the subject-verb-pair test to distinguish
  phrases from clauses, and I identify multiple phrase types in
  extended text"**: installed by TA-4 and TA-5, the concept's genuine
  mastery bar, resolving
  MC-PHRASES-AND-CLAUSES-ARE-THE-SAME-THING by requiring the learner to
  actively check for subject-verb-pair presence rather than relying on
  surface-level "smaller than a sentence" similarity.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 doesn't use a phrase/clause distinction the same
  way, or has a different internal phrase-structure system, expect this
  concept — especially the phrase-vs-clause discrimination (TA-4) — to
  require dedicated, explicit, contrastive teaching, since this
  structural analysis skill is foundational to the immediately
  following clauses concept and does not necessarily transfer directly.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here. The any-group-of-words
failure exists because "phrase" carries a much looser, everyday meaning
outside grammatical instruction — extending that loose meaning into the
grammar classroom is a completely reasonable default, with nothing
marking the stricter, head-word-and-unified-function requirement until a
learner is directly shown a genuinely non-functional word grouping and
asked whether it behaves like a single unit the way "the dog" or
"barked at" does. The phrases-and-clauses-are-the-same failure exists
because both categories genuinely share the surface property of being
"smaller than a full sentence," making them feel like the same kind of
thing — nothing marks the defining subject-verb-pair distinction until a
learner directly contrasts "in the morning" (no subject-verb pair)
against "when the sun rises" (a genuine subject-verb pair) and is asked
to name the structural difference.

## Misconceptions

The existing Blueprint (`docs/curriculum/blueprints/
eng.grammar.phrases.md`, Component 1) documents both misconceptions this
concept produces, with full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/
discrimination content — reused by reference below, with birth-type
classification added
(`../../misconceptions/01-birth-taxonomy.md`).

**MC-A-PHRASE-IS-JUST-ANY-GROUP-OF-WORDS (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 3, language contamination**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: Type 3, not Type 1 — this misconception
  is not an overextension of an English grammatical pattern, but a
  direct carryover of "phrase" as an ordinary English WORD (a loose,
  everyday sense meaning "a few words" or "an expression") into a
  technical grammatical context where the term has a stricter,
  specialized meaning; the everyday and technical senses of the same
  word genuinely diverge, and the learner's first exposure is
  reasonably to the everyday sense.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a
  random, non-functional word grouping, spontaneously says it "doesn't
  really do anything" or "isn't really one thing" before being told is
  showing the head-word-unity test has been genuinely internalized.

**MC-PHRASES-AND-CLAUSES-ARE-THE-SAME-THING (Blueprint Component 1) —
Type 1, overgeneralization**
- *Why (birth-type addition)*: exact Type 1 signature — both categories
  genuinely share the surface property of being smaller than a full
  sentence, so conflating them is a reasonable generalization from that
  real, shared surface feature; the misconception survives until the
  specific, checkable subject-verb-pair distinction is directly
  demonstrated.
- *Full trigger/probe/bridge/replacement/discrimination content*: see
  Blueprint Component 1, verbatim — not restated here.
- *Characteristic phrase (addition)*: a learner who, when shown a fresh
  word group, spontaneously checks for a subject performing an action
  (a subject-verb pair) before classifying it as a phrase or clause is
  showing the defining structural test has generalized beyond the
  specific TA-4 practice items.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the phrase-unit blocks itself is the analogy, not a
  supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically grouping
  related word cards into rubber-banded "unit blocks" (identifying each
  block's head word), then contrasting against a subject-verb pair that
  resists the same block-grouping treatment, directly embodies both the
  head-word-unity test AND the phrase/clause structural distinction as
  a single, concrete, hands-on demonstration. Breaking point: the
  blocks anchor demonstrates noun and verb phrases well but doesn't on
  its own extend to the less visually obvious prepositional/
  adjectival/adverbial phrase types (TA-3), which need their own
  separate function-based practice.
- **Alternative — a single Lego piece vs. a small assembled Lego
  vehicle vs. a fully-drivable toy car**: a random scattered handful of
  Lego pieces (a non-phrase word string) isn't a unit; a small
  assembled sub-structure (a phrase) is a recognizable, functional unit
  built around a core piece, but can't move on its own; a fully-
  drivable toy car (a clause, with its own subject-verb "engine") has
  independent internal motive structure. Breaking point: useful for the
  three-way "not a unit / a unit / a self-sufficient unit" progression,
  but doesn't address the specific five phrase-type functional
  categories, which the unit-blocks anchor's own TA-1–3 practice
  handles directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe clauses as simply "bigger phrases" or
  "phrases with more words"**: this framing directly reinforces
  MC-PHRASES-AND-CLAUSES-ARE-THE-SAME-THING by implying the distinction
  is merely one of size or word count rather than the presence or
  absence of a genuine subject-verb pair; a one-word-longer phrase is
  still a phrase, and a very short clause ("she left") is still a
  clause — length is never the determining factor.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the phrase-unit blocks)**: the Blueprint's
  Component 3 anchor — canonical, referenced not restated.
- **Home/no-equipment**: any sentence the learner can produce about a
  real object or action supplies real TA-1/TA-2 noun-phrase and verb-
  phrase material; any description of location or manner ("under the
  table," "very carefully") supplies real TA-3 material across the
  remaining three phrase types.
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-5's extended-text phrase
  identification is inherently hands-on, requiring the learner to
  actively locate and classify multiple phrase types within continuous
  prose, not merely recognize an isolated stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the subject-verb-pair test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether "in
  the morning" has a subject doing an action, the way "when the sun
  rises" does, making the phrase-vs-clause distinguishing test directly
  audible and testable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  phrase identification, head-word naming, or phrase-vs-clause
  classification attempt (TA-1 through TA-5), ask the learner to state
  their own attempt before the tutor confirms — Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The head-
word-unity test and the subject-verb-pair distinction are both genuine,
directly testable phenomena well-suited to guided discovery: **need**
(the learner, having secured TA-1's noun-phrase practice, is asked
whether a deliberately scrambled, non-functional word string counts as
a phrase too, discovering their instinct to say yes feels wrong once
tested against unified function); **playground/invention** (TA-1's
noun-phrase practice and TA-2's verb-phrase practice build initial
confidence with the most intuitive phrase types before the harder
prepositional/adjectival/adverbial types and the phrase/clause
distinction are introduced); **collision** (the Blueprint's own
scrambled-word-group probe breaks the any-group-of-words assumption;
the "in the morning" vs. "when the sun rises" probe breaks the phrases-
and-clauses-are-the-same assumption); **formalization** (MP-5's own-
words statement of both the head-word-unity definition and the subject-
verb-pair phrase/clause test); **compression** (TA-5's fluent multi-
type phrase identification in extended text, applying the head-word-
unity test directly without each instance separately re-derived). The
specific terms "phrase," "head word," "clause," and the five phrase-
type names (noun/verb/prepositional/adjectival/adverbial) are
convention-bound grammatical terminology best introduced by direct
instruction once the learner has already felt the underlying
distinctions through the unit-blocks anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (noun phrases) is placed first as the concept's most intuitive
entry point, per the Blueprint's own unit-blocks anchor — establishing
the head-word-unity test with the most visually concrete phrase type
before any complication is introduced. TA-2 (verb phrases) follows
immediately, directly targeting
MC-A-PHRASE-IS-JUST-ANY-GROUP-OF-WORDS by requiring identification of
the unifying function across a second, structurally different phrase
type (built from helping verbs, not modifiers). TA-3 (prepositional,
adjectival, and adverbial phrases) extends the same head-word-unity
test to the three remaining, less visually obvious phrase types,
completing the full five-type inventory. TA-4 (distinguishing phrases
from clauses) is placed next, directly targeting
MC-PHRASES-AND-CLAUSES-ARE-THE-SAME-THING, addressing a SECOND,
independent dimension (the phrase/clause boundary, not phrase-internal
classification) once all five phrase types are individually secure.
TA-5 (identifying phrases in extended text) is placed last as the
concept's integration capstone, applying the full five-type
classification fluently within realistic, continuous reading. This
entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-by-turn session script
(Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously identifies
  the head word of a fresh phrase without hesitation, and whether the
  learner spontaneously checks for a subject-verb pair when classifying
  a fresh ambiguous word group (rather than relying on length or
  surface similarity) — these are the primary diagnostic signals for
  whether TA-1/TA-2 and TA-4's disciplines have genuinely generalized
  beyond the specific practice items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before classifying a
  fresh, potentially phrase-or-clause-ambiguous word group, followed by
  a correct, subject-verb-pair-based classification, suggests the
  learner is consciously applying the defining test rather than
  guessing by length — a good sign. Instant, confident classification
  of a genuine clause as "just a longer phrase" (without checking for a
  subject-verb pair) is the concerning pattern for
  MC-PHRASES-AND-CLAUSES-ARE-THE-SAME-THING.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Protocol Routing — for S9 learners whose L1 doesn't
  use the same phrase/clause distinction, reassure that this structural
  analysis skill is foundational for the immediately following clauses
  concept, warranting genuine mastery here rather than surface-level
  familiarity, since transfer cannot be assumed.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "does this word group have a subject
  doing something?" is this concept's load-bearing sentence for
  MC-PHRASES-AND-CLAUSES-ARE-THE-SAME-THING, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh potentially phrase-or-clause-ambiguous word group is
  introduced, making the subject-verb-pair check a routinely-invited
  response rather than an afterthought.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework — a five-type functional
classification system (noun/verb/prepositional/adjectival/adverbial
phrases), each defined by head-word unity, combined with a structural
boundary test (subject-verb-pair presence) distinguishing the whole
category from clauses; mastery is demonstrated by correctly
identifying fresh phrase types with their head words and correctly
discriminating fresh phrases from clauses (MP-1 through MP-4) rather
than by recognizing a fixed, previously-taught example list.
Interleaving partners: mix phrase-type-identification items (testing
TA-1/TA-2/TA-3) with phrase-vs-clause-discrimination items (testing
TA-4) in ongoing review, so the learner continues exercising both the
internal-classification and the category-boundary dimensions of
phrase-level analysis.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: identifying fresh phrases across all five types with their
  head words; correctly discriminating fresh phrases from clauses using
  the subject-verb-pair test.
- **Far**: this concept's structural-unit framework and its phrase/
  clause boundary test are the direct prerequisite for
  `eng.grammar.clauses`'s formal, contrastive definition — a clause is
  defined precisely as everything a phrase structurally is not (the
  presence, not absence, of a subject-verb pair) — making genuine
  mastery here essential, not merely helpful, for that downstream
  concept.
- **Real-world**: every sentence the learner analyzes or constructs —
  in reading comprehension or in writing — depends on recognizing
  phrase-level units to parse meaning correctly and to avoid
  constructing ambiguous or malformed sentences.
- **Expert-transfer**: sophisticated sentence construction and precise
  reading comprehension at any advanced level depend on fluent phrase-
  level structural awareness, since the ability to recognize and
  correctly combine noun, verb, prepositional, adjectival, and
  adverbial phrases underlies more complex sentence-combining and
  clause-embedding skills addressed in later concepts.
