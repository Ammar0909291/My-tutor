## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Explain that competent writing requires multiple passes (drafting, revising, editing) rather than a single, final-quality pass.
2. Distinguish revising (changing content, organization, or clarity of ideas) from editing (fixing surface-level grammar, spelling, and punctuation).
3. Revise a rough draft for content and organization before editing it for surface correctness.
4. Apply the full multi-pass process (draft, revise, edit) to a new piece of writing independently.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **competent writers do not produce a finished, well-organized, clearly-argued piece on a single attempt** — professional and skilled writing is the visible result of an invisible multi-pass process (drafting, revising, editing), and expecting a first draft to already be "done" sets up predictable frustration and a false judgment of one's own ability. Second, **revising and editing are not the same activity** — revising changes the actual content, organization, or clarity of ideas (does this paragraph belong here? is this argument clear?), while editing fixes surface-level correctness (spelling, punctuation, grammar) without touching the underlying content; doing them in the wrong order (editing sentences that get cut in revision) wastes effort, and treating "I fixed the commas" as equivalent to "I revised this" leaves real organizational problems unaddressed.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "a good writer just writes the final version directly; a single pass fixing typos counts as revision."**
The simulation: judge one's own first draft as a failure if it isn't already polished, and consider a comma-and-spelling pass sufficient "revision."
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a visibly messy, heavily-marked-up draft from a skilled writer's actual first attempt, and a content-level reorganization that a spelling-only edit would never catch.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Building the house, then painting it — you don't paint the frame. Draft the structure and content first, revise it, then edit the surface."

**Intermediate — "I now separate revising from editing, but I sometimes still edit before I've finished revising, wasting effort on sentences that get cut."**
The simulation: performs both passes correctly in isolation but sequences them out of order under time pressure.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct sequencing practice, given a draft with both content problems and surface errors, sequencing revision before editing explicitly.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Don't polish a sentence you might delete. Revise the structure and content fully first — then edit what's left."

**Advanced — "I apply the full draft-revise-edit sequence to a new piece of writing independently, in the correct order."**
The simulation: integrates drafting, content-level revision, and surface-level editing as a correctly-sequenced whole. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: `eng.writing.outlining-and-planning`'s pre-writing extension, since planning before drafting completes the full writing-process arc this concept begins mid-process.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you draft-revise-edit. Planning before you draft is its own skill, worth its own attention next."

**Expert — "at fluency, the multi-pass process happens as an integrated writing habit, without deliberately labeling each pass."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit draft/revise/edit sequence is a scaffold for a writing habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated practice.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **school writing tasks are frequently graded on a single submitted draft**, reinforcing the expectation that a first attempt should already be final-quality, with no visible model of the invisible revision work skilled writers actually do.

The second mechanism is that **"revise your paper" is often taught and practiced as a proofreading pass** (check spelling and punctuation), because surface correction is easier to teach and assess than content-level judgment, so the content/surface distinction goes unmodeled until a concept like this one deliberately confronts it.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-GOOD-WRITERS-PRODUCE-A-FINISHED-POLISHED-PIECE-ON-THE-FIRST-ATTEMPT
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 5 (instruction-induced), from school writing tasks typically being graded on a single submitted draft with no visible model of the underlying multi-pass process.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **show the invisible process directly** (a real skilled writer's messy first draft), since the misconception is sustained by never seeing what a first draft actually looks like.
- **Verification of death**: the learner produces a genuinely rough first draft without judging it as a failure, expecting to revise it.

### MC-B-REVISING-AND-EDITING-ARE-THE-SAME-THING-FIXING-SURFACE-ERRORS-COUNTS-AS-REVISING
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 3 (language contamination), from "revise" and "edit" being used loosely interchangeably in casual speech and even some instruction, collapsing a genuine content/surface distinction into one word.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **giving the learner a draft with both content and surface problems**, so the two passes must be performed as visibly different operations rather than distinguished only by definition.
- **Verification of death**: given a rough draft, the learner revises content/organization first, distinctly from a separate later editing pass for surface correctness.

## Analogies

**Best — Building the House, Then Painting It** (Blueprint Component 3): a house's frame and rooms (content and organization) must be built and rearranged before painting the walls (surface correctness) — directly seeding the fix for MC-A (a frame is visibly unfinished before it's built, matching a first draft's incompleteness) and MC-B (you don't paint a wall you're about to move).
*Breaking point*: a house's frame, once built, is not usually torn down and rebuilt the way a paragraph might be deleted entirely during revision — writing's content stage is more fluid and reversible than a physical frame.

**Alternative — sculpting from clay versus carving from stone.** Clay (a draft) can be reshaped, added to, and pulled apart repeatedly before it hardens; only once the form is right does fine surface detailing (editing) make sense.
*Breaking point*: clay eventually hardens on its own schedule, while a writer chooses when to stop revising and start editing — the boundary is a judgment call, not a physical property.

**Visual anchor — Building the House, Then Painting It**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Revising is just checking your paper over once more."** Directly installs the surface-only misconception as a stated definition.
- **"A good writer gets it right the first time."** Directly installs the single-pass misconception as a stated definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **Building the House, Then Painting It (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Would you paint a wall before deciding where the rooms go?"
2. **The messy-first-draft collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Do you think a skilled writer's actual first draft looks finished, or messy?"
3. **The content-vs-surface collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "If you only fix spelling, have you fixed a paragraph that's in the wrong place?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by comparing a genuine multi-draft example against a single-pass assumption.

1. **Need**: "If your first attempt at a paragraph isn't very good, does that mean you're a bad writer, or does that just mean it's a first draft?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely revises a rough paragraph's content before being told the official draft-revise-edit sequence.
3. **Invention**: "What's different about fixing a comma versus moving a whole paragraph to a different spot in the essay?"
4. **Collision**: present a draft with both a comma error and a misplaced paragraph, and ask which fix should happen first.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — writing takes multiple passes, and revising (content) differs from editing (surface) — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Draft it, revise the content, then edit the surface."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Modeling the invisible multi-pass process (TA-1) comes first**, directly confronting the first misconception before the revise/edit distinction is introduced, since accepting that drafts are supposed to be rough is the foundation the rest of the process depends on.
- **Distinguishing revising from editing (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting the second misconception once the multi-pass expectation is established.
- **Sequencing revision before editing (TA-3) comes third**, extending TA-2 into correct ordering practice on a draft with both content and surface problems.
- **Full independent application (TA-4) comes last**, the generative capstone integrating both misconception fixes into one new piece of writing — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reason for a change** — whether they can say whether a given edit changes content/organization or only surface correctness — since a correctly-ordered draft-revise-edit sequence with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from accidental ordering.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated content-versus-surface classification** ("this fix changes what the paragraph says, this one just fixes a comma") applied to a new change — the direct behavioral signature of the revise/edit distinction taking hold.
- **Voluntary acceptance of a messy first draft** without self-judgment, moving straight to revision — direct evidence the multi-pass fix is internalized.
- **Editing performed before revision is complete** (polishing a sentence that later gets deleted) — the direct behavioral signature of MC-B still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Build the house before you paint it — revise the content first, edit the surface last."*

## Assessment Signals

Item bank: Blueprint **Component 6 — Mastery Probe Set** (MP-1…MP-5). Response-pattern reads:

- **Distinguishing revision from editing (MP-1) and sequencing them correctly (MP-2).** Per the Blueprint's S9 routing, mastery must NOT be certified unless both pass — a learner who can define the distinction (MP-1) but sequences edits before revision on a real draft (MP-2) has not internalized the ordering discipline this concept requires.
- **Accepting a rough first draft without self-judgment (MP-3).** Confirms the multi-pass expectation is secure.
- **Revising a draft for content (MP-4).** A harder, generative version of the content-change skill.
- **Full independent draft-revise-edit application (MP-5).** The synthesis item; listen for both fixes applied in correct sequence, not just performed separately.

**Mastery certification trigger**: a correct revise/edit distinction with examples of each; correct sequencing of revision before editing on a real draft; acceptance of a rough first draft as expected, not a failure; a genuine content-level revision of a draft paragraph; a full independent application of the draft-revise-edit sequence to new writing.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But I already fixed it, isn't that revising?"** — take this as an accurate observation about effort spent, not a mistake; respond by adding the stricter test: "You fixed something, that's true — but did it change what the paragraph says, or just how it's spelled? If just spelling, that's editing, not revising."
- **"My first draft is bad, I must not be good at this"** — a genuine, reasonable worry given how school writing is often graded; respond by naming the invisible norm: "Every skilled writer's first draft looks like this. The skill isn't writing it perfectly the first time — it's revising it well."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full draft-revise-edit application down to **classifying five single changes as either 'revision' or 'editing,' with no actual drafting demanded.** Isolates the core distinction before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just fix the spelling and grammar."** This directly licenses the surface-only pattern, undermining the content-versus-surface discipline this concept specifically builds.

- **Near**: `eng.writing.outlining-and-planning`, extending the process backward into pre-writing.
- **Real-world**: revising an email, a resume, or any piece of practical writing across multiple passes rather than sending a first draft; recognizing that any polished creative or professional work (a song, a piece of software, a design) is typically the product of an invisible iterative process.
- **Expert transfer**: the durable skill is **separating content-level judgment from surface-level correctness and sequencing effort accordingly**, the same discipline used in code review (logic first, style second) and design critique (concept first, polish second).
