## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Correctly identify which of the three appeals (ethos, pathos, logos) a given persuasive passage primarily uses, with textual evidence.
2. Explain that most effective persuasive writing combines more than one appeal rather than relying on just one.
3. Distinguish emotional language genuinely connected to a specific claim (pathos) from generic emotional decoration disconnected from the argument.
4. Draft a short persuasive passage that deliberately deploys a specified appeal or combination for a stated audience and purpose.

## Core Understanding

Two corrections define this concept. First, **pathos does not mean simply being emotional or dramatic** — genuine pathos connects a real feeling directly to the SPECIFIC claim being made, so the emotion does actual persuasive work; emotional language disconnected from the argument (a vague, generic sad story with no link to the specific claim) is decoration, not pathos, and if a reader could feel the emotion and still not know what they're being asked to believe or do, the connection is missing. Second, **logos does not mean simply using numbers or statistics** — genuine logos builds a chain of reasoning where evidence logically connects to a conclusion; a number floating near a claim with no explained connection (an irrelevant or mismatched-scope statistic) looks like structure but isn't actually holding anything up, the way a support beam propped in mid-air touches nothing.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "pathos just means adding emotional language, and logos just means adding numbers."**
The simulation: insert an unrelated sad story into an essay simply because it has emotion, and cite an irrelevant or cherry-picked statistic as if its numerical nature alone made the argument logical.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — a passage linking emotion directly to a specific proposed cut compared against generic "how sad" language, and a directly relevant local statistic compared against an irrelevant national average.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A lawyer who cries about 'how sad it all is' convinces no one — a lawyer who makes the jury feel a SPECIFIC piece of evidence is using pathos correctly. And a number floating near a claim with no explained connection is a support beam touching nothing."

**Intermediate — "I can now check whether emotional language and statistics connect to my claim, but I still sometimes default to one appeal exclusively regardless of my audience."**
The simulation: correctly filters disconnected pathos and floating logos but writes entire passages relying on just one appeal even when the audience calls for a different mix.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-4's direct strategic-combination-planning practice, given an audience profile and choosing which one or two appeals would be most effective.
*Shelf-life warning*: "A three-legged stool with one leg missing wobbles — even a strong logos leg doesn't hold up a stool that also needs pathos for this audience."

**Advanced — "I draft a persuasive passage using at least two appeals, deliberately chosen for a stated audience, with each passing its connection test."**
The simulation: integrates the pathos-connection test, the logos-connection test, and strategic combination into one coherent, self-directed persuasive draft. Matches TA-4/WE-3.
*Upgrade trigger*: `eng.composition.rhetorical-devices`'s specific language techniques executing these appeals, and `eng.composition.persuasive-techniques`'s applied combinations in real persuasive writing.
*Shelf-life warning*: "This gives you the three levers themselves. The specific techniques that pull each lever, and full applied combinations, come next."

**Expert — "at fluency, choosing and connecting appeals to a specific claim and audience happens as an integrated part of ordinary persuasive writing, without a separate deliberate checklist."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit identify-connect-combine routine is a scaffold for a persuasive-writing habit that, at fluency, operates as an integrated part of composition.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **emotional language and numerical data are each genuinely, visibly present regardless of whether they're actually doing persuasive work**, so their mere presence is easy to mistake for the appeal itself, since the surface feature (emotion, a number) is far more salient than the harder-to-verify underlying connection to the specific claim.

The second mechanism is that **each of the three appeals is memorable as an independent, named category** (ethos, pathos, logos), which can encourage treating them as a checklist of ingredients to include rather than tools requiring deliberate, audience-specific selection and combination.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-PATHOS-MEANS-JUST-BEING-EMOTIONAL-OR-DRAMATIC
*(Blueprint Component 1, first entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 4 (notation-induced), from emotional language's visible presence being mistaken for the underlying persuasive connection it's meant to signal.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair must **draw a literal arrow from the emotional sentence to the specific claim it's supposed to support**, since the connection requirement is far more convincing demonstrated through a direct link-test than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given two passages using emotional language on the same topic, the learner determines which passes the "connected to the claim" test.

### MC-B-LOGOS-MEANS-JUST-USING-NUMBERS-OR-STATISTICS
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 4 (notation-induced), from a statistic's visible, quantified presence being mistaken for the underlying logical connection it's meant to signal.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **completing an explicit "this number matters to my claim because..." sentence**, since the reasoning-connection requirement is far more convincing demonstrated by attempting to state it than asserted abstractly.
- **Verification of death**: given two passages using statistics on the same topic, the learner determines which passes the "because" bridge test.

## Analogies

**Best — the Three-Legged Stool Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): a stool missing one leg, or with one leg much shorter, wobbles or tips — an argument leaning entirely on one appeal is similarly unstable, and a decorative pathos leg or a floating logos leg LOOKS like a leg but isn't load-bearing, directly seeding fixes for both misconceptions.
*Breaking point*: a stool's three legs are functionally interchangeable structural supports, while ethos, pathos, and logos serve genuinely different persuasive functions (credibility, emotion, logic) rather than three identical load-bearing roles, a difference the interchangeable-legs framing understates.

**Alternative — a legal case's three types of supporting testimony (expert witness, victim impact statement, physical evidence).** An expert witness builds credibility (ethos), a victim impact statement connects emotion to the specific harm (pathos), and physical evidence provides logical proof (logos) — a case relying only on one type is weaker than one combining all three appropriately.
*Breaking point*: courtroom testimony operates under strict rules of relevance and admissibility that formally enforce the connection requirement, while ordinary persuasive writing has no such external enforcement, making the discipline more self-imposed than in a courtroom setting.

**Visual anchor — the Three-Legged Stool Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"Just add some emotional language to make it persuasive."** Directly installs the pathos-is-just-emotion misconception as a stated definition.
- **"Include a statistic and your argument becomes logical."** Directly installs the logos-is-just-numbers misconception as a stated definition.

## Demonstrations

Prediction first in every case.

1. **The Three-Legged Stool Anchor (learner activity).** Blueprint Component 3 — full script there. *Predict first*: "Does a stool missing one leg (or with a much shorter one) stay stable?"
2. **The pathos-connection collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the first misconception. *Predict first*: "Which passage actually makes you more likely to agree with a specific position, and which just makes you feel bad in general?"
3. **The logos-connection collision (teacher-led, learner-resolved).** Blueprint's own conflict evidence for the second misconception. *Predict first*: "Which statistic actually gives you a reason to believe the claim, and which is just a number sitting nearby?"

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both facts are directly checkable by drawing connection-tests from emotional or numerical content to the specific claim.

1. **Need**: "If a passage makes you feel sad but you can't say what specific position it's arguing for, has it persuaded you of anything?"
2. **Playground**: the learner freely identifies which appeal three passages use before being told the official connection tests.
3. **Invention**: "Can you write a statistic-filled sentence where the number doesn't actually support the claim at all?"
4. **Collision**: present a relevant local statistic and an irrelevant national average on the same claim, and ask which one actually reasons to the conclusion.
5. **Formalization**: name the two structural facts — pathos requires emotion connected to the specific claim, and logos requires evidence explicitly reasoned to the claim — from the learner's own testing.
6. **Compression**: "Draw the arrow from emotion to claim; complete the 'because' from number to claim."

## Teaching Sequence

- **Identifying ethos (TA-1) comes first**, introducing the least misconception-laden appeal before the two flawed ones are addressed.
- **Identifying pathos, connected to claim (TA-2) comes second**, directly confronting the first misconception.
- **Identifying logos, connected to claim (TA-3) comes third**, directly confronting the second misconception.
- **Combining appeals strategically (TA-4) comes last**, the generative capstone integrating both connection tests into deliberate, audience-aware combination — matching this program's established sequencing principle of reserving the most integrative task for the culminating stage.

In recommended order, from `../teaching-actions/`:

1. **Demonstration** (SHOW) — the Three-Legged Stool anchor and both collision scenarios.
2. **Matching** (DO) — TA-1's ethos-move identification across three passages is a classification action.

**Does not fit**: **Game** — the careful, audience-sensitive judgment this concept requires could be undermined by speed-pressured competition. **Role-Play** — while persuasion has a social dimension, the actual skill (connecting an appeal to a claim in writing) is better served by direct drafting practice than improvised dialogue.

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated connection-test application when identifying or producing an appeal**, since a correctly-labeled appeal with no stated reasoning cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated arrow-drawing check** ("does this emotional sentence connect to my specific claim, or is it just floating nearby?") applied to a new passage — the direct behavioral signature of the pathos-connection fix taking hold.
- **Voluntary completion of a "this number matters to my claim because..." sentence** before using a statistic — direct evidence the logos-connection fix is internalized.
- **Inserting emotional language or a statistic with no stated link to the specific claim** — the direct behavioral signature of MC-A or MC-B still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Draw the arrow from emotion to claim — and complete the 'because' from number to claim."*

- **Appeal identification (MP-1).** Failure indicates basic appeal-recognition (TA-1 through TA-3) needs review.
- **Pathos connection test (MP-2).** Failure alone indicates residual MC-A.
- **Logos connection test (MP-3).** Failure alone indicates residual MC-B.
- **Strategic combination planning (MP-4).** Failure with MP-1 through MP-3 passing indicates a strategic-planning gap (TA-4) rather than a recognition gap.

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But this part has real emotion in it, isn't that pathos?"** — take this as an accurate observation about the emotional content, not a mistake; respond by adding the connection test: "It does have real emotion, that's true — but if I cover up your claim, does this emotional sentence still tell me what you want me to believe or do?"
- **"I put in a statistic, so this part should be logos"** — a genuine, reasonable assumption given the numerical content; respond by naming the additional layer: "You did include a number, yes — but can you finish the sentence 'this number matters to my claim because...'? If not, it's not doing logical work yet."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full persuasive draft down to **determining which of two passages passes the connection test for a single appeal, with strategic combination and drafting set aside.** Isolates the most basic connection-testing skill before returning to fuller tasks.
- **Never shrink to "just add some feelings and some numbers."** This directly licenses both misconceptions, undermining the connection-testing discipline this concept specifically builds.
