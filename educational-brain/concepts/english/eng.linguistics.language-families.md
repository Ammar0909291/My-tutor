## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Explain that language family classification is based on systematic, regular sound correspondences across many words, not superficial word-similarity.
2. Distinguish borrowed words from genuine cognates using the presence or absence of a systematic correspondence pattern.
3. Explain why a shared writing system or geographic proximity alone does not indicate genealogical relationship.
4. Classify scenarios (shared script/different families, different scripts/same family, neighboring/unrelated) by actual genealogical relationship rather than surface features.
5. Fully evaluate whether a new dataset's evidence supports a genuine language family relationship.

## Core Understanding

This concept applies `eng.linguistics.historical-linguistics-intro`'s systematic-change principle as the evidentiary basis for classifying language families. Two corrections define it. First, **a single similar-sounding word between two languages does not prove genealogical relationship** — similar-sounding words can arise from borrowing (adopting a word from an unrelated language) or pure coincidence; genuine genealogical relationship requires SYSTEMATIC, regular sound correspondences across MANY words, established through the comparative method, not one matching pair. Second, **a shared writing system or geographic proximity does not by itself indicate a genealogical language family relationship** — these are historically and culturally contingent facts entirely separate from genealogical descent; languages from different, unrelated families can share a script through cultural contact or colonization, and genuinely related languages can use entirely different scripts today. Both corrections require the learner to weigh actual systematic linguistic evidence over surface-level resemblance or convenience.

## Mental Models

**Beginner — "any two languages with similar-sounding words must be related; languages using the same writing system or in the same region are automatically in the same family."**
The simulation: conclude relationship from one matching word, and assume shared script or geography implies shared genealogy.
*Upgrade trigger*: the Blueprint's own conflict evidence — two unrelated languages sharing one similar-sounding word by coincidence versus a systematic correspondence pattern across dozens of words between genuinely related languages, asked what each actually proves; and two unrelated languages sharing a writing system alongside two related languages using different scripts, asked whether script tells us anything about genealogy.
*Shelf-life warning*: "One matching word proves nothing — look for systematic patterns across many words. Script and geography are separate from genealogy."

**Intermediate — "I look for systematic patterns now, but I still sometimes let writing system or geographic closeness influence my judgment about whether two languages are related."**
The simulation: correctly requires multiple correspondences before concluding relationship but retains a residual, unexamined pull toward treating script/geography as supporting evidence.
*Upgrade trigger*: TA-3's direct classification practice across three script/geography/genealogy combination scenarios.
*Shelf-life warning*: "Ignore script and geography entirely when judging genealogical relationship — only the correspondence evidence counts."

**Advanced — "I distinguish systematic correspondence from coincidence, distinguish cognates from borrowings, disregard script and geography as evidence, and evaluate a full dataset for genuine family relationship."**
The simulation: integrates correspondence analysis, cognate/borrowing distinction, and script/geography disregard into one coherent evaluative judgment. Matches TA-4.
*Upgrade trigger*: none within this concept — confirmed terminal, completing the historical-linguistics sequence (etymology → phonology → historical linguistics → language families).
*Shelf-life warning*: "This completes the systematic-change-to-classification arc this sequence has been building."

**Expert — "at fluency, correspondence-based evaluation and script/geography disregard happen as an integrated part of any comparative linguistic reasoning."**
Named to mark the arc; the explicit correspondence-testing and scenario-classification routine is a scaffold for a habit that, at true fluency, is inseparable from doing historical/comparative linguistics at all.

## Why Students Fail

The dominant mechanism is that **a single, striking similar-sounding word is a highly salient, memorable piece of "evidence"** that feels compelling on its own, while the actual requirement (checking dozens of words for a systematic pattern) is effortful and far less immediately convincing to intuition — the same salience-versus-rigor mismatch this program has identified in other evidence-evaluation concepts.

The second mechanism is that **writing system and geography are the two most visible, immediately observable facts about a language** (you can see the script; you know where it's spoken), while the comparative method's actual evidence (systematic sound correspondence) requires specialized linguistic analysis invisible to casual observation — making the visible-but-irrelevant facts an easy, if incorrect, default proxy.

## Misconceptions

The Blueprint's **Component 1 — Misconception Register** owns trigger signals, conflict evidence, bridge/replacement text, and discrimination pairs for both entries below. Reused by reference; birth-type classification and teaching consequence added here.

### MC-A-ANY-TWO-LANGUAGES-WITH-SIMILAR-SOUNDING-WORDS-MUST-BE-RELATED
*(Blueprint Component 1, MC-A — includes the coincidental-match/systematic-correspondence discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from a single striking similarity being a highly salient, immediately noticeable piece of apparent evidence, mistaken for sufficient proof when the actual evidentiary standard (systematic correspondence across many words) requires much more.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **direct contrast of a genuine coincidental match against a genuine systematic pattern**, since the fix requires recalibrating what counts as sufficient evidence, not merely being told single matches are insufficient.
- **Verification of death**: given two sets of word pairs, the learner identifies which set shows a systematic, regular sound correspondence pattern supporting a genuine family relationship claim.

### MC-B-LANGUAGES-USING-THE-SAME-WRITING-SYSTEM-OR-LOCATED-IN-THE-SAME-REGION-ARE-AUTOMATICALLY-IN-THE-SAME-LANGUAGE-FAMILY
*(Blueprint Component 1, second entry — includes the shared-script-different-families/different-scripts-same-family discrimination pair.)*
- **Birth type**: Type 2 (perceptual intuition), from writing system and geography being the most visible, immediately observable language facts, mistaken for evidence of the invisible, historically deeper genealogical relationship.
- **Teaching consequence**: the repair requires **concrete real-world counterexamples in both directions** (unrelated-but-same-script; related-but-different-script), since the fix requires demonstrating the independence of these facts from genealogy in both logical directions, not just one.
- **Verification of death**: given a scenario describing two languages' writing systems and geographic locations, the learner explains why neither fact alone indicates genealogical relationship.

## Analogies

**Best — the Coincidental-Surname and Shared-Currency-Not-Federation Anchor** (Blueprint Component 3): two unrelated people sharing a rare surname purely by coincidence directly seeds the single-match-insufficient fix; two countries sharing a currency without being part of the same political federation directly seeds the script/geography-independence fix.
*Breaking point*: a surname's coincidental match is a single, discrete fact easily recognized as insufficient once named, while linguistic correspondence patterns require actual technical analysis across many data points to distinguish from coincidence — useful for the single-match-insufficient intuition, less precise for conveying the actual technical rigor of the comparative method.
*Breaking point (currency)*: currency adoption is a conscious, documented political/economic choice, while writing-system adoption is often a matter of much older cultural/religious contact whose specific history may be less precisely documented — useful for the independence-from-deeper-relationship intuition, less precise for the historical mechanism itself.

**Alternative — two unrelated recipes that happen to use the same spice.** Two cuisines from entirely different culinary traditions might both use cumin, without that shared ingredient indicating any deeper historical culinary relationship between the two traditions as a whole.
*Breaking point*: culinary traditions don't have an equivalent to the comparative method's rigorous, systematic verification process, making the analogy looser for conveying HOW linguists actually establish genuine relationship.

**Visual anchor — the Coincidental-Surname and Shared-Currency-Not-Federation Anchor**, as the primary teaching metaphor and working instrument.

### ANTI-ANALOGIES (do not use)

- **"If two languages have a similar word for something, they're probably related."** Directly installs MC-A as a stated inference rule.
- **"Languages spoken in the same region or using the same alphabet are usually related."** Directly installs MC-B as a stated inference rule.

## Discovery Questions

Well suited to **guided discovery**, since both evidentiary facts are directly checkable by comparing datasets of word pairs and script/geography scenarios against actual genealogical relationships.

1. **Need**: "If two languages both have a word that sounds like 'mama' for mother, does that prove they're related?"
2. **Playground**: the learner examines several word-pair datasets freely, guessing which show genuine relationship, before being told the official comparative-method standard.
3. **Invention**: "Can you find a systematic sound-correspondence pattern across at least five word pairs in this dataset?"
4. **Collision**: present two languages sharing a script but from different families; ask what the shared script actually proves.
5. **Formalization**: name the two evidentiary facts — genuine relationship requires systematic correspondence across many words, not one match; script and geography are separate from genealogy — from the learner's own dataset analysis.
6. **Compression**: "Look for the pattern across many words, not one match — and ignore script and geography entirely."

## Voice Teaching Notes

Core evidence is **the learner's stated reasoning for accepting or rejecting a proposed family relationship**, since a correct-looking classification with no cited correspondence pattern (or with cited but irrelevant script/geography reasoning) cannot be distinguished from a lucky guess or the misconception itself.

What the ideal tutor perceives:

- **A stated demand for multiple corresponding word pairs** before accepting a relationship claim ("that's one word — do we see the same pattern in other words too?") — the direct behavioral signature of the correspondence fix taking hold.
- **Explicit dismissal of script or geography as irrelevant** when evaluating a genealogical claim ("they use the same alphabet, but that doesn't tell us if they're actually related") — direct evidence the script/geography-independence fix is internalized.
- **A relationship conclusion drawn from a single similar word, or influenced by shared script/geography** — the direct behavioral signature of either misconception still active.

**Load-bearing sentence, delivered slowly**: *"Look for the pattern across many words — script and geography tell you nothing about genealogy."*

## Tutor Recovery Strategy

Generic engine: `../foundations/01-recovery-engine.md`. Concept-specific notes only:

- **"But these two words sound so similar, they must be related"** — take this as a reasonable reaction to a genuinely striking similarity; respond by raising the evidentiary bar: "That's one word — let's check if the same kind of sound pattern shows up across many other words too."
- **"They use the same alphabet, so they're probably from the same family"** — a reasonable-sounding surface inference; respond by naming the independence: "Writing systems spread through trade and culture, completely separately from genealogical descent. Let's check the actual sound-correspondence evidence instead."
- **Smaller question to shrink to**: from a full dataset evaluation down to **classifying a single pair of word sets (one coincidental, one systematic) with no borrowing-distinction or script/geography task demanded.** Isolates the most basic evidentiary standard before returning to fuller evaluation.
- **Never shrink to "just see if the words sound alike."** This directly re-licenses the single-match-sufficiency pattern this concept specifically works to dismantle.
