# Sentence Types by Function — `eng.grammar.sentence-types-by-function`

## Identity

- **Concept ID**: `eng.grammar.sentence-types-by-function` (canonical
  English KG)
- **Curriculum location**: English / grammar / "Sentence Types by
  Function" — the four functional sentence types (declarative,
  interrogative, imperative, exclamatory), classified by grammatical
  structure and communicative purpose rather than by ending punctuation
  alone.
- **Prerequisites**: `eng.grammar.word-order` — load-bearing because
  recognizing sentence type by function depends directly on recognizing
  DEVIATIONS from the baseline SVO order that concept established —
  most concretely, interrogative sentences formed by subject-auxiliary
  INVERSION are, by definition, a departure from standard SVO order,
  which a learner cannot recognize as a departure without first having
  the baseline order secure.
- **Unlocks** (from KG): `eng.grammar.question-formation`,
  `eng.grammar.negation`, `eng.grammar.capitalization-rules`.
  Question-formation formally extends this concept's interrogative
  category into the mechanics of producing both question types;
  negation depends on recognizing declarative vs. imperative structure
  to correctly place negation markers; capitalization rules depend on
  recognizing sentence-type boundaries (a new sentence, regardless of
  type, requires capitalization) that this concept's classification
  skill directly supports.
- **Difficulty**: developing · **Bloom**: understand · **Mastery
  threshold**: 0.75 · **Est. hours**: 2

## Learning Objective

The learner can:
1. Identify declarative sentences (statements) and their standard
   period ending.
2. Identify BOTH types of interrogative sentences — wh-word questions
   and yes/no questions formed by subject-auxiliary inversion without a
   question word.
3. Identify imperative sentences (commands/requests), including those
   with an implied "you" subject, regardless of politeness level or
   punctuation.
4. Identify exclamatory sentences (strong emotion) and distinguish them
   from similarly-punctuated declarative or imperative sentences.
5. Classify a mixed set of sentences by underlying grammatical function
   rather than by ending punctuation alone, including cases where
   punctuation could mislead.

## Core Understanding

A sentence's TYPE is defined by its grammatical structure and
communicative PURPOSE — not by which punctuation mark happens to appear
at the end. "Stop!" and "Stop." are both imperative (commands);
punctuation changes the emphasis, not the underlying function. There
are four functional types: DECLARATIVE sentences state a fact or
opinion (ending typically in a period); INTERROGATIVE sentences ask a
question, and — this is the concept's second load-bearing insight —
they can be formed in TWO genuinely different ways: with a wh-word at
the start ("What is your name?") OR by inverting the subject and
auxiliary/helping verb with NO question word at all ("Are you coming?"
"Did she leave?"); IMPERATIVE sentences give a command or make a
request, typically with an implied "you" subject ("Close the door" =
"[You] close the door"); EXCLAMATORY sentences express strong emotion.
Punctuation is a useful, supporting SIGNAL for these functions in the
common case, but it is never the sole determinant — the underlying
grammatical structure and communicative purpose is what actually
defines the type, and the two can diverge (a command punctuated with a
period is still a command; "What a beautiful sunset!" starts with
"What" but is exclamatory, not interrogative, because it isn't
genuinely asking a question).

## Mental Models

- **Beginner model — "the punctuation mark at the end tells you the
  sentence type, and questions always start with a question word"**:
  the arriving, pre-instructional model this concept exists to correct
  — a natural default because punctuation IS a highly visible, easy-to-
  check signal that correctly predicts sentence type in the common
  case, and because wh-questions are typically the first, most visually
  distinctive question type a learner meets.
- **Intermediate model — "I identify declarative sentences and both
  types of interrogative sentences, including inversion-only yes/no
  questions"**: installed by TA-1 and TA-2, using the Component 3 four-
  function sorting-boxes anchor, resolving
  MC-QUESTIONS-ALWAYS-START-WITH-A-QUESTION-WORD. Upgrade trigger:
  correctly classifying a fresh yes/no question (no wh-word present) as
  interrogative without hesitation.
- **Advanced model — "I identify imperative sentences, including those
  with an implied subject, and exclamatory sentences expressing strong
  emotion"**: installed by TA-3 and TA-4, beginning to resolve
  MC-PUNCTUATION-MARK-ALONE-DEFINES-SENTENCE-TYPE by directly
  demonstrating a case where punctuation and function diverge (a
  command with period-ending punctuation is still a command).
- **Expert model — "I classify a mixed set of sentences by function,
  including cases where punctuation could actively mislead"**: installed
  by TA-5, the concept's genuine mastery bar — fully resolving
  MC-PUNCTUATION-MARK-ALONE-DEFINES-SENTENCE-TYPE by requiring the
  learner to override a misleading punctuation cue in favor of the
  underlying structural/functional analysis.
- **Do not upgrade early**: per the Blueprint's own Protocol Routing, for
  S9 learners whose L1 forms questions very differently (a question
  particle rather than word-order inversion, or intonation-only
  question marking), expect TA-2's subject-auxiliary inversion pattern
  specifically to require dedicated, explicit, contrastive teaching —
  English's yes/no-question inversion structure is a distinctive,
  often non-intuitive feature, not something to assume transfers
  automatically.

## Why Students Fail

Two independent failure mechanisms operate here. The punctuation-alone
failure exists because ending punctuation genuinely IS a highly visible,
easy-to-check, and usually CORRECT predictor of sentence type — over-
relying on it is a reasonable strategy built on real, mostly-accurate
partial evidence, with nothing marking the exception cases
(punctuation/function mismatch) until a learner is directly shown a
sentence like "Stop." and asked whether it's still a command despite
lacking an exclamation point. The questions-need-a-question-word
failure exists because wh-questions are typically the first, most
visually distinctive question type a learner meets (the question word
sits right at the front, unmissable) — extending "questions start with
a question word" to ALL questions is a reasonable generalization until
a learner is directly shown a genuine question like "Are you coming?"
that has no wh-word at all, formed instead by inverting the subject and
auxiliary verb.

## Analogies

- **Best analogy — the four-function sorting boxes itself is the
  analogy, not a supplement to it** (Blueprint Component 3): physically
  sorting sentence cards into "Tells," "Asks," "Commands," "Exclaims"
  boxes by FUNCTION — including deliberately punctuation-mismatched
  cards like "Stop." and "Stop!" sorted into the same box — directly
  embodies function-over-punctuation as a concrete, checkable sorting
  task rather than an abstract classification rule. Breaking point:
  the sorting-boxes anchor demonstrates the four-function classification
  well but doesn't on its own address the dual-question-formation
  insight (TA-2), which needs its own separate wh-word-vs-inversion
  contrast.
- **Alternative — a person's job title vs. what they're actually doing
  right now**: a name tag (punctuation) usually matches what a person
  is actually doing (function), but not always — someone wearing a
  "Manager" badge could still be sweeping the floor in an emergency; you
  check what they're DOING, not just their badge, to know their true
  role in that moment. Breaking point: useful for the punctuation-vs-
  function distinction, but doesn't address the dual-question-formation
  insight, which the sorting-boxes anchor's own TA-2 practice handles
  directly.
- **ANTI-ANALOGY — do NOT describe inversion-formed yes/no questions as
  "informal" or "shortened" versions of wh-questions**: this framing
  directly reinforces MC-QUESTIONS-ALWAYS-START-WITH-A-QUESTION-WORD by
  implying wh-questions are the "real" or "complete" question form and
  inversion questions are a lesser, derived shortcut; both are equally
  legitimate, equally common, structurally independent question-
  formation strategies.

## Demonstrations

- **Physical/visual (the four-function sorting boxes)**: the Blueprint's

- **Home/no-equipment**: any genuine statement, question, request, or
  emotional reaction from the learner's own day supplies real material
  for all four TA categories; any household rule or request supplies
  real TA-3 implied-subject imperative material ("Close the door" =
  "[You] close the door").
- **Interactive/learner-activity**: TA-5's mixed-set classification task
  (including deliberately punctuation-mismatched sentences) is
  inherently hands-on judgment practice, requiring the learner to
  actively override a misleading surface cue, not merely recognize a
  stated fact.
- **Teacher-demo (the still-a-command test)**: the tutor models the
  Blueprint's own conflict-evidence demonstration — asking whether
  "Stop." (period ending) is still giving a command, making the
  function-over-punctuation test directly audible and testable.
- **Prediction to elicit FIRST, every time**: before confirming a
  sentence-type classification attempt (TA-1 through TA-5), ask the
  learner to state their own attempt before the tutor confirms —
  Universal Principle 3.

## Discovery Questions

**A brief, argued discovery step suits both misconceptions.** The
function-over-punctuation test and the dual-question-formation insight
are both genuine, directly testable phenomena well-suited to guided
discovery: **need** (the learner, having secured TA-1's declarative
identification, is asked whether "Stop." is a command, discovering
their punctuation-based instinct feels wrong once the underlying
function is examined); **playground/invention** (TA-1's declarative
practice and TA-2's wh-question practice build initial confidence with
the most visually clear-cut cases before the harder ones are
introduced); **collision** (the Blueprint's own "Stop." vs. "Stop!"
probe breaks the punctuation-alone assumption; the "Are you coming?"
probe breaks the questions-need-a-question-word assumption);
**formalization** (MP-5's own-words statement of both the four
functional types and the punctuation-is-secondary principle);
**compression** (TA-5's fluent mixed-set classification, applying the
function-over-punctuation discipline directly without each instance
separately re-derived). The specific terms "declarative,"
"interrogative," "imperative," and "exclamatory" are convention-bound
grammatical terminology best introduced by direct instruction once the
learner has already felt the underlying distinctions through the
sorting-boxes anchor and TA-1–3.

## Teaching Sequence

TA-1 (declarative sentences) is placed first as the concept's most
stable, unambiguous entry point, per the Blueprint's own sorting-boxes
anchor — establishing the most straightforward functional category
before any complication is introduced. TA-2 (interrogative sentences,
both types) follows, directly targeting
MC-QUESTIONS-ALWAYS-START-WITH-A-QUESTION-WORD by presenting wh-
questions and inversion-only yes/no questions together as equally
legitimate. TA-3 (imperative sentences) introduces the implied-subject
structural feature, connecting back to the subject-and-predicate
foundation. TA-4 (exclamatory sentences) follows, directly targeting
MC-PUNCTUATION-MARK-ALONE-DEFINES-SENTENCE-TYPE — per the Blueprint's
own session architecture, this conflict-evidence probe is placed right
after TA-4, once all four base categories are individually secure and
a genuine punctuation/function mismatch can be meaningfully
demonstrated. TA-5 (classifying mixed sentence sets by function, not
punctuation alone) is placed last as the concept's integration
capstone, requiring the learner to apply the full four-way
classification while actively overriding potentially misleading
punctuation cues. This entry does not restate the Blueprint's own turn-
by-turn session script (Component 7) — see Blueprint References below.

## Voice Teaching Notes

- **What to listen for**: whether the learner spontaneously classifies
  a fresh inversion-only yes/no question as interrogative (rather than
  hesitating or misclassifying it for lacking a wh-word), and whether
  the learner spontaneously classifies a punctuation-mismatched
  sentence by its underlying function rather than its ending mark —
  these are the primary diagnostic signals for whether TA-2 and TA-4's
  disciplines have genuinely generalized beyond the specific practice
  items.
- **Characteristic hesitations**: a brief pause before classifying a
  punctuation-mismatched sentence, followed by a correct, function-
  based classification, suggests the learner is consciously checking
  structure/purpose rather than defaulting to the punctuation mark — a
  good sign. Instant, confident classification based purely on the
  ending punctuation mark (without checking function) is the concerning
  pattern for MC-PUNCTUATION-MARK-ALONE-DEFINES-SENTENCE-TYPE.
- **Native-intuition-gap stakes**: real and specifically named in the
  Blueprint's own Protocol Routing — for S9 learners whose L1 forms
  questions via a particle or intonation alone rather than word-order
  inversion, reassure that English's subject-auxiliary inversion
  structure is a genuinely distinctive feature requiring dedicated,
  explicit, contrastive practice, not an assumed intuitive transfer.
- **The load-bearing sentence**: "is this actually asking something, or
  telling/commanding/exclaiming something?" is this concept's load-
  bearing sentence for
  MC-PUNCTUATION-MARK-ALONE-DEFINES-SENTENCE-TYPE, per
  `../../foundations/03-voice-first-learning-model.md §3` — asked
  whenever a fresh potentially punctuation-mismatched sentence is
  introduced, making the function check a routinely-invited response
  rather than an afterthought.

## Memory Hooks

**Concept type**: fact/framework — a four-category functional
classification system (declarative/interrogative/imperative/
exclamatory), with an internal two-way split within the interrogative
category (wh-word vs. inversion), applied to genuinely novel sentences;
mastery is demonstrated by correctly classifying fresh, previously-
unseen sentences — including deliberately punctuation-mismatched ones
(MP-2 through MP-4) — rather than by recognizing a fixed, previously-
taught example list. Interleaving partners: mix all four functional
types together in ongoing review sentence sets (rather than blocking
practice by type), so the learner continues exercising the active
function-identification judgment rather than defaulting to whichever
type was most recently practiced.

## Transfer Connections

- **Near**: classifying fresh sentences by function across all four
  types, including inversion-only interrogatives and punctuation-
  mismatched sentences.
- **Far**: this concept's functional-classification framework is the
  direct prerequisite for `eng.grammar.question-formation`'s formal
  extension of the mechanics behind both interrogative-formation
  strategies, `eng.grammar.negation`'s dependence on recognizing
  declarative vs. imperative structure for correct negation-marker
  placement, and `eng.grammar.capitalization-rules`'s dependence on
  recognizing sentence-type boundaries for correct capitalization.
- **Real-world**: every statement, question, command, or exclamation
  the learner produces or interprets — in speech or writing — offers
  immediate, ungated opportunities to apply correct functional
  classification and, correspondingly, correct punctuation.
- **Expert-transfer**: precise, purposeful communication at any advanced
  level depends on correctly matching sentence structure to intended
  function — using the right question-formation strategy, correctly
  signaling a command's implied subject, or correctly punctuating a
  sentence to match its true function — a discrimination that remains
  relevant at every proficiency level, including professional and
  academic writing.
