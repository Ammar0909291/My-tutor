## Component 1 — Learning Objectives

- LO1: Define the **equalizer** of $f,g:A\to B$ as an object $E$ with a morphism $e:E\to A$ satisfying $f\circ e=g\circ e$, **universal** among all such morphisms (any other $e':E'\to A$ with $f\circ e'=g\circ e'$ factors uniquely through $e$), and recognize this as precisely the limit of the two-parallel-arrows diagram already introduced in `math.cat.limits`.
- LO2: Compute the equalizer of two specific functions $f,g:A\to B$ in $\mathbf{Set}$ as $E=\{a\in A : f(a)=g(a)\}$ with $e$ the inclusion map, verifying universality directly for a given example.
- LO3: Define the **coequalizer** as the dual (colimit) construction, compute it in $\mathbf{Set}$ as the quotient of $B$ by the equivalence relation generated by $\{(f(a),g(a)):a\in A\}$, and correctly identify why coequalizer is NOT simply "the set of $b\in B$ where something holds" the way the equalizer is a subset — it is a genuinely different (quotient, not sub-) construction.

## Component 2 — Prerequisite Check

Assumes mastery of `math.cat.limits` (cones, universal properties, and the specific claim — previewed there — that the equalizer is the limit of a two-parallel-arrows diagram shape).

## Component 3 — Core Explanation

The **equalizer** of two parallel morphisms $f,g:A\to B$ is an object $E$ together with a morphism $e:E\to A$ such that $f\circ e=g\circ e$ (i.e., $e$'s image lands where $f$ and $g$ "agree"), and this pair is **universal**: for any other object $E'$ with $e':E'\to A$ satisfying $f\circ e'=g\circ e'$, there is a UNIQUE morphism $u:E'\to E$ with $e\circ u=e'$. This is exactly the limit (as defined in `math.cat.limits`) of the diagram shape consisting of two objects and two parallel arrows between them.

**In $\mathbf{Set}$**: given $f,g:A\to B$, the equalizer is $E=\{a\in A : f(a)=g(a)\}$ — the subset of $A$ where $f$ and $g$ genuinely agree — with $e:E\hookrightarrow A$ the inclusion map. Universality here says: any set $E'$ mapping into $A$ such that $f$ and $g$ agree on its image must factor through this particular subset $E$ — $E$ is the LARGEST such subset, captured canonically rather than by ad hoc description.

**Coequalizer (the dual construction)**: the coequalizer of $f,g:A\to B$ is the COLIMIT of the same diagram shape — an object $Q$ with a morphism $q:B\to Q$ such that $q\circ f=q\circ g$, universal among such (every other such map from $B$ factors uniquely through $q$). In $\mathbf{Set}$, the coequalizer is $Q=B/{\sim}$, the **quotient** of $B$ by the smallest equivalence relation $\sim$ containing every pair $(f(a),g(a))$ for $a\in A$ (and then closed under reflexivity, symmetry, transitivity), with $q$ the canonical quotient map $b\mapsto[b]$.

**Equalizer is a SUBSET; coequalizer is a QUOTIENT — genuinely dual, not the same construction "read backwards" naively**: the equalizer restricts $A$ (via $e$, INTO $A$) to where $f,g$ already agree; the coequalizer collapses $B$ (via $q$, OUT OF $B$) by forcing $f(a)$ and $g(a)$ to become equal for every $a$. One narrows a domain; the other identifies points in a codomain — dual operations, not mirror images of the same computation.

## Component 4 — Worked Examples

**Example 1 (LO1/LO2 — computing an equalizer directly, in $\mathbf{Set}$)**: Let $f,g:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$, $f(x)=x^2$, $g(x)=3x-2$. The equalizer is $E=\{x\in\mathbb{R} : x^2=3x-2\}=\{x : x^2-3x+2=0\}=\{1,2\}$ (factoring $(x-1)(x-2)=0$), with $e:E\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}$ the inclusion of the two-point set $\{1,2\}$. Verify universality informally: any set $E'$ mapping into $\mathbb{R}$ such that $f,g$ agree on its image can only hit values in $\{1,2\}$ (since those are the ONLY places $f,g$ agree), so the map necessarily factors through $E=\{1,2\}$.

**Example 2 (LO3 — computing a coequalizer directly, breaking MC-1)**: Let $A=\{*\}$ (one point), $B=\{1,2,3\}$, and $f,g:A\to B$ with $f(*)=1,g(*)=2$. The coequalizer identifies $1\sim2$ (the pair $(f(*),g(*))=(1,2)$), giving equivalence classes $\{1,2\}$ and $\{3\}$ — so $Q=\{[1{=}2],[3]\}$, a TWO-element set (NOT three, and notably NOT a subset of $B$ at all — it is a genuinely different set, a quotient). The quotient map $q:B\to Q$ sends $1,2\mapsto[1{=}2]$ and $3\mapsto[3]$.

**Example 3 (LO1 — equalizer as literally the limit already defined, closing the loop from `math.cat.limits`)**: Recall from `math.cat.limits` that a limit is a universal cone over a diagram. For the two-parallel-arrows diagram shape $J$ (two objects $\bullet\rightrightarrows\bullet$ with $f,g$ as the two arrows between them), a cone over the composite diagram $D=(f,g):A\to B$ consists of an object $C$ with maps into BOTH diagram objects compatible with $f,g$ — but since $C\to B$ is determined by composing $C\to A$ with either $f$ or $g$ (and these must agree, forcing $f\circ e=g\circ e$), a cone here reduces EXACTLY to the equalizer data: an object with one map into $A$ satisfying $f\circ e=g\circ e$. This is not an analogy — it is the SAME universal-cone definition, specialized to this one diagram shape.

## Component 5 — Teaching Actions

### Teaching Action A01 — Equalizer as a Specific Limit, Computed in `Set` (Primitive P11: Representation Shift)

State: "you already know what a limit is — the equalizer is simply the limit of the two-parallel-arrows diagram shape, which `math.cat.limits` previewed but didn't fully compute." Work Example 3's identification directly (cone data reduces to equalizer data), then work Example 1's concrete $\mathbf{Set}$ computation.

- **MC-2 hook**: ask "is the equalizer just 'some subset of $A$ where $f$ and $g$ happen to agree,' described any way you like, or is it a SPECIFIC canonically-determined subset?" — an answer treating "any agreeing subset" as equally valid reveals MC-2 (missing that universality picks out the LARGEST/canonical such subset, not an arbitrary one).

### Teaching Action A02 — Coequalizer as a Genuinely Dual (Quotient, not Subset) Construction (Primitive P06: Contrast Pair)

**Contrast (targets MC-1)**: place Example 1's equalizer (a SUBSET of $A$, via inclusion $e$) directly beside Example 2's coequalizer (a QUOTIENT of $B$, via the surjection $q$) — count elements explicitly: equalizer $E=\{1,2\}\subseteq\mathbb{R}$ has the SAME kind of elements as $A$; coequalizer $Q=\{[1{=}2],[3]\}$ has DIFFERENT elements from $B$ (equivalence classes, not points of $B$). State: "the coequalizer is not 'the equalizer but for the other diagram direction' in any naive sense — it is a quotient construction, categorically dual, but computationally and conceptually a different kind of object."

### Teaching Action A03 — Mastery Gate (Primitive P91)

P91 = P77 → P55 → P76 → P55 → P75 → P55 → P74 → P55 → P78.

- **P77 (4-problem set)**:
  1. Compute the equalizer of $f(x)=2x$ and $g(x)=x+3$ as functions $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$.
  2. Compute the coequalizer of $f,g:\{*\}\to\{1,2,3,4\}$ with $f(*)=2,g(*)=4$, listing the resulting equivalence classes explicitly.
  3. Explain, using Example 3's argument, why the equalizer is exactly the limit of the two-parallel-arrows diagram, rather than merely being ANALOGOUS to a limit.
  4. Explain why the coequalizer of $f,g:A\to B$ is a subset neither of $A$ nor of $B$ in general, contrasting explicitly with the equalizer (a subset of $A$).
- **P76 (Transfer Probe, mode = independence)**: "In group theory, the coequalizer of two group homomorphisms $f,g:H\to G$ (in the category of groups) is closely related to the quotient of $G$ by the normal subgroup generated by all elements of the form $f(h)g(h)^{-1}$ for $h\in H$. (a) Using this lesson's coequalizer definition, explain informally why this quotient construction plays the same 'collapse $f(h)$ and $g(h)$ together' role that the coequalizer plays in $\mathbf{Set}$. (b) Explain why a group-theoretic coequalizer needs a NORMAL subgroup specifically (rather than an arbitrary subgroup or an arbitrary equivalence relation, as in $\mathbf{Set}$) — connecting to the general categorical fact that coequalizers must respect the ambient category's structure (here, groups), not merely produce an unstructured quotient set."
- **P55 (Score)** after each stage.

## Component 6 — Misconception Registry

| ID | Label | Description | Severity |
|---|---|---|---|
| MC-1 | COEQUALIZER-TREATED-AS-EQUALIZER-MIRRORED | Believing the coequalizer is computed the same way as the equalizer (a subset where something holds), rather than recognizing it as a genuinely different quotient construction | Foundational |
| MC-2 | EQUALIZER-TREATED-AS-ARBITRARY-AGREEING-SUBSET | Believing any subset of $A$ where $f,g$ happen to agree qualifies as "the" equalizer, missing that universality picks out one canonical (the largest, all-encompassing) such subset | Moderate |
| MC-3 | EQUALIZER-NOT-RECOGNIZED-AS-A-LIMIT-INSTANCE | Treating the equalizer as an unrelated new definition rather than recognizing it as the specific limit of the two-parallel-arrows diagram shape already covered in `math.cat.limits` | Moderate |

### Protocol B — Repair Actions

- **B01 (targets MC-1)**: P27 (name it: "Coequalizer Treated as Equalizer Mirrored") → P41 (detect: ask a student to compute a coequalizer by describing "the subset of $B$ where $f,g$ agree") → P64 (conceptual shift: re-walk Example 2's quotient construction, counting elements to show the result is not a subset of $B$).
- **B02 (targets MC-2)**: P27 (name it: "Equalizer Treated as Arbitrary Agreeing Subset") → P41 (detect: ask whether $\{1\}\subset\{1,2\}$ — a proper subset of the true equalizer from Example 1 — could also be called "an equalizer") → P64 (conceptual shift: re-derive universality's uniqueness-of-factoring requirement, showing only the full agreeing set satisfies it).
- **B03 (targets MC-3)**: P27 (name it: "Equalizer Not Recognized as a Limit Instance") → P41 (detect: ask what diagram shape the equalizer is the limit of) → P64 (conceptual shift: re-walk Example 3's cone-data-reduces-to-equalizer-data argument).
