## 1. Concept Spine

Group 2 elements do NOT all react vigorously with water like Group 1 metals — the reactivity trend is genuinely PROGRESSIVE, not uniform: Be does NOT react with cold water at all (protective BeO layer), Mg reacts very slowly with cold water but vigorously with steam, Ca reacts steadily with cold water, and only Sr and Ba react vigorously — "Group 2 reacts vigorously with water" overgeneralizes from the heaviest members to the whole group; sulphate solubility DECREASES down Group 2 (MgSO₄ very soluble→BaSO₄ essentially insoluble), the OPPOSITE trend from hydroxide solubility (which INCREASES down the group, Mg(OH)₂ sparingly soluble→Ba(OH)₂ soluble) — these two trends genuinely diverge because the anion's size determines which energy term (lattice or hydration) changes more steeply as the cation grows; and smaller cations do NOT always produce higher-thermal-stability salts via "higher lattice energy" — for carbonates/nitrates, it is specifically ANION-DESTABILIZING cation POLARIZATION that governs decomposition temperature, and SMALLER, higher-charge-density cations (like Mg²⁺) polarize and DESTABILIZE the carbonate anion MORE, causing LOWER decomposition temperatures (MgCO₃~540°C vs. BaCO₃~1360°C) — high lattice energy and high decomposition temperature do NOT track together here.

## 2. Four-Stage CPA+ Mental Model

**Concrete**: Comparing Be+cold water (no reaction, protective oxide layer) against Ba+cold water (vigorous reaction) explicitly, making the progressive (not uniform) reactivity trend concrete across the group.

**Representational**: A two-panel solubility-trend diagram showing hydroxide solubility increasing down the group (Mg(OH)₂→Ba(OH)₂) alongside sulphate solubility decreasing down the group (MgSO₄→BaSO₄), visually contrasting the opposite directions.

**Abstract**: The general principle that reactivity/solubility/stability trends down a group can be progressive rather than uniform, and can genuinely diverge in direction for different anion types; the general anion-destabilization-via-cation-polarization mechanism governing carbonate/nitrate thermal decomposition, distinct from lattice-energy-based reasoning.

**Transfer**: Given an unfamiliar Group 2 element/water reaction, correctly predicting the progressive reactivity trend rather than assuming uniform vigor; given an unfamiliar Group 2 salt, correctly predicting solubility trend direction based on anion identity (hydroxide vs. sulphate-type); given an unfamiliar Group 2 carbonate/nitrate, correctly predicting thermal stability from cation polarizing power, not lattice energy alone.

## 3. Why Beginners Fail

Students, having learned that Group 1 alkali metals react vigorously and increasingly so down the group with water, extend this same "reacts vigorously, increasingly so down the group" pattern uniformly to Group 2 without checking the actual progression, missing that Group 2's reactivity with water is genuinely graded from essentially no reaction (Be) through slow (Mg, cold water) to vigorous (Sr, Ba) — a much more varied progression than Group 1's more uniformly vigorous behavior; students, having correctly learned that hydroxide solubility increases down Group 2 (a commonly emphasized trend), assume this same directional trend must apply to ALL Group 2 salts including sulphates, missing that sulphate solubility actually DECREASES down the group — a genuinely opposite trend driven by the different anion's size relative to the lattice-energy/hydration-energy balance; and students, having learned that lattice energy generally correlates with "stability" in other contexts, assume smaller cations (which produce higher lattice energies) should always produce more thermally stable salts, missing that for carbonate/nitrate decomposition SPECIFICALLY, the governing factor is the small cation's stronger POLARIZING effect on the anion (destabilizing it, making decomposition easier/lower-temperature), a mechanism that runs COUNTER to the naive "higher lattice energy=more stable" expectation.

## 4. Misconception Library

### MC-1: All Group 2 elements react vigorously with water like Group 1 metals
- **Probe**: "What happens when Mg metal is added to cold water?"
- **Characteristic phrase**: "Group 2 elements all react with water quickly."
- **Trigger (Type 1, overgeneralization)**: Overgeneralization from Group 1's more uniformly vigorous water reactivity, applied without checking Group 2's actually more varied progression.
- **Conflict evidence [P28]**: Be does NOT react with cold water at all (protective BeO layer). Mg reacts very slowly with cold water but vigorously with steam. Ca reacts steadily with cold water; only Sr and Ba react vigorously. The trend is progressive; it is NOT uniform "vigorous" across the group.
- **Bridge [P30]**: Group 1's water reactivity happens to be uniformly vigorous across essentially the whole group (with only modest variation in intensity), which can create an expectation that ALL alkali/alkaline-earth-type metals behave this way — but Group 2's specific combination of factors (protective oxide layers for the lightest members, genuinely lower reactivity per unit charge for smaller cations) produces a much more GRADED reactivity progression, ranging from essentially inert (Be) to vigorous (Ba), rather than Group 1's more consistently vigorous pattern.
- **Replacement [P31]**: Group 2's reactivity with water is progressive (Be~inert, Mg slow, Ca steady, Sr/Ba vigorous) — never assume uniform vigorous reactivity across the whole group.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]**: Be+cold water (no reaction, protective oxide layer) vs. Ba+cold water (vigorous reaction) — same group, dramatically different reactivity.
- **S6 repair path**: Present the explicit element-by-element reactivity progression, having the student trace the trend from Be to Ba.

### MC-2: Sulphate solubility increases down Group 2, like hydroxide solubility
- **Probe**: "Which is more soluble: MgSO₄ or BaSO₄?"
- **Characteristic phrase**: "solubility increases down Group 2 for all salts."
- **Trigger (Type 1, overgeneralization)**: Overgeneralization from the correctly-learned hydroxide solubility trend to all Group 2 salts, without checking anion-specific behavior.
- **Conflict evidence [P28]**: Hydroxide solubility INCREASES down Group 2 (Mg(OH)₂ sparingly soluble; Ba(OH)₂ soluble). Sulphate solubility DECREASES down Group 2 (MgSO₄ very soluble; BaSO₄ essentially insoluble). The two trends go in OPPOSITE DIRECTIONS because the size of the anion affects which energy term (lattice or hydration) changes more steeply.
- **Bridge [P30]**: Solubility trends down a group depend on the RELATIVE rates of change of two competing energy terms (lattice energy and hydration energy) as the cation grows — and which term dominates this competition depends specifically on the ANION's own size: for a SMALL anion (OH⁻), lattice energy changes more steeply as cation size varies, giving one trend direction; for a LARGER anion (SO₄²⁻), the balance shifts, and hydration-energy changes dominate instead, producing the OPPOSITE trend direction — the anion identity, not a single universal rule, determines trend direction.
- **Replacement [P31]**: Always check the SPECIFIC anion before predicting a Group 2 solubility trend direction — hydroxide solubility increases down the group, but sulphate solubility decreases, and these are genuinely opposite trends, not a single universal pattern.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]**: Hydroxides (solubility increases down group, Mg(OH)₂→Ba(OH)₂) vs. sulphates (solubility decreases down group, MgSO₄→BaSO₄) — opposite trends for different anion types.
- **S6 repair path**: Present the explicit lattice-energy/hydration-energy balance argument for both anion types, deriving the opposite trend directions.

### MC-3: Smaller ions always make salts with higher thermal stability because the lattice energy is higher
- **Probe**: "Which decomposes at a lower temperature: MgCO₃ or BaCO₃? Explain using polarisation."
- **Characteristic phrase**: "small ion → strong lattice → more stable salt → higher decomposition temperature."
- **Trigger (Type 5, instruction-induced)**: Lattice energy is often generically associated with "stability" without specifying which specific property (melting point vs. thermal decomposition resistance) it actually governs.
- **Conflict evidence [P28]**: For anion-destabilising effects, the smaller the CATION, the MORE it polarises and DESTABILISES the anion in the salt. MgCO₃ (small Mg²⁺, high charge density) decomposes at ~540°C; BaCO₃ (large Ba²⁺, low charge density) decomposes at ~1360°C. High lattice energy and high decomposition temperature do NOT always go together: it is ANION DESTABILISATION, not lattice energy, that determines carbonate/nitrate thermal stability.
- **Bridge [P30]**: Thermal DECOMPOSITION of a carbonate/nitrate salt is governed by a genuinely different mechanism than simple lattice-energy-based melting-point reasoning — a small, high-charge-density cation POLARIZES the large, polarizable carbonate/nitrate anion, distorting its electron distribution toward instability (facilitating the anion's breakdown into a smaller oxide anion plus CO₂/NO₂ gas), and this destabilizing polarization effect is STRONGER for smaller cations, meaning smaller cations actually PROMOTE (not resist) thermal decomposition, the opposite consequence from what a naive lattice-energy argument would predict.
- **Replacement [P31]**: For carbonate/nitrate thermal stability specifically, smaller cations polarize and destabilize the anion MORE, LOWERING decomposition temperature — never assume "smaller cation=higher lattice energy=more thermally stable" for this class of decomposition reaction.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]**: MgCO₃ (small, polarizing Mg²⁺, anion destabilized, decomposes at low temperature~540°C) vs. BaCO₃ (large, weakly polarizing Ba²⁺, anion less destabilized, decomposes at high temperature~1360°C) — smaller cation, LOWER thermal stability, opposite the naive lattice-energy expectation.
- **S6 repair path**: Present the explicit cation-polarization-destabilizes-anion mechanism, deriving the (counterintuitive) inverse relationship between cation size and decomposition temperature.

## 5. Explanation Library

**Primary explanation**: Group 2's water reactivity is a genuinely progressive trend (Be essentially inert, through Mg's slow reaction, to Ba's vigorous reaction), unlike Group 1's more uniformly vigorous behavior — never assume uniform reactivity across the group. Solubility trends depend specifically on the anion involved: hydroxide solubility increases down the group, while sulphate solubility decreases, these opposite directions arising from which energy term (lattice vs. hydration) dominates for each specific anion size.

**Secondary explanation (anion-destabilization governs carbonate/nitrate thermal stability)**: Carbonate and nitrate thermal decomposition temperature is governed by cation-polarization-induced anion destabilization, not simple lattice energy — smaller, higher-charge-density cations polarize and destabilize the anion more strongly, actually LOWERING decomposition temperature, the opposite consequence from a naive "smaller cation=stronger lattice=more stable" expectation.

## 6. Analogy Library

- **Primary analogy**: A dimmer switch (Group 2's progressive water reactivity) rather than an on/off switch (Group 1's more uniform vigor) — reactivity genuinely turns up gradually across the group, rather than being uniformly "on."
- **Breaking point**: The dimmer-switch analogy conveys the progressive-reactivity concept well but doesn't naturally capture the anion-dependent solubility-trend divergence (MC-2) or the polarization-driven thermal-stability inversion (MC-3) — those need the explicit lattice/hydration-energy balance argument and the cation-polarization mechanism.
- **Anti-analogy**: Do NOT say "Group 2 behaves just like Group 1, just a bit less reactive overall" — this directly reinforces MC-1 by implying a uniformly-scaled-down version of Group 1's pattern rather than a genuinely graded progression.

## 7. Demonstration Library

- **Demonstration 1 (element-by-element Group 2 water-reactivity progression)**: Present the explicit Be→Mg→Ca→Sr→Ba reactivity trend, tracing the progression from inert to vigorous.
- **Demonstration 2 (hydroxide-vs-sulphate solubility-trend comparison)**: Present both trends explicitly side by side, deriving the opposite directions from the lattice/hydration-energy balance argument.
- **Demonstration 3 (cation-polarization-destabilizes-anion diagram for MgCO₃ vs. BaCO₃)**: Present the explicit polarization mechanism, deriving the inverse cation-size/decomposition-temperature relationship.

## 8. Discovery Lesson

**Opening**: "Does magnesium react with cold water as vigorously as sodium (Group 1) does?"

**Exploration**: Students trace the explicit Group 2 water-reactivity progression, discovering it's genuinely graded, not uniformly vigorous.

**Synthesis**: Guide toward: Group 2's reactivity/solubility/stability trends must be checked specifically, never assumed uniform or directly analogous to Group 1.

**Closure**: "Does a smaller cation always make a carbonate salt more thermally stable?" (Directly resolves MC-3.)

## 9. Teaching Actions (session_cap = 4)

- **TA-1 (SHOW)**: Present the explicit Group 2 water-reactivity progression from Be to Ba.
- **TA-2 (TELL)**: State the anion-dependent solubility-trend divergence explicitly, anchored to the hydroxide-vs-sulphate comparison.
- **TA-3 (DO)**: Student predicts decomposition temperature ranking for an unfamiliar set of Group 2 carbonates from cation-polarization reasoning.
- **TA-4 (TEST-THINKING)**: Present the MgCO₃-vs-BaCO₃ probe and ask the student to justify the counterintuitive thermal-stability trend.

## 10. Voice Teaching

Whenever Group 2 reactivity is discussed, narrate "check the specific element — the trend is progressive, not uniform." Whenever a Group 2 solubility or stability trend is predicted, state "check the specific anion — trends can genuinely diverge in direction" as the standing reminder.

## 11. Assessment

**Mastery gate**: Student can (a) correctly predict the progressive water-reactivity trend across Group 2, (b) correctly predict opposite solubility trends for hydroxides vs. sulphates, (c) correctly predict carbonate/nitrate thermal stability from cation-polarization reasoning.

- **FA-1**: "What happens when Mg metal is added to cold water?" — targets MC-1.
- **FA-2**: "Which is more soluble: MgSO₄ or BaSO₄?" — targets MC-2.
- **FA-3**: "Which decomposes at a lower temperature: MgCO₃ or BaCO₃? Explain using polarisation." — targets MC-3.

**Confidence calibration**: Predict high-confidence-wrong answers on MC-3 among students who have only encountered lattice-energy-based "stability" reasoning in other contexts (like melting point) without exposure to the anion-destabilization mechanism.

**Delayed retrieval**: Re-probe MC-1's progressive reactivity trend and MC-3's anion-destabilization mechanism as foundational knowledge for subsequent industrial (Plaster of Paris, CaO/Ca(OH)₂) and inorganic-trends applications.

## 12. Recovery Notes

- **S3 (stuck)**: For the uniform-reactivity confusion, have the student explicitly trace the element-by-element progression before making any group-wide claim.
- **S4 (frustrated)**: Normalize — extending Group 1's uniform pattern to Group 2 is genuinely common on first exposure, since the two groups are studied in close succession.
- **S6 (collision)**: Use the explicit lattice/hydration-energy balance argument for MC-2; use the cation-polarization mechanism for MC-3.
- **S9 (post-repair check)**: Ask the student to explain, unprompted, why MgCO₃ decomposes at a much lower temperature than BaCO₃.
