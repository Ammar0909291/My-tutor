## Component 1 — Learning Objectives

- LO1: State the **two basis conditions** — (i) every point lies in some basis element, (ii) for any $x \in B_1 \cap B_2$ with $B_1,B_2\in\mathcal{B}$ there exists $B_3\in\mathcal{B}$ with $x\in B_3\subseteq B_1\cap B_2$ — and explain why these guarantee a well-defined topology where $U$ is open iff every point of $U$ is in some basis element contained in $U$.
- LO2: **Verify** a given collection is a basis; determine when two bases generate the **same topology** (iff each basis element of one is a union of basis elements of the other, equivalently: each basis element of $\mathcal{B}_1$ is covered by $\mathcal{B}_2$ and vice versa).
- LO3: Construct the **standard bases** for $\mathbb{R}^n$ (open balls), the order topology on a linearly ordered set (open intervals $(a,b)$), and the product topology ($\pi_\alpha^{-1}(U_\alpha)$ finite intersections); use the basis comparison criterion to show that the lower-limit topology $\mathcal{B}_{LL}=\{[a,b):a<b\}$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is **strictly finer** than the standard topology.

## Component 2 — Prerequisite Check

Assumes mastery of `math.top.topological-space` (the three open-set axioms, the notions of open and closed sets, subspace topology). No metric space or distance notion is assumed — the basis formalism is purely set-theoretic.

## Component 3 — Core Explanation

**Definition.** A **basis** for a topology on $X$ is a collection $\mathcal{B}$ of subsets of $X$ (called basis elements) satisfying:
- (B1) For every $x\in X$, there exists $B\in\mathcal{B}$ with $x\in B$.
- (B2) If $x\in B_1\cap B_2$ with $B_1,B_2\in\mathcal{B}$, there exists $B_3\in\mathcal{B}$ with $x\in B_3\subseteq B_1\cap B_2$.

The **topology generated by $\mathcal{B}$**: $U$ is open iff for every $x\in U$ there exists $B\in\mathcal{B}$ with $x\in B\subseteq U$. Equivalently, every open set is a union of basis elements; and $\mathcal{B}$ itself is a basis for the topology $\mathcal{T}=\{\bigcup_\alpha B_\alpha : B_\alpha\in\mathcal{B}\}$.

**Key points**: A basis is NOT itself required to be a topology (it need not be closed under arbitrary unions or contain $X$ explicitly — those properties follow automatically from B1–B2 for the generated topology). However, every topology is a basis for itself.

**Basis comparison.** If $\mathcal{B}_1$ and $\mathcal{B}_2$ are bases on the same set $X$, then $\mathcal{T}_1\subseteq\mathcal{T}_2$ (i.e., $\mathcal{T}_2$ is finer) iff for every $B_1\in\mathcal{B}_1$ and every $x\in B_1$, there exists $B_2\in\mathcal{B}_2$ with $x\in B_2\subseteq B_1$.

**Standard examples**:
- $\mathbb{R}^n$: open balls $\{B(x,\varepsilon): x\in\mathbb{R}^n, \varepsilon>0\}$ form a basis; equivalently, open rational-radius balls around rational-coordinate centers also generate the standard topology.
- Order topology: on a linearly ordered $(X,<)$, basis = open intervals $(a,b)$ plus rays $(-\infty,b)$ and $(a,+\infty)$.
- Product topology: on $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$, the finite intersections $\pi_{\alpha_1}^{-1}(U_1)\cap\cdots\cap\pi_{\alpha_n}^{-1}(U_n)$ (finitely many constraints) form a basis.
- Lower-limit (Sorgenfrey line): $\mathcal{B}_{LL}=\{[a,b): a<b, a,b\in\mathbb{R}\}$ generates a topology strictly finer than the standard (every open interval is a union of half-open intervals, but $[0,1)$ is open in $\mathcal{T}_{LL}$ yet not in $\mathcal{T}_{std}$).

## Component 4 — Worked Examples

**Example 1 (LO1–LO2 — verifying basis conditions)**: Show $\mathcal{B}=\{(a,b):a<b, a,b\in\mathbb{R}\}$ is a basis for the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$. **(B1)** For any $x\in\mathbb{R}$, $(x-1,x+1)\in\mathcal{B}$ and $x\in(x-1,x+1)$. ✓ **(B2)** If $x\in(a_1,b_1)\cap(a_2,b_2)$, set $a=\max(a_1,a_2)$, $b=\min(b_1,b_2)$; then $a<x<b$ (since $x$ is in both intervals), so $(a,b)\in\mathcal{B}$ and $x\in(a,b)\subseteq(a_1,b_1)\cap(a_2,b_2)$. ✓ The generated topology: $U$ is open iff for every $x\in U$ some open interval around $x$ lies in $U$ — this matches the standard Euclidean topology on $\mathbb{R}$.

**Example 2 (LO3 — two bases, finer topology)**: Compare $\mathcal{B}_{std}=\{(a,b)\}$ and $\mathcal{B}_{LL}=\{[a,b)\}$ on $\mathbb{R}$. Does $\mathcal{T}_{std}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{LL}$? For any $(a,b)\in\mathcal{B}_{std}$ and $x\in(a,b)$: take $[x, b)\in\mathcal{B}_{LL}$, so $x\in[x,b)\subseteq(a,b)$ ✓ — every standard-open set is also LL-open. Does $\mathcal{T}_{LL}\subseteq\mathcal{T}_{std}$? Consider $[0,1)\in\mathcal{B}_{LL}$ and $x=0$: every $(a,b)$ containing 0 extends to the left ($a<0$), so no standard interval fits inside $[0,1)$ at $x=0$ ✗ — $[0,1)$ is LL-open but NOT standard-open. Conclusion: $\mathcal{T}_{std}\subsetneq\mathcal{T}_{LL}$ (lower-limit strictly finer).

**Example 3 (LO3 — product topology basis)**: In $\mathbb{R}^2=\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$, a basis element for the product topology is $\pi_1^{-1}(a_1,b_1)\cap\pi_2^{-1}(a_2,b_2)=(a_1,b_1)\times(a_2,b_2)$: an open rectangle. For any $(x,y)\in(a_1,b_1)\times(a_2,b_2)$, the open disk $B((x,y),\varepsilon)\subseteq(a_1,b_1)\times(a_2,b_2)$ (for small enough $\varepsilon$) shows open rectangles and open disks generate the same topology — both are bases for the standard $\mathbb{R}^2$ topology.

## Component 5 — Teaching Actions

### Teaching Action A01 — Motivation via Open Balls (Primitive P16: Counterexample)

Ask: "If we want a topology on $\mathbb{R}^n$ consistent with distance, must we list every open set?" — No: specifying all open balls is enough, since every open set is a union of open balls. Introduce the basis as the generator: a small manageable collection from which all open sets are built by taking unions.

- **MC-2 hook**: present two collections of intervals ($\{(a,b)\}$ vs $\{(a,b): a,b\in\mathbb{Q}\}$) and ask if they generate the same topology — they do (rational-radius balls still cover every point in every standard open set), illustrating that a topology has many possible bases.

### Teaching Action A02 — The Two Conditions (Primitive P25: Deductive)

State conditions B1 and B2. Check B1 is a coverage condition (every point is in some basis element). Check B2 is a consistency/intersection condition (basis elements intersect "nicely" — not required to have their intersection be a basis element, only to be refinable at each point). Work Example 1 carefully.

- **MC-1 hook**: ask "must the intersection of two basis elements be a basis element?" — No; only a refinement at each point is required. If the student says yes, B2 is being mis-read as closure under intersection (that would make $\mathcal{B}$ a topology, which is not required).

### Teaching Action A03 — Basis Comparison (Primitive P11: Representation Shift)

State the finer/coarser comparison criterion. Work Example 2 to show $\mathcal{T}_{LL}$ is strictly finer than $\mathcal{T}_{std}$ using the comparison criterion directly. Then work Example 3 for the product topology.

- **MC-3 hook**: ask "does the lower-limit basis $[a,b)$ generate the SAME topology as $(a,b)$?" — many students say yes because both look like intervals. The comparison criterion resolves this rigorously.

### Teaching Action A04 — Mastery Gate (Primitive P91)

P91 = P77 → P55 → P76 → P55 → P75 → P55 → P74 → P55 → P78.

- **P77 (5-problem set)**:
  1. Verify that the collection $\mathcal{B}=\{(a,b)\times(c,d): a<b, c<d\}$ of open rectangles satisfies B1 and B2 in $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  2. Show that $\mathcal{B}=\{B(x,1/n): x\in\mathbb{R}^2, n\in\mathbb{Z}^+\}$ (unit-fraction-radius disks only) generates the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}^2$.
  3. Show that $\mathcal{B}=\{\{x\}: x\in X\}$ (singletons) generates the discrete topology on any set $X$; verify B1 and B2.
  4. On $\mathbb{R}$, compare $\mathcal{B}_{LL}=\{[a,b)\}$ with $\mathcal{B}_{RR}=\{(a,b]\}$. Is $\mathcal{T}_{LL}=\mathcal{T}_{RR}$? (Show $[0,1)\in\mathcal{T}_{LL}$ but $[0,1)\notin\mathcal{T}_{RR}$ and vice versa for $(0,1]$.)
  5. Prove: if $\mathcal{T}$ is a topology on $X$ and $\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{T}$ with the property that every member of $\mathcal{T}$ is a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$, then $\mathcal{B}$ is a basis for $\mathcal{T}$. (Verify B1 and B2.)
- **P76 (Transfer Probe, mode = independence)**: "The **second-countability axiom** states that a topological space has a **countable basis**. (a) Show that $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the standard topology is second-countable by exhibiting an explicit countable basis. (b) Show that the lower-limit topology $\mathcal{T}_{LL}$ on $\mathbb{R}$ is NOT second-countable: any basis for $\mathcal{T}_{LL}$ must contain uncountably many elements. Hint: for each $x\in\mathbb{R}$, the set $[x,x+1)$ is open but cannot be expressed as a union of basis elements that 'start before' $x$, so each $x$ must contribute its own basis element."
- **P55 — Transition Prompt**: "Take a moment. Now try the next challenge."
- **P75 (Mastery Assessment against MAMR = 5/5)**.
- **P74 (Routing Decision)**: MAMR met → P78. Not met → Protocol B on missed MC → re-gate.
- **P78 (Completion)**: Basis for a Topology — certified.

## Component 6 — Misconception Registry

| ID | Label | Description | Severity |
|---|---|---|---|
| MC-1 | BASIS-IS-CLOSED-UNDER-INTERSECTION | Believing a basis must be closed under pairwise intersection — confusing condition B2 (find a refinement at each point) with closure under $\cap$ (which would make $\mathcal{B}$ itself a topology) | Foundational |
| MC-2 | DIFFERENT-BASES-MEANS-DIFFERENT-TOPOLOGIES | Believing two different collections $\mathcal{B}_1\neq\mathcal{B}_2$ always generate different topologies — missing that $\{(a,b)\}$ and $\{(a,b): a,b\in\mathbb{Q}\}$ generate the same standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$ | Moderate |
| MC-3 | LOWER-LIMIT-SAME-AS-STANDARD | Believing $[a,b)$ and $(a,b)$ generate the same topology on $\mathbb{R}$ because both are "intervals" — missing that $[0,1)$ is lower-limit-open but not standard-open, making $\mathcal{T}_{LL}$ strictly finer | Moderate |

### Protocol B — Repair Actions

- **B01 (targets MC-1)**: P27 (name it: "Basis Need Not Be Closed Under Intersection") → P41 (detect: ask whether $B_1\cap B_2$ must be in $\mathcal{B}$) → P64 (conceptual shift: re-read B2 carefully — it only requires finding $B_3\subseteq B_1\cap B_2$ containing each $x$; for open balls, $B(x,r_1)\cap B(x,r_2)=B(x,\min(r_1,r_2))$ happens to be in the basis, but that is a coincidence of balls, not a requirement for bases in general).
- **B02 (targets MC-2)**: P27 (name it: "Different Bases Can Generate the Same Topology") → P41 (detect: ask whether rational-radius balls generate a different topology than all open balls on $\mathbb{R}$) → P64 (conceptual shift: apply the comparison criterion — for any open ball $B(x,\varepsilon)$ and any $y\in B(x,\varepsilon)$, a rational-radius ball around $y$ fits inside $B(x,\varepsilon)$ by density of rationals; so both bases generate the same open sets).
- **B03 (targets MC-3)**: P27 (name it: "Lower-Limit Topology Is Strictly Finer") → P41 (detect: ask whether $[0,1)$ is open in the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$) → P64 (conceptual shift: apply the comparison criterion in the failure direction — for $[0,1)$ and the point $0$, every standard open interval containing 0 extends to negative numbers and thus is not contained in $[0,1)$, so no standard basis element covers $0$ within $[0,1)$, proving $[0,1)\notin\mathcal{T}_{std}$).

## Component 8 — Teaching Notes

- The lower-limit (Sorgenfrey) line is the canonical worked example for basis comparison and appears in every serious topology course; its inclusion here is intentional and standard.
- B2 (the intersection refinement condition) is the precise condition students most often misread — they either confuse it with closure under $\cap$, or they skip checking it. The worked example of open rectangles vs. open disks in $\mathbb{R}^2$ makes B2 concrete.
- Second-countability in the transfer probe is the natural next concept (used in Urysohn's metrization theorem and separability); introducing it at the transfer level here plants the seed without requiring mastery now.
- MAMR = 5/5 with estimated_hours = 3 reflects that the basis concept has substantial verification overhead (B1, B2 both must be checked for every candidate basis) and requires understanding topology comparison, not just definition recall.
