## 1. Concept Spine

SiO₂ does NOT behave like CO₂ despite carbon and silicon sharing Group 14 — SiO₂ is a network covalent solid that does NOT dissolve in water at room temperature (unlike CO₂, which readily forms carbonic acid), because the carbon-to-silicon analogy breaks down specifically at π-bonding: silicon cannot form π bonds with oxygen the way carbon does (Si's larger size prevents effective p-orbital overlap for π bonding), so SiO₂ forms an extended 3D covalent network rather than discrete molecules — SiO₂ instead reacts with molten NaOH or HF, not with liquid water; and diamond does NOT conduct electricity despite being made of the same element as graphite, since diamond uses all four of each carbon's valence electrons in localized σ bonds to four neighboring atoms (no delocalized electrons available), while graphite's layered structure leaves one electron per carbon delocalized across each layer's π system, enabling conduction — the BONDING ARRANGEMENT, not the element itself, determines conductivity.

## 2. Four-Stage CPA+ Mental Model

**Concrete**: Attempting to dissolve SiO₂ (sand) in water, discovering it simply doesn't dissolve, unlike CO₂ readily forming carbonic acid in water.

**Representational**: A side-by-side structural comparison of diamond (each C bonded to 4 neighbors via localized σ bonds, no delocalization) against graphite (layered structure with delocalized π electrons across each layer), visually connecting structure to conductivity.

**Abstract**: The general principle that period-2-to-period-3 analogies within the same group can break down at the level of specific bonding capabilities (like π-bond formation), even when general valence-electron count is shared; the general principle that conductivity in carbon allotropes depends on bonding arrangement (localized vs. delocalized electrons), not merely on elemental identity.

**Transfer**: Given an unfamiliar Group 14 compound or carbon allotrope, correctly predicting whether a same-group analogy (like C-to-Si) will hold or break down based on π-bonding capability, and correctly predicting electrical conductivity based on the specific bonding arrangement (localized versus delocalized electrons), not elemental identity alone.

## 3. Why Beginners Fail

Students assume SiO₂ behaves analogously to CO₂ (both being Group 14 element oxides), missing that silicon's larger atomic size prevents the effective p-orbital overlap needed for π-bond formation with oxygen (unlike carbon's smaller size, which allows strong C=O π bonding), causing SiO₂ to form an extended covalent network solid rather than a small, water-soluble molecule; and they assume diamond must conduct electricity simply because it's "made of carbon" (the same element as graphite, a known conductor), missing that conductivity depends specifically on the BONDING ARRANGEMENT — diamond's four localized σ bonds per carbon leave no delocalized electrons available, while graphite's layered structure genuinely does leave delocalized π electrons free to conduct.

## 4. Misconception Library

### MC-1: SiO₂ behaves like CO₂ — it dissolves in water to form an acid
- **Probe**: "Write the equation for SiO₂ dissolving in water."
- **Characteristic phrase**: "silicon dioxide + water = silicic acid."
- **Trigger (Type 6, analogy overextension)**: Students correctly know CO₂ dissolves in water to form carbonic acid and directly extend this behavior to SiO₂ by same-group analogy, without checking whether the underlying bonding capability actually transfers.
- **Conflict evidence [P28]**: SiO₂ is a NETWORK COVALENT SOLID that does NOT dissolve in water at room temperature — the carbon-to-silicon analogy breaks down specifically because silicon CANNOT form effective π bonds with oxygen (unlike carbon's C=O π bonding in CO₂), due to silicon's larger atomic size preventing good p-orbital overlap; without π-bond stabilization, silicon instead forms an extended 3D network of Si-O single bonds (SiO₂'s actual structure), which behaves as an inert, insoluble solid under normal aqueous conditions — SiO₂ genuinely reacts with molten NaOH or with HF, but NOT with liquid water.
- **Bridge [P30]**: Same-group elements share the same valence-electron COUNT (and hence similar general oxidation states and stoichiometric formulas), but they don't necessarily share the same specific BONDING CAPABILITIES (like effective π-bond formation) — silicon's larger atomic radius genuinely disrupts the p-orbital overlap that makes carbon's π bonding with oxygen so effective, producing a structurally very different oxide.
- **Replacement [P31]**: SiO₂ is a network covalent solid, insoluble in water at room temperature, due to silicon's inability to form effective π bonds with oxygen (unlike carbon) — never assume C-to-Si analogies transfer automatically, especially for π-bonding-dependent behavior.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]**: CO₂ (small carbon, effective π bonding, discrete molecule, dissolves in water) vs. SiO₂ (larger silicon, no effective π bonding, extended covalent network, insoluble in water).
- **S6 repair path**: Present the explicit atomic-size/p-orbital-overlap argument, connecting silicon's larger size directly to its inability to form C=O-style π bonds.

### MC-2: Diamond conducts electricity because it is made of carbon like graphite
- **Probe**: "Which allotrope of carbon conducts electricity — diamond or graphite?"
- **Characteristic phrase**: "carbon conducts electricity."
- **Trigger (Type 2, perceptual intuition)**: Students associate "carbon" as a single, uniform material with fixed properties, missing that different bonding arrangements (allotropes) of the same element can produce dramatically different physical properties.
- **Conflict evidence [P28]**: Diamond has NO delocalized electrons — all four of each carbon atom's valence electrons are used in localized σ bonds to four neighboring carbon atoms, leaving nothing free to conduct current, making diamond genuinely an electrical insulator; graphite, by contrast, has a distinctly LAYERED structure where each carbon forms only 3 σ bonds within its layer, leaving one electron per carbon delocalized across a continuous π system spanning each entire layer — this delocalized π system genuinely does conduct electricity, explaining graphite's well-known conductivity.
- **Bridge [P30]**: Electrical conductivity depends specifically on whether electrons are DELOCALIZED (free to move) or LOCALIZED (fixed in specific bonds) — this is a property of the BONDING ARRANGEMENT (how the atoms are connected and how electrons are distributed among those bonds), not simply a property of which element is present.
- **Replacement [P31]**: Diamond (all electrons localized in σ bonds, no delocalization) is an electrical insulator; graphite (delocalized π system across each layer) is a conductor — the bonding arrangement, not the elemental identity (carbon), determines conductivity.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]**: Diamond (all 4 valence electrons in localized σ bonds, insulator) vs. graphite (3 σ bonds + 1 delocalized π electron per carbon, conductor) — same element, dramatically different conductivity from different bonding.
- **S6 repair path**: Present both structures explicitly, having the student count localized versus delocalized electrons in each, connecting the count directly to conductivity.

## 5. Explanation Library

**Primary explanation**: Same-group elements share valence-electron count and general oxidation-state patterns, but this doesn't guarantee identical bonding CAPABILITIES — silicon's larger atomic size prevents the effective π-bond formation with oxygen that makes carbon's oxide (CO₂) a small, water-soluble molecule, causing silicon's oxide (SiO₂) to instead form an extended, insoluble covalent network. Carbon's own allotropes (diamond and graphite) demonstrate that even the SAME element can show dramatically different properties (like electrical conductivity) depending entirely on how its atoms are bonded together — diamond's fully localized bonding structure prevents conduction, while graphite's delocalized π system enables it.

**Secondary explanation (structure-determines-property framing)**: Physical and chemical properties of a substance are determined by its actual bonding structure and arrangement, not merely by which element(s) are present — same-group or same-element comparisons can mislead if the specific bonding details (π-bond capability, electron localization/delocalization) genuinely differ between the substances being compared.

## 6. Analogy Library

- **Primary analogy**: Two buildings made of identical bricks (carbon atoms) but built with genuinely different architectural designs — one a solid, windowless bunker (diamond, every brick locked in place, nothing flows through) and the other an open-plan building with connected hallways on each floor (graphite, allowing free movement/flow within each layer) — the material (brick/carbon) is identical, but the structural design determines the very different functional properties.
- **Breaking point**: The building-architecture analogy conveys the structure-determines-property concept well but doesn't naturally capture the specific π-bonding/atomic-size argument for the SiO₂-vs-CO₂ case — that needs the explicit orbital-overlap reasoning.
- **Anti-analogy**: Do NOT say "same element means same properties" — this directly reinforces MC-2.

## 7. Demonstration Library

- **Demonstration 1 (SiO₂-vs-CO₂ solubility comparison)**: Attempt (or describe) dissolving SiO₂ (sand) in water directly, contrasting the observed non-dissolution with CO₂'s well-known ready dissolution.
- **Demonstration 2 (diamond-vs-graphite conductivity demonstration)**: Present or describe a conductivity test comparing diamond and graphite, connecting the observed result directly to each structure's localized-versus-delocalized electron arrangement.

## 8. Discovery Lesson

**Opening**: "CO₂ readily dissolves in water to form carbonic acid. Since silicon is right below carbon in Group 14, do you expect SiO₂ to dissolve in water the same way?"

**Exploration**: Students examine silicon's larger atomic size and its effect on π-bond formation with oxygen, discovering why SiO₂'s structure differs fundamentally from CO₂'s.

**Synthesis**: Guide toward: same-group elements share valence-electron count but not necessarily specific bonding capabilities like π-bond formation.

**Closure**: "Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. Does that mean they have the same electrical conductivity?" (Directly resolves MC-2.)

## 9. Teaching Actions (session_cap = 4)

- **TA-1 (SHOW)**: Present the explicit atomic-size/p-orbital-overlap argument for why Si cannot form C=O-style π bonds.
- **TA-2 (TELL)**: State the localized-vs-delocalized-electron principle explicitly, worked through for diamond versus graphite.
- **TA-3 (DO)**: Student predicts whether a given Group 14 same-group analogy will hold, using the π-bonding-capability criterion.
- **TA-4 (TEST-THINKING)**: Present MC-2's diamond probe and ask the student to justify diamond's insulating behavior using its bonding structure.

## 10. Voice Teaching

Whenever a same-group analogy is proposed for Group 14 (or any group), ask "does the specific bonding capability (like π-bonding) actually transfer?" before accepting the analogy. Whenever conductivity is discussed for a carbon allotrope, always check the specific bonding arrangement (localized vs. delocalized electrons) rather than relying on elemental identity.

## 11. Assessment

**Mastery gate**: Student can (a) correctly explain why SiO₂ doesn't dissolve in water like CO₂ does, using the π-bonding/atomic-size argument, (b) correctly explain diamond's insulating behavior versus graphite's conductivity using bonding-structure reasoning.

- **FA-1**: "Write the equation for SiO₂ dissolving in water." — targets MC-1.
- **FA-2**: "Which allotrope of carbon conducts electricity — diamond or graphite?" — targets MC-2.

**Confidence calibration**: Predict high-confidence-wrong answers on FA-1 among students who've just learned CO₂'s water-dissolution behavior and are extending it by same-group analogy.

**Delayed retrieval**: Re-probe MC-1's π-bonding argument and MC-2's structure-determines-conductivity principle before `chem.pblock.trends` synthesizes p-block behavior across multiple groups.

## 12. Recovery Notes

- **S3 (stuck)**: For the SiO₂-CO₂ confusion, present the explicit atomic-size comparison and connect it directly to π-orbital overlap effectiveness.
- **S4 (frustrated)**: Normalize — the same-group, same-stoichiometry analogy is a very reasonable, common first assumption, making its breakdown here a legitimate, instructive surprise.
- **S6 (collision)**: Use the explicit localized-vs-delocalized electron counting for MC-2.
- **S9 (post-repair check)**: Ask the student to explain, unprompted, why diamond and graphite, both pure carbon, have such different electrical properties.
