## 1. Concept Spine

**One-sentence definition:** Solid, liquid, and gas are three states of the SAME particles differing only in spacing, arrangement, and motion — heating or cooling interconverts between states without changing the particles' own chemical identity, and evaporation (a surface phenomenon, occurring at any temperature) must be distinguished from boiling (a bulk phenomenon, occurring only at one specific temperature).

**The core insight:** A state change is a PHYSICAL change — the exact same particles, with the exact same internal bonds and identity, simply become more or less tightly packed and more or less free to move. Nothing about the particles themselves is created, destroyed, or chemically altered. The three states differ only in how closely particles are packed and how much they move relative to each other — a purely spatial/kinetic distinction, never a compositional one.

**Conceptual chain:**
1. Solids: particles tightly packed in a fixed arrangement, vibrating in place but not translating past each other — fixed shape, fixed volume.
2. Liquids: particles close together (similar spacing to solids) but free to move past each other — fixed volume, but shape conforms to the container.
3. Gases: particles far apart (roughly 10× the spacing of a liquid/solid) and moving freely and rapidly in all directions — no fixed shape or volume, expanding to fill any container.
4. Heating increases average particle kinetic energy, eventually overcoming the attractive forces holding particles in a more ordered arrangement (solid -> liquid -> gas); cooling does the reverse.
5. The particles' own internal identity (their own atoms, their own chemical bonds) is completely unchanged across all three states — only the spacing and motion between separate particles changes, never anything within a single particle.
6. Evaporation is a SURFACE phenomenon: even well below the boiling point, a small fraction of surface molecules always have enough kinetic energy (from the distribution of particle energies) to escape into the gas phase — this is why a puddle evaporates at room temperature. Boiling is a BULK phenomenon: vaporization occurs throughout the entire liquid, only at one specific temperature (where vapor pressure equals atmospheric pressure).

**Central relations:**
- Solid < liquid < gas, in order of increasing particle spacing and freedom of motion.
- Heating increases kinetic energy -> increasing spacing/freedom (solid -> liquid -> gas); cooling reverses this.
- A single particle's internal identity/bonds are unchanged by any state change — only inter-particle spacing and motion change.
- Evaporation (surface, any temperature) ≠ boiling (bulk, one specific temperature only).

## 2. Four-Stage CPA+ Mental Model

### Concrete (Enactive)
- A crowded room analogy acted out or described: people standing in fixed assigned seats (solid), people standing close together but free to walk around each other (liquid), people spread out and moving freely across a large open field (gas).
- A puddle disappearing over the course of a day at room temperature — a directly observable, everyday example of evaporation occurring far below 100°C.

### Representational (Iconic)
- A particle-spacing diagram: three boxes at the same physical scale, showing a solid's tight regular lattice, a liquid's close-but-disordered particles, and a gas's widely-spaced particles — with the SAME particle symbol used in all three, emphasizing unchanged identity.
- A heating curve graph: temperature vs. heat added, with flat plateaus at the melting and boiling points (where added heat goes into overcoming attractive forces, not raising temperature) and rising segments within each state.

### Abstract (Symbolic)
- The particle-spacing ratio: solid ≈ liquid spacing (roughly 1× particle diameter) << gas spacing (roughly 10× particle diameter or more).
- The Maxwell-Boltzmann-style qualitative energy distribution: at any temperature, particles have a RANGE of kinetic energies, not one single value — evaporation occurs because the high-energy tail of that distribution always has some particles with enough energy to escape, at any temperature above absolute zero.

### Transfer (+)
- Kinetic molecular theory (the immediate successor concept) formalizes the particle-motion picture introduced qualitatively here into a full quantitative model.
- Weather and climate science: evaporation from oceans/lakes at ambient temperature (never requiring boiling) drives the entire water cycle.
- Industrial processes (distillation, refrigeration, freeze-drying) all depend on precise control of state-change conditions.

## 3. Why Beginners Fail

**Mode 1 — Believing state changes alter chemical composition:** Correct: boiling, melting, freezing, and condensing are physical changes — the same molecules, with the same internal bonds, simply change spacing and motion; no chemical bonds within a molecule are broken or formed.

**Mode 2 — Believing gas particles themselves expand to fill a larger container:** Correct: individual gas particles are a fixed, unchanging size; what changes when a gas fills a larger container is the EMPTY SPACE between particles, not the particles' own physical size.

**Mode 3 — Believing evaporation only happens at the boiling point:** Correct: evaporation is a surface phenomenon that occurs at ANY temperature (even well below boiling), because some fraction of particles always have enough energy to escape from the surface; boiling is the separate, bulk phenomenon that occurs only at one specific temperature.

## 4. Misconception Library

### MC-1: "Melting/boiling changes composition"
- **Probe:** "When liquid water boils, what is inside the steam bubbles? What are the particles?"
- **Characteristic phrase:** "When water boils it breaks into hydrogen and oxygen."
- **Trigger:** Instruction-induced — early instruction emphasizes that state changes are "physical" without giving students a particle-level anchor for what specifically stays constant, so students default to assuming "something must be changing" chemically.
- **Conflict evidence [P28]:** The bubbles inside boiling water contain water VAPOR — gaseous H₂O molecules, chemically identical to the liquid H₂O molecules they came from. If boiling decomposed water into hydrogen and oxygen gas, condensing the steam back to liquid would not simply give water back — it would require the reverse chemical reaction (which does not happen upon ordinary condensation).
- **Bridge [P30]:** "Picture the same H₂O molecule in all three states — same two hydrogen atoms, same one oxygen atom, same bonds between them, in solid ice, liquid water, and water vapor. What changes between the states is only how far apart these molecules are and how freely they move — nothing about the molecule itself, its atoms, or its bonds ever changes during a state change."
- **Replacement [P31]:** State changes (melting, boiling, freezing, condensing) are physical changes — the same molecule, same bonds, same chemical identity throughout; only inter-particle spacing and motion change.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]:** Boiling water (physical change, produces water vapor, same H₂O molecules) vs. electrolyzing water (chemical change, produces separate H₂ and O₂ gas, different molecules entirely — met already in chem.found.matter).
- **S6 repair path:** Draw the same-molecule-three-states particle diagram (Section 2) explicitly before returning to the probe.

### MC-2: "Gas particles expand to fill the container"
- **Probe:** "If you double the size of a container holding a gas, what happens to each individual particle? What happens to the space between particles?"
- **Characteristic phrase:** "The particles get bigger to fill the space."
- **Trigger:** Perceptual intuition — visually, the gas as a whole clearly does fill the larger container, and students attribute this visible fact to particle expansion rather than to particle motion plus increased empty space.
- **Conflict evidence [P28]:** Individual gas particles (atoms or molecules) have a fixed size — roughly 0.1-0.5 nm — set entirely by their own atomic/molecular structure, completely independent of container size. What changes when the container doubles in size is the EMPTY SPACE between particles: the same number of same-size particles simply spread out over a larger total volume, with more empty space between each pair.
- **Bridge [P30]:** "Contrast particle SIZE (fixed, roughly 0.1-0.5 nm, set by the molecule's own structure, never changes) against the SPACE BETWEEN particles in a gas (roughly 10 times the particle size itself, and this is exactly what changes — grows or shrinks — as container volume changes). It's the gaps that expand, never the particles themselves."
- **Replacement [P31]:** Gas particles have a fixed size, unaffected by container volume; what changes with container size is the empty space between particles, not particle size itself.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]:** A gas in a small container (particles closer together, less empty space) vs. the SAME gas in a larger container (particles farther apart, more empty space, but each individual particle is exactly the same size in both cases).
- **S6 repair path:** Draw the particle-size-vs-spacing contrast diagram (Section 2) explicitly, with actual scale numbers, before returning to the probe.

### MC-3: "Evaporation only happens at 100°C"
- **Probe:** "Why does a puddle disappear on a day when the temperature is only 20°C?"
- **Characteristic phrase:** "Water can only become gas at 100 degrees."
- **Trigger:** Instruction-induced — boiling point (100°C for water) is the temperature students most strongly associate with "water becomes gas," leading them to extrapolate that this is the ONLY temperature at which the transition can occur.
- **Conflict evidence [P28]:** A puddle at 20°C visibly disappears over hours, evidence that water is escaping into the gas phase, despite being nowhere near 100°C. This is because particle kinetic energies at any temperature form a distribution (not one single value) — even at 20°C, some fraction of surface molecules have enough energy, purely by chance, to escape the liquid's surface, exactly as the highest-energy members of any distribution always exist regardless of the average.
- **Bridge [P30]:** "Particles in a liquid don't all move at exactly the same speed — there's a whole distribution of energies, from slow to fast, at any given temperature. Evaporation only requires the FASTEST surface molecules to have enough energy to escape, and there are always SOME such molecules, at any temperature above absolute zero. Boiling is different: that's when the ENTIRE liquid — not just the fastest surface molecules — has enough vapor pressure to form bubbles throughout the bulk, which only happens at one specific temperature."
- **Replacement [P31]:** Evaporation is a surface phenomenon occurring continuously at any temperature (some fraction of surface molecules always have enough energy to escape); boiling is a distinct, bulk phenomenon occurring only at one specific temperature.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]:** A puddle slowly disappearing at 20°C (evaporation — surface only, gradual) vs. a pot of water bubbling vigorously at 100°C (boiling — throughout the bulk, at one fixed temperature).
- **S6 repair path:** Introduce the particle-energy-distribution idea explicitly (Section 2) and connect it directly to why SOME molecules can always escape, regardless of average temperature.

## 5. Explanation Library

**Explanation A — Same particle, three states (conceptual):**
"Solid, liquid, and gas are not three different SUBSTANCES — they are the same substance's same particles, differing only in how tightly packed and how free to move they are. A water molecule in ice, in liquid water, and in steam is the identical H₂O molecule in all three cases; heating and cooling never touch the molecule's own internal bonds, only the spacing and motion between separate molecules."

**Explanation B — Evaporation vs. boiling (conceptual):**
"Evaporation happens continuously, at the SURFACE only, at any temperature above absolute zero — because particle kinetic energies form a distribution, and there are always some fast-enough surface molecules to escape. Boiling happens only at ONE specific temperature, throughout the ENTIRE bulk of the liquid at once, when the liquid's vapor pressure rises to match the surrounding atmospheric pressure. These are genuinely different phenomena, not the same process at different intensities."

---

## 6. Analogy Library

**Primary analogy — A crowded room vs. an open field:**
Particles in a solid are like people standing in assigned, fixed seats, only wiggling in place; in a liquid, like people standing close together in a crowded room but free to walk around each other; in a gas, like the same number of people spread out across a huge open field, moving freely with lots of space between them. Same people (same particles) throughout — only their spacing and freedom of movement change.

**Breaking point:** People in a room can choose to change their own size or shape somewhat (crouching, spreading arms); gas particles' own physical size is completely fixed and never changes with container volume — this is exactly the point where the analogy must be explicitly corrected, directly targeting MC-2.

**Anti-analogy:** Do NOT describe gas particles as "spreading out" in a way that implies the particles themselves grow or stretch — always specify it is the EMPTY SPACE between particles that spreads out, not the particles.

## 7. Demonstration Library

**Demo 1 — Same-molecule three-states particle diagram:**
Draw (or present) the identical H₂O molecule structure shown unchanged across solid, liquid, and gas depictions, directly targeting MC-1.

**Demo 2 — Particle size vs. spacing scale diagram:**
Present actual scale numbers (particle size ~0.1-0.5 nm, fixed; gas inter-particle spacing ~10× particle size, variable with container volume) directly targeting MC-2.

**Demo 3 — Puddle evaporation vs. pot boiling comparison:**
Contrast a slowly-disappearing puddle at room temperature against a vigorously bubbling pot at 100°C, directly targeting MC-3 and Explanation B.

## 8. Discovery Lesson

**Opening (5 min):** "When water boils, bubbles of gas form and escape. What's actually inside those bubbles — hydrogen and oxygen, or something else?"

**Exploration (15 min):**
- Run Demo 1 (same-molecule three-states diagram), directly targeting MC-1.
- Build Explanation A (same particle, three states) step by step.

**Synthesis (10 min):**
- Run Demo 2 (particle size vs. spacing), directly targeting MC-2.
- Run Demo 3 (puddle vs. pot comparison), directly targeting MC-3, then build Explanation B.

**Closure:** "Every state change you'll study — melting, freezing, boiling, condensing — is the SAME particles rearranging their spacing and motion. Nothing about the particles themselves ever changes. That single idea explains why ice, water, and steam are all still, unambiguously, water."

## 10. Voice Teaching

**Opening:**
"When water boils, bubbles of gas form and rise up. What's actually inside those bubbles? Hydrogen and oxygen, or something else entirely?"

**At the composition clarification:**
"Picture the exact same water molecule — two hydrogens, one oxygen, bonded together — sitting in ice, floating in liquid water, and drifting as steam. Same molecule, same bonds, all three times. Melting and boiling never touch what's INSIDE a molecule. All that changes is how far apart the molecules are from each other and how freely they can move."

**At the evaporation-vs-boiling clarification:**
"Here's the key distinction: boiling happens throughout the whole pot, all at once, but only at exactly one temperature. Evaporation is completely different — it happens only at the surface, and it happens at ANY temperature, because there's always a range of particle speeds, and there are always a few fast enough surface molecules to escape, even on a cool day. That's why a puddle vanishes without ever reaching a boil."

## 11. Assessment

**Mastery gate:** Student correctly explains why state changes preserve chemical identity, correctly distinguishes particle size from inter-particle spacing, and correctly distinguishes evaporation from boiling. Score ≥ 70%.

**FA-1 — Composition preservation:**
*Q: Explain, at the particle level, why steam condensing back into liquid water gives you water back, and not a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen.*
Expected: Steam consists of gaseous H₂O molecules, chemically identical to liquid water molecules; condensation only changes spacing/motion, never breaking the molecule's own internal bonds.
Threshold: Must explicitly state that no bonds were broken during the state change.

**FA-2 — Particle size vs. spacing:**
*Q: A gas is transferred from a small container to a container twice the volume. What happens to the size of each gas particle? What happens to the space between particles?*
Expected: Particle size is completely unchanged (fixed by the molecule's own structure); the empty space between particles increases to fill the larger volume.
Threshold: Must correctly separate "particle size" (constant) from "inter-particle spacing" (variable).

**FA-3 — Evaporation vs. boiling:**
*Q: A cup of water at 25°C slowly loses volume over several days, without ever boiling. Explain this using the particle-energy-distribution idea.*
Expected: At any temperature, particle kinetic energies form a distribution; some surface molecules always have enough energy to escape into the gas phase (evaporation), regardless of the average temperature being far below the boiling point.
Threshold: Must reference the energy-distribution idea, not just restate "evaporation happens at any temperature" without the underlying mechanism.

**Confidence calibration:** After FA-1, students rate confidence before revealing the answer; students confident but wrong are walked through Demo 1's same-molecule diagram again before re-attempting a parallel item.

**Delayed retrieval (session + 3):** "Explain why a gas fills a larger container without its individual particles getting bigger." Expected: particle size is fixed; only the empty space between particles increases.

## 12. Recovery Notes

**S3:** Student can name the three states but cannot explain what specifically changes between them. Re-run Demo 1 (same-molecule diagram) and Explanation A explicitly before returning to the probe.

**S4:** Student conflates particle size with inter-particle spacing (MC-2). Re-run Demo 2's scale diagram with the actual numbers before returning to the probe.

**S6:** Student is anxious about "how can water disappear without boiling — where does it go?" Anchor entirely in the puddle-disappearing everyday observation (Demo 3) before introducing the abstract energy-distribution explanation.

**S9:** Extend into sublimation (solid directly to gas, e.g. dry ice) as enrichment, previewing the full state-change diagram developed in kinetic molecular theory.
