## 1. Concept Spine

"Alkali metals" are named for their PRODUCT, not an affinity — all Group 1 metals react with water to produce strongly alkaline (basic) solutions of MOH, but the metals themselves are not alkalis; not all Group 1 metals burn in oxygen to form the simple oxide M₂O — sodium specifically forms the PEROXIDE Na₂O₂ (O oxidation state −1), while K/Rb/Cs form SUPEROXIDES (O oxidation state −½), with the specific oxide type determined by which anion size is best stabilized by the given cation's size (only Li, the smallest cation, forms the simple oxide Li₂O); and Group 1 reactivity INCREASES down the group (opposite the halogen trend), since metals react by LOSING electrons (easier with lower IE going down), making Li — despite being at the "top" — the LEAST reactive alkali metal, not the most.

## 2. Four-Stage CPA+ Mental Model

**Concrete**: Observing sodium burning in excess oxygen and discovering the product is Na₂O₂ (peroxide), not the "expected" simple oxide Na₂O.

**Representational**: A cation-size-to-anion-stability diagram showing Li's small cation stabilizing compact O²⁻ (simple oxide), while larger K/Rb/Cs cations stabilize the less compact O₂²⁻/O₂⁻ (peroxide/superoxide).

**Abstract**: The general principle that reactivity direction for metals (down-group increase, driven by decreasing IE) is opposite to non-metals' direction (down-group decrease, driven by decreasing EA magnitude) — never transfer one group's trend direction to the other.

**Transfer**: Given an unfamiliar Group 1 reaction or comparison, correctly predicting the specific oxide product type based on cation size, correctly predicting the reactivity ranking (increasing down the group), and correctly explaining the "alkali" naming as describing the reaction product, not the metal's own nature.

## 3. Why Beginners Fail

Students assume "alkali metal" describes an inherent property of the metal itself (perhaps an "affinity" for alkaline substances), missing that the name specifically describes the PRODUCT of the metal's reaction with water (a strongly alkaline MOH solution), not a property of the metal itself; they assume all Group 1 metals burning in oxygen produce the same simple oxide type (M₂O), missing that the specific oxide/peroxide/superoxide product genuinely depends on which cation size best stabilizes which anion size — sodium specifically forms the peroxide, not the simple oxide; and they transfer the halogen-group reactivity pattern (where reactivity decreases down the group) onto Group 1, assuming lithium (at the "top") must be the most reactive alkali metal, missing that metal reactivity trends in the OPPOSITE direction from non-metal reactivity, making lithium genuinely the LEAST reactive.

## 4. Misconception Library

### MC-1: Alkali metals are called "alkali" because they dissolve in alkalis
- **Probe**: "Why are Group 1 metals called alkali metals?"
- **Characteristic phrase**: "they like alkalis."
- **Trigger (Type 3, language contamination)**: The name "alkali metal" superficially suggests some kind of affinity or attraction relationship, rather than describing what the metal actually PRODUCES upon reaction.
- **Conflict evidence [P28]**: "Alkali" refers specifically to the PRODUCT of the metal's reaction with water — all Group 1 metals react with water to produce strongly alkaline solutions (MOH, fully-dissociating strong bases); the metals THEMSELVES are not alkalis (they're reactive metals) — they PRODUCE alkalis when reacted with water.
- **Bridge [P30]**: The name describes a causal/product relationship ("these metals PRODUCE alkalis when reacted with water"), not a descriptive/affinity relationship ("these metals ARE somehow alkaline or attracted to alkalinity") — a subtle but important linguistic distinction.
- **Replacement [P31]**: Group 1 metals are named "alkali metals" because reacting them with water produces strongly alkaline (basic) solutions — the name describes the reaction's product, not an inherent property of the metal itself.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]**: "The metal reacts to produce an alkali" (correct — a product relationship) vs. "the metal is itself an alkali or attracted to alkalis" (incorrect — a mistaken affinity/identity claim).
- **S6 repair path**: Present the explicit water-reaction equation (2M+2H₂O→2MOH+H₂), showing the alkaline MOH is the PRODUCT, distinct from the metal reactant itself.

### MC-2: All Group 1 metals burn in oxygen to form M₂O
- **Probe**: "Write the product of sodium burning in excess oxygen."
- **Characteristic phrase**: "sodium oxide forms."
- **Trigger (Type 1, overgeneralization)**: Students assume uniform group behavior (since Group 1 elements share the same valence-electron count and general reaction PATTERN), missing that the SPECIFIC oxide product type genuinely varies by cation size within the group.
- **Conflict evidence [P28]**: Sodium forms the PEROXIDE Na₂O₂ (oxygen oxidation state −1), not the simple oxide Na₂O, when burned in excess oxygen; potassium, rubidium, and cesium form SUPEROXIDES (oxygen oxidation state −½) instead; the specific oxide type depends on which anion size (O²⁻, O₂²⁻, or O₂⁻) is best stabilized by the specific cation's size — only lithium, the smallest Group 1 cation, is small enough to stabilize the compact O²⁻ ion, correctly forming the simple oxide Li₂O.
- **Bridge [P30]**: "Group means predictable product" is only reliable for the general REACTION TYPE (all Group 1 metals do react with oxygen), not for the SPECIFIC product identity, which depends on the size-matching between the specific cation and the various possible oxygen-anion species — a genuine, real exception to naive uniform-group-behavior expectations.
- **Replacement [P31]**: Li forms the simple oxide (Li₂O); Na forms the peroxide (Na₂O₂); K/Rb/Cs form superoxides — the specific product depends on cation-size-to-anion-size matching, not a single uniform M₂O product across the whole group.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]**: Li (smallest cation, stabilizes compact O²⁻, simple oxide) vs. Na (larger cation, stabilizes O₂²⁻, peroxide) vs. K/Rb/Cs (largest cations, stabilize O₂⁻, superoxides).
- **S6 repair path**: Present the cation-size-to-anion-stability relationship explicitly, connecting Na's specific size to its peroxide (not simple oxide) product.

### MC-3: Li is the most reactive because it's at the top of the group, like in the halogen series
- **Probe**: "Which reacts more vigorously with water — Li or Cs?"
- **Characteristic phrase**: "higher up = more reactive for Group 1."
- **Trigger (Type 5, instruction-induced)**: Students transfer the halogen group's reactivity-decreases-down-the-group pattern directly onto Group 1, assuming the same directional trend applies universally across all groups.
- **Conflict evidence [P28]**: Group 1 REACTIVITY INCREASES DOWN the group (Cs reacts far more vigorously with water than Li does) — since metal reactivity is governed by ease of electron LOSS (lower ionization energy going down the group, due to increasing atomic size/distance from nucleus), Li (highest IE₁ in the group, smallest radius, most charge-dense) is genuinely the LEAST reactive alkali metal, the exact OPPOSITE of a naive "top of the group = most reactive" assumption borrowed from the halogen trend.
- **Bridge [P30]**: Metals react by LOSING electrons (easier going down a group, as IE decreases); non-metals like halogens react by GAINING electrons (easier going up a group, as EA magnitude decreases going down) — these are fundamentally opposite reaction mechanisms, producing opposite reactivity-trend directions; a rule correct for one group type (non-metals) cannot be assumed to hold for the other (metals).
- **Replacement [P31]**: Group 1 metal reactivity increases down the group (Cs most reactive, Li least reactive) — this is the OPPOSITE direction from halogen (non-metal) reactivity trends, since metals and non-metals react via opposite electron-transfer mechanisms.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]**: Group 1 metals (reactivity increases down the group, Cs > Li) vs. Group 17 halogens (reactivity decreases down the group, F > I) — opposite directions for opposite reaction mechanisms.
- **S6 repair path**: Have the student explicitly state whether the element in question reacts by losing or gaining electrons before predicting the down-group reactivity direction.

## 5. Explanation Library

**Primary explanation**: "Alkali metal" names Group 1 elements for the alkaline (basic) product their reaction with water produces (MOH), not for any inherent alkaline property of the metals themselves. While all Group 1 metals share the general pattern of reacting with oxygen, the SPECIFIC product type (simple oxide, peroxide, or superoxide) genuinely varies with cation size — only lithium, the smallest cation, is small enough to stabilize the compact simple-oxide anion; larger cations preferentially stabilize the less compact peroxide or superoxide anions instead.

**Secondary explanation (opposite-trend-direction framing)**: Group 1 metal reactivity increases down the group, driven by decreasing ionization energy (easier electron loss) — genuinely opposite to non-metal groups like the halogens, where reactivity decreases down the group (driven by decreasing electron affinity magnitude, meaning harder electron gain); metal and non-metal reactivity trends move in opposite directions because they involve opposite electron-transfer mechanisms, and one group's trend direction should never be assumed to apply to another group type.

## 6. Analogy Library

- **Primary analogy**: A chef known for "making sweet dishes" — the chef themselves isn't sweet, but everything they PRODUCE is — exactly parallel to alkali metals not being alkalis themselves, but reliably PRODUCING alkaline solutions when they react with water.
- **Breaking point**: The chef analogy conveys the product-vs-inherent-property distinction well but doesn't naturally capture the cation-size-to-oxide-type relationship or the opposite metal/non-metal reactivity directions — those need the explicit size-matching and electron-transfer-mechanism arguments.
- **Anti-analogy**: Do NOT say "higher up the group means more reactive" without specifying metal or non-metal — this directly reinforces MC-3.

## 7. Demonstration Library

- **Demonstration 1 (oxide-type-by-cation-size comparison)**: Present the explicit Li/Na/K oxide-product differences (simple oxide, peroxide, superoxide) alongside their cation sizes, connecting size-matching directly to product type.
- **Demonstration 2 (Li-vs-Cs water-reaction vigor comparison)**: Present or describe the observed reaction vigor difference between Li and Cs with water, connecting the trend directly to their ionization energies.

## 8. Discovery Lesson

**Opening**: "If sodium is a Group 1 metal like lithium, do you expect it to form the same type of oxide (Na₂O) when burned in oxygen?"

**Exploration**: Students examine the actual observed product (Na₂O₂, the peroxide) and connect this to sodium's cation size relative to lithium's.

**Synthesis**: Guide toward: the specific oxide product depends on cation-size-to-anion-size matching, not uniform group behavior.

**Closure**: "Which reacts more vigorously with water: lithium or caesium?" (Directly resolves MC-3.)

## 9. Teaching Actions (session_cap = 4)

- **TA-1 (SHOW)**: Present the water-reaction equation explicitly, showing the alkaline MOH as the product, not a property of the metal.
- **TA-2 (TELL)**: State the cation-size-to-oxide-type relationship explicitly, worked through for Li/Na/K.
- **TA-3 (DO)**: Student predicts the correct oxide type for a given Group 1 metal using cation-size reasoning.
- **TA-4 (TEST-THINKING)**: Present MC-3's Li-vs-Cs probe and ask the student to justify the correct reactivity ranking using the electron-loss mechanism.

## 10. Voice Teaching

Whenever "alkali metal" is introduced, immediately clarify "this describes the PRODUCT of their reaction with water, not the metal itself" to preempt MC-1. Whenever Group 1 reactivity is discussed, explicitly contrast the direction with the halogen (non-metal) trend to preempt the trend-direction confusion.

## 11. Assessment

**Mastery gate**: Student can (a) correctly explain the "alkali metal" naming as describing the reaction product, not the metal, (b) correctly predict the specific oxide/peroxide/superoxide product for a given Group 1 metal using cation-size reasoning, (c) correctly rank Group 1 reactivity as increasing down the group, opposite the halogen trend.

- **FA-1**: "Why are Group 1 metals called alkali metals?" — targets MC-1.
- **FA-2**: "Write the product of sodium burning in excess oxygen." — targets MC-2.
- **FA-3**: "Which reacts more vigorously with water — Li or Cs?" — targets MC-3.

**Confidence calibration**: Predict high-confidence-wrong answers on FA-3 among students who've just studied halogen reactivity trends and are transferring that direction directly.

**Delayed retrieval**: Re-probe MC-2's cation-size-to-oxide-type relationship and MC-3's opposite-trend-direction principle before `chem.sblock.alkaline-earth` requires fluent, correct application of s-block periodic reasoning.

## 12. Recovery Notes

- **S3 (stuck)**: For the naming confusion, return to the explicit water-reaction equation and have the student identify which side of the equation the "alkali" (MOH) actually appears on.
- **S4 (frustrated)**: Normalize — the halogen-trend transfer is a very reasonable, common error, since students naturally look for a single, universal "down the group" rule.
- **S6 (collision)**: Use the explicit cation-size-to-oxide-type diagram for MC-2; use the electron-loss-mechanism explanation for MC-3.
- **S9 (post-repair check)**: Ask the student to explain, unprompted, why lithium is the least (not most) reactive alkali metal.
