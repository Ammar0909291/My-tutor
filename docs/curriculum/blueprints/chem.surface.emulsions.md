## 1. Concept Spine

Emulsifiers do NOT make oil and water miscible (dissolve into each other at the molecular level) — instead, they STABILIZE the interface between the two immiscible phases, keeping oil as discrete DROPLETS suspended in water (or vice versa), preventing those droplets from coalescing back into separate bulk layers — the phases remain genuinely, fundamentally immiscible throughout; and oil-in-water (O/W) and water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion types are NOT interchangeable descriptions — the CONTINUOUS (surrounding) phase, not simply which liquids are present, determines the emulsion's dominant texture and properties, so butter (water droplets in continuous fat, W/O) feels oily/greasy because the CONTINUOUS phase is oil, while milk (fat droplets in continuous water, O/W) feels aqueous because the CONTINUOUS phase is water — identical component liquids can form emulsions with opposite textures depending on which phase is continuous.

## 2. Four-Stage CPA+ Mental Model

**Concrete**: Comparing shaking oil and water alone (rapid phase separation) against shaking oil, water, and soap together (stable droplet suspension, no separation), isolating the emulsifier's interface-stabilizing role rather than any claim of molecular dissolution.

**Representational**: A side-by-side droplet diagram for butter (water droplets suspended in continuous oil/fat) and milk (fat droplets suspended in continuous water), labeling which phase is continuous in each case.

**Abstract**: The general principle that emulsifiers stabilize phase-separated droplet interfaces, never achieving true molecular miscibility; the general principle that an emulsion's texture/properties are dominated by its CONTINUOUS phase, not simply by which two liquids are present.

**Transfer**: Given an unfamiliar emulsion, correctly explaining the emulsifier's interface-stabilization role (never miscibility); given an unfamiliar emulsion's texture, correctly inferring which phase is continuous from the observed properties.

## 3. Why Beginners Fail

Students observe that adding soap to a shaken oil-water mixture prevents visible separation and infer that the soap must be causing the oil and water to actually DISSOLVE into each other (a molecular-level mixing, the everyday meaning of "miscible"), missing that the soap specifically stabilizes the INTERFACE between still-separate, still-immiscible oil droplets and the surrounding water — the oil remains as discrete droplets, never truly dissolved into the water phase; and students, upon learning that emulsions come in two types (O/W and W/O), fail to distinguish which phase is CONTINUOUS (the surrounding medium) from which is DISPERSED (the suspended droplets), missing that this specific structural distinction — not merely which two liquids are present — determines the emulsion's dominant texture and behavior, so two emulsions with identical component liquids (oil and water) can have opposite textures (oily vs. aqueous) depending on which phase happens to be continuous.

## 4. Misconception Library

### MC-1: Emulsifiers make oil and water miscible
- **Probe**: "If you add soap to oil and water and shake, do the oil and water dissolve into each other? What does the soap actually do?"
- **Characteristic phrase**: "Soap makes oil dissolve in water."
- **Trigger (Type 3, language contamination)**: The everyday sense of "mixing successfully" (no visible separation) is conflated with the technical meaning of "miscible" (molecular-level dissolution).
- **Conflict evidence [P28]**: Soap does not make oil dissolve in water (miscibility requires molecular-level mixing). It prevents phase separation by stabilising the interface — oil remains as droplets in water, but those droplets are stabilised against coalescence by the soap film at their surface.
- **Bridge [P30]**: "No visible separation" (the observable, everyday sign of "successful mixing") can arise from two genuinely different underlying mechanisms — true molecular-level dissolution (miscibility) or the stabilization of persistently separate microscopic droplets against coalescence (emulsification) — and emulsifiers specifically achieve the latter, coating and stabilizing droplet SURFACES rather than dissolving the droplets' contents into the surrounding phase.
- **Replacement [P31]**: Emulsifiers stabilize the interface between persistently separate oil and water droplets/phases, preventing coalescence — they never achieve true molecular-level miscibility between the two immiscible liquids.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]**: True miscibility (e.g., ethanol and water, molecular-level mixing, no discrete droplets) vs. emulsification (oil droplets stabilized within water by soap, discrete droplets persisting, genuinely still immiscible).
- **S6 repair path**: Present the explicit droplet-stabilization diagram, showing the soap film coating discrete oil droplets rather than any molecular dissolution.

### MC-2: O/W and W/O types are interchangeable
- **Probe**: "Butter feels oily and greasy. Is it oil-in-water or water-in-oil? How does the type relate to its texture?"
- **Characteristic phrase**: (implicit, from failing to distinguish continuous from dispersed phase).
- **Trigger (Type 1, overgeneralization)**: Students don't distinguish which phase is continuous (the surrounding medium) vs. dispersed (the droplets).
- **Conflict evidence [P28]**: Butter is W/O — water droplets in a continuous fat phase. The continuous oily phase gives the oily feel. Milk is O/W — fat droplets in a continuous water phase. The continuous water phase gives the aqueous feel. The continuous phase dominates the texture.
- **Bridge [P30]**: An emulsion's macroscopic, observable properties (texture, feel, how it interacts with your skin or other materials) are determined predominantly by whichever phase is CONTINUOUS (the phase you're actually in contact with at any given point, since it surrounds the dispersed droplets) — the dispersed phase's droplets are, by definition, discrete and enclosed, contributing much less to the bulk material's surface-contact properties than the continuous phase that actually surrounds and connects throughout the material.
- **Replacement [P31]**: Always identify which phase is continuous (not merely which liquids are present) before predicting an emulsion's texture/properties — O/W and W/O with the same component liquids have genuinely opposite textures.
- **Discrimination pairs [P33]**: Butter (W/O, continuous fat phase, oily texture) vs. milk (O/W, continuous water phase, aqueous texture) — same two component liquids (fat and water), opposite continuous phase, opposite texture.
- **S6 repair path**: Present the explicit continuous-vs-dispersed-phase diagram for both butter and milk, deriving each texture from its specific continuous phase.

## 5. Explanation Library

**Primary explanation**: Emulsifiers achieve visible "no separation" not by making genuinely immiscible liquids miscible, but by stabilizing the interface of persistently discrete droplets against coalescence — the two phases remain fundamentally, molecularly immiscible throughout, with the emulsifier coating droplet surfaces rather than dissolving droplet contents.

**Secondary explanation (continuous phase determines texture)**: An emulsion's dominant macroscopic texture and properties are determined by which phase is CONTINUOUS (surrounding the dispersed droplets), not merely by which two liquids are present — identical component liquids (like fat and water) can produce emulsions with opposite textures (butter's oily W/O vs. milk's aqueous O/W) depending on which phase is continuous.

## 6. Analogy Library

- **Primary analogy**: Marbles (dispersed oil droplets) coated in a thin protective film (emulsifier) suspended throughout a pool of water (continuous phase) — the marbles never dissolve INTO the water, they remain discrete, protected objects merely suspended within it.
- **Breaking point**: The coated-marbles analogy conveys the persistent-droplet, interface-stabilization concept well but doesn't naturally capture the continuous-phase-determines-texture principle (MC-2) — that needs the explicit butter-vs-milk structural comparison.
- **Anti-analogy**: Do NOT say "soap dissolves oil into water, just like sugar dissolves in water" — this directly reinforces MC-1 by conflating emulsification with true molecular dissolution.

## 7. Demonstration Library

- **Demonstration 1 (droplet-stabilization diagram for soap-emulsified oil-water)**: Present the explicit soap-film-coated-droplet diagram, contrasted with a hypothetical (incorrect) molecular-dissolution picture.
- **Demonstration 2 (continuous-vs-dispersed-phase diagram for butter and milk)**: Present both structural diagrams explicitly, deriving each material's texture from its continuous phase.

## 8. Discovery Lesson

**Opening**: "When soap prevents oil and water from separating after shaking, does the oil actually dissolve into the water?"

**Exploration**: Students examine the droplet-stabilization mechanism, discovering the oil remains as discrete, stabilized droplets rather than dissolving.

**Synthesis**: Guide toward: emulsifiers stabilize persistent droplet interfaces, never achieving true molecular miscibility.

**Closure**: "Butter feels oily. Is it oil-in-water or water-in-oil, and why does that determine its feel?" (Directly resolves MC-2.)

## 9. Teaching Actions (session_cap = 4)

- **TA-1 (SHOW)**: Present the explicit droplet-stabilization diagram for soap-emulsified oil-water.
- **TA-2 (TELL)**: State the continuous-phase-determines-texture principle explicitly, anchored to the butter-vs-milk comparison.
- **TA-3 (DO)**: Student predicts texture for an unfamiliar emulsion given its continuous-phase identity.
- **TA-4 (TEST-THINKING)**: Present the "does soap dissolve oil" probe and ask the student to justify the interface-stabilization (not miscibility) mechanism.

## 10. Voice Teaching

Whenever an emulsion is discussed, narrate "the phases stay separate — the emulsifier stabilizes the interface, never dissolves one into the other." Whenever emulsion texture is predicted, state "identify the continuous phase first — that's what determines the feel" as the standing reminder.

## 11. Assessment

**Mastery gate**: Student can (a) correctly explain emulsifiers as interface stabilizers, never achieving true miscibility, (b) correctly predict emulsion texture from continuous-phase identity.

- **FA-1**: "If you add soap to oil and water and shake, do the oil and water dissolve into each other? What does the soap actually do?" — targets MC-1.
- **FA-2**: "Butter feels oily and greasy. Is it oil-in-water or water-in-oil? How does the type relate to its texture?" — targets MC-2.

**Confidence calibration**: Predict high-confidence-wrong answers on MC-1 among students who interpret "no visible separation" as evidence of true dissolution.

**Delayed retrieval**: Re-probe MC-1's interface-stabilization mechanism and MC-2's continuous-phase-texture principle as foundational knowledge for subsequent food science and formulation-chemistry applications.

## 12. Recovery Notes

- **S3 (stuck)**: For the miscibility confusion, have the student explicitly identify whether discrete droplets persist (emulsion) or truly dissolve (miscibility) before concluding.
- **S4 (frustrated)**: Normalize — conflating "no visible separation" with true dissolution is genuinely common on first exposure to emulsions.
- **S6 (collision)**: Use the explicit continuous-vs-dispersed-phase diagram for MC-2.
- **S9 (post-repair check)**: Ask the student to explain, unprompted, why butter and milk have opposite textures despite containing the same two component liquids.
