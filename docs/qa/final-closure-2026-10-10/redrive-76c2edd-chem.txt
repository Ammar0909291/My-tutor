Welcome to **Rate Law and Order** – a lesson about how we describe the speed of a chemical reaction and how the amounts of the reactants control that speed. By the end you’ll be able to write a rate law, tell the order of each reactant, find the overall order, work with the rate constant k (including its units), and use the initial‑rate method to determine those numbers.  

Imagine a kitchen faucet: when you turn the handle a little, a thin stream of water drips out; turn it more, and a strong stream rushes out. In a chemical reaction, the “handle” is the concentration of each reactant – opening it more (adding more molecules) makes the reaction go faster, just like the water flow. Picture countless tiny particles bouncing around; the more of them there are, the more frequent the collisions that lead to product formation.  

In plain language, a **rate law** tells us how the reaction rate depends on the concentrations of the reactants. It’s written as  

\[
\text{rate} = k\,[A]^m\,[B]^n
\]

where *k* is the rate constant, *m* and *n* are the orders with respect to reactants A and B.

Let’s see how the **units of the rate constant k** change when the overall order of a reaction changes.

Imagine we measure a reaction rate in **moles per litre per second** (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹). The rate law has the form 

\[
\text{rate} = k\,[\text{A}]^{m}\,[\text{B}]^{n}
\]

where the concentration of each reactant is expressed in **moles per litre** (mol L⁻¹). 

To find the units of k, we rearrange the equation:

\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{A}]^{m}\,[\text{B}]^{n}}
\]

Now plug in the units:

* **Zero‑order overall** (m + n = 0): the denominator is dimensionless, so 
 \[
 k \text{ has units of } \text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}. \]

* **First‑order overall** (m + n = 1): denominator contributes one factor of mol L⁻¹, so 
 \[
 k \text{ has units of } \text{s}^{-1}. \]

* **Second‑order overall** (m + n = 2): denominator contributes (mol L⁻¹)², giving 
 \[
 k \text{ has units of } \text{L mol}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}. \]

* **General nth‑order overall** (m + n = n): 
 \[
 k \text{ units } = \frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{\,n}}
 = \text{L}^{\,n-1}\,\text{mol}^{\,1-n}\,\text{s}^{-1}. \]

**Example:** For a reaction that is overall second order, say 

\[
\text{rate} = k[\text{A}]^{2}
\]

If the measured rate is 0.02 mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ and [A] = 0.10 mol L⁻¹, then 

\[
k = \frac{0.02}{(0.10)^{2}} = \frac{0.02}{0.01}=2.0\;\text{L mol}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1},
\]

which matches the unit we derived for a second‑order reaction.

These unit patterns let you check whether the order you’ve determined experimentally makes sense—if the calculated k ends up with the wrong units, something’s off with the assumed orders.

Corrosion (like iron RUSTING) is essentially an unwanted, spontaneous ELECTROCHEMICAL process — a tiny galvanic cell forms directly ON the metal surface. Remember that the rusting of iron is really a tiny galvanic cell built into the metal itself. In any galvanic pair the metal with the **more negative standard reduction potential** acts as the **anode** (it loses electrons), while the metal with the less negative (or more positive) potential becomes the **cathode** (it gains electrons). 

Here are the relevant potentials (E°) for the three metals we’re discussing:

- **Iron (Fe²⁺/Fe)** E° ≈ ‑0.44 V 
- **Zinc (Zn²⁺/Zn)** E° ≈ ‑0.76 V 
- **Tin (Sn²⁺/Sn)** E° ≈ ‑0.14 V 

Because **zinc’s potential is the most negative**, when a piece of zinc touches iron it becomes the anode and corrodes first. The iron then acts as the cathode and is protected—even if the zinc coating is scratched, the sacrificial zinc continues to dissolve and “take the hit” for the iron. This is why **galvanizing** (coating iron with zinc) works as a long‑lasting protection method.

In contrast, **tin’s potential is less negative than iron’s**. When iron is coated with tin and the coating is damaged, the iron becomes the anode while the tin becomes the cathode. The iron now corrodes faster at the breach, and the tin layer offers only a physical barrier when intact. That’s why **tinning** does not provide sacrificial protection—it merely blocks moisture and oxygen until the coating is broken.

So the key idea is: **compare electrode potentials to decide which metal will act as the sacrificial anode**. The more negative metal sacrifices itself, protecting the more positive one.

**Lesson: Electron Flow and Arrow Notation**  

In this lesson we’ll learn how chemists draw the movement of electrons with curved arrows. By the end, you’ll be able to read and write these arrows to show bond breaking and making in reactions such as SN2, E2, and addition.

Think of a **ball rolling along a curved track** on a playground. The ball’s path is clear: it starts at one point, follows the curve, and ends at another point.  

In chemistry, the “ball” is a pair of electrons, and the “track” is the curved arrow that chemists draw on a reaction diagram. When a reaction occurs, electrons leave one place (the start of the arrow) and arrive at another (the tip of the arrow), just like the ball moving along its track.

Picture a tiny, invisible arrow curving from a lone‑pair of electrons on one atom toward a bond that is about to form on another atom. The arrow tells us *where* the electrons are coming from and *where* they are going.

The curved arrow is a shorthand that shows the flow of electrons during a chemical change. It always begins at the source of electrons (a lone pair or a bond) and points to the destination (a new bond or a place where a bond will break).

**curved‑arrow notation**  

**New term:** **nucleophile** – an atom or molecule that has a pair of electrons it can donate to form a new bond.
