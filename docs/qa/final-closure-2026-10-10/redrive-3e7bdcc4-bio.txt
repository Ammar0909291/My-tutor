**Lesson: Innate Behaviour and Instinct**

In this lesson we’ll explore behaviours that animals are born with – they don’t have to learn them. By the end you’ll be able to recognise fixed action patterns, understand what triggers them, tell reflexes apart from more complex instincts, and explain how genes shape these innate actions.
Imagine a simple music box. When you turn the key, a tiny metal comb is plucked and the same short melody plays every time, without any choice or change.

Now picture a real animal: a male stickleback fish sees a bright red patch on another fish’s belly. In an instant it darts toward the red spot and attacks, even though it has never been taught to do this.

Think of a “ready‑made” response that pops out the moment the right signal appears, just like the music box automatically starts its tune when wound.

Many animals carry built‑in programs that run a set sequence of actions automatically. These sequences are very stereotyped – they look the same each time – and they are hard to modify by experience.

**Fixed action pattern (FAP)** – a hard‑wired, stereotyped behavioural sequence that is released by a specific cue.

**Key term to add:** **Stereotyped** – meaning the behavior looks the same every time it occurs.

Fixed action patterns (FAPs) are stereotyped (performed the same way across individuals of a species) and largely unmodifiable behavioural sequences — once triggered, a FAP typically runs to completion in its characteristic form, largely independent of ongoing feedback. Critically, a FAP is NOT spontaneous: it requires a specific sign stimulus (a specific, often simple environmental cue) to release it — the sequence is performed only when that specific trigger is present. Innate behaviour must be further distinguished from simple reflexes: a reflex is a direct, typically single-muscle-group stimulus-response (like the knee-jerk reflex) — simple, immediate, minimal neural processing. A fixed action pattern is substantially more complex, coordinated, and multi-step (like an elaborate courtship display or nest-building sequence) — many more coordinated movements over an extended time, though still triggered by a specific sign stimulus and still largely stereotyped once initiated. Innate behaviour also has a genetic basis: it is a heritable trait shaped by natural selection over evolutionary time, exactly as physical structures are — a FAP that reliably improves survival or reproduction can be favoured by selection and become a stable, species-typical trait.

**Lesson: Photosynthesis**  

In this lesson we’ll explore how plants turn sunlight into food. By the end you’ll be able to describe the light‑dependent reactions, name the two photosystems, explain how electrons move to make ATP and NADPH, and outline the Calvin cycle and the special C₄ and CAM adaptations.

---

Imagine a **solar panel** on a roof. It catches sunlight and changes it into electricity that powers a house.  

Now picture a **green leaf** on a tree. Inside each leaf are tiny factories called **chloroplasts** that work just like that solar panel, but instead of electricity they make chemical energy for the plant.  

Think of the leaf as a miniature power plant: sunlight hits the leaf, the plant’s pigment **chlorophyll** (the green “solar cells”) absorbs the light, and the energy is used to split water molecules and create energy‑carrying molecules.  

In plain words, the **light‑dependent reactions** (often just called the *light reactions*) are the first stage of photosynthesis. They capture light energy, break water (H₂O) into oxygen, protons, and electrons, and produce two energy carriers—**ATP** and **NADPH**—that will later fuel the next stage, the Calvin cycle.  

*Key term:* **chlorophyll** – the green pigment that grabs light energy.

Let’s look at a concrete situation: imagine a plant growing in a very hot, dry desert where water is scarce and the air is full of oxygen. In these conditions, the ordinary “C3” Calvin cycle that we just saw can waste a lot of energy because the enzyme RuBisCO starts grabbing oxygen instead of carbon dioxide – a process called **photorespiration**. Photorespiration uses up the ATP and NADPH the light reactions made, and it also releases CO₂ that the plant has already spent energy to capture.

To avoid this waste, some plants have evolved special versions of photosynthesis:

**C4 pathway** – In C4 plants (like corn and sugarcane), CO₂ is first captured in the leaf’s outer cells by an enzyme called PEP carboxylase, which does not bind oxygen. The CO₂ is turned into a four‑carbon compound and then shuttled to inner bundle‑sheath cells where the Calvin cycle runs. By concentrating CO₂ around RuBisCO, the plant greatly reduces photorespiration.

**CAM pathway** – In CAM plants (such as cacti and pineapple), the leaf opens its tiny pores (stomata) at night when it’s cooler and more humid. CO₂ is taken in and fixed into a four‑carbon acid (malate) and stored in vacuoles. During the hot daytime, the stomata stay closed to save water, and the stored malate releases CO₂ for the Calvin cycle. This temporal separation also limits photorespiration and water loss.

These adaptations show how plants can tweak the two‑stage photosynthetic process to thrive in challenging environments, still using the light‑dependent reactions to make ATP and NADPH, but feeding the Calvin cycle with a steady, protected supply of CO₂.

Let’s walk through a single, concrete picture of what happens in a leaf cell during photosynthesis, using the diagram you’re looking at.

1. **Light‑dependent reactions (the blue box on the left).** 
 Sunlight hits chlorophyll in the thylakoid membrane. The energy splits a water molecule — this is called *photolysis* — producing one molecule of oxygen (O₂) that leaves the leaf, two electrons that travel through the electron‑transport chain, and a proton that helps make ATP. At the end of this stage the thylakoid produces **3 ATP** and **2 NADPH** for every pair of photons absorbed.

2. **Calvin cycle (the blue box on the right).** 
 The ATP and NADPH travel through the grey arrow into the stroma, where carbon dioxide (CO₂) is fixed. The enzyme RuBisCO attaches CO₂ to a five‑carbon sugar called **RuBP** (ribulose‑1,5‑bisphosphate). After a series of reductions, the energy from the 3 ATP and 2 NADPH turns the attached carbon into one molecule of G3P (glyceraldehyde‑3‑phosphate). Two G3P molecules later combine to make one glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), while the remaining RuBP is regenerated to keep the cycle going.

So, in this single “run” of the cycle, light energy captured in the thylakoid is turned into the chemical energy stored in glucose, and the arrow in the figure shows exactly where the ATP and NADPH move from the light‑dependent reactions to power the Calvin cycle.
