**Lesson: Innate Behaviour and Instinct**  
In this lesson we’ll explore behaviours that animals are born with – they don’t have to learn them. By the end you’ll be able to recognize fixed action patterns, the cues that trigger them, how they differ from simple reflexes, and why genetics shapes these innate actions.

Imagine a chicken spotting a tiny, wriggling worm on the ground. When it sees that worm‑like shape, the chicken’s head darts forward, its beak opens, it snaps shut, pulls the worm in, and then repeats the motion exactly the same way each time. This whole, unchanging sequence of movements is called a **fixed action pattern (FAP)** – a stereotyped behaviour that runs its course once triggered.

Fixed action patterns (FAPs) are stereotyped (performed the same way across individuals of a species) and largely unmodifiable behavioural sequences — once triggered, a FAP typically runs to completion in its characteristic form, largely independent of ongoing feedback. Critically, a FAP is NOT spontaneous: it requires a specific sign stimulus (a specific, often simple environmental cue) to release it — the sequence is performed only when that specific trigger is present. Innate behaviour must be further distinguished from simple reflexes: a reflex is a direct, typically single-muscle-group stimulus-response (like the knee-jerk reflex) — simple, immediate, minimal neural processing. A fixed action pattern is substantially more complex, coordinated, and multi-step (like an elaborate courtship display or nest-building sequence) — many more coordinated movements over an extended time, though still triggered by a specific sign stimulus and still largely stereotyped once initiated. Innate behaviour also has a genetic basis: it is a heritable trait shaped by natural selection over evolutionary time, exactly as physical structures are — a FAP that reliably improves survival or reproduction can be favoured by selection and become a stable, species-typical trait.

**Lesson: Photosynthesis**  

In this lesson we’ll explore how plants turn sunlight into food. By the end you’ll be able to:

* Describe the two sets of reactions (light and dark) that make glucose.  
* Explain the roles of photosystem I, photosystem II, the electron‑transport chain, and photophosphorylation.  
* Outline the steps of the Calvin (C₃) cycle and how C₄ and CAM plants adapt to different environments.

---

Imagine a **green leaf** on a sunny windowsill.  
When the leaf is in bright light, it works like a tiny solar panel, catching sunlight and using it to power a series of chemical steps.  

Picture the leaf as a factory: sunlight is the energy that powers machines (the photosystems), moving tiny particles (electrons) along a conveyor belt (the electron‑transport chain) to build a product—glucose.  

In plain words, **photosynthesis** is the process plants use to convert light energy, water, and carbon dioxide into sugars and oxygen.  

*Key term:* **photosystem** – a protein‑pigment complex that captures light and starts the flow of electrons.  

Now, let’s look at the first part of the process: **the light reactions** (the “solar‑panel” stage).

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy (glucose), using CO2 and water as raw materials. Overall equation: 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6O2. Two stages occur in the chloroplast. Light-dependent reactions (thylakoid membranes): light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll; water is split (photolysis), releasing O2 as a by-product; the energy is used to produce ATP and NADPH (energy carriers). Light-independent reactions / Calvin cycle (stroma): ATP and NADPH from stage 1 drive the fixation of CO2 into glucose via a cycle of reactions. CO2 combines with RuBP (a 5-carbon compound) via RuBisCO enzyme; the products are reduced using ATP and NADPH to produce G3P, which is used to make glucose. RuBP is regenerated to continue the cycle. Key point: O2 is released from water splitting, not from CO2.

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Here’s a concrete walk‑through of photosynthesis in a typical C₃ plant such as wheat:

**Light‑dependent reactions (in the thylakoid membrane)** 
1. **Water splitting (photolysis):** Two H₂O molecules are broken by light absorbed by chlorophyll in photosystem II, releasing 4 H⁺, 4 e⁻ and O₂ (the oxygen you see leaving the leaf). 
2. **Electron flow:** The electrons travel from photosystem II through plastoquinone, the cytochrome b₆f complex, plastocyanin, and finally to photosystem I. 
3. **NADPH formation:** After photosystem I re‑excites the electrons, they reduce NADP⁺ + H⁺ to NADPH. 
4. **ATP synthesis:** The proton gradient built by the cytochrome b₆f complex drives ATP synthase, making ATP from ADP + Pᵢ.

**Calvin cycle (in the stroma)** 
1. **CO₂ fixation:** The enzyme **RuBisCO** attaches CO₂ to ribulose‑1,5‑bisphosphate (RuBP), forming an unstable six‑carbon intermediate that splits into two molecules of 3‑phosphoglycerate (3‑PGA). 
2. **Reduction:** Using ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, 3‑PGA is converted to glyceraldehyde‑3‑phosphate (G3P). 
3. **Regeneration:** Some G3P leaves the cycle to eventually become glucose, while the rest is used, with more ATP, to regenerate RuBP, allowing the cycle to continue.

**Result:** For every three CO₂ molecules fixed, the cycle yields one net G3P molecule; two of these (from six CO₂) can be linked together to form one glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) after several turns.
