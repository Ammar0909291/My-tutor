[open provider=groq fig=none flags=-]
**SI Units and Measurement** – In this lesson we’ll explore the International System of Units (SI) and learn how the seven base units let us describe any physical quantity. By the end, you’ll be able to name each base unit, recognize the everyday objects that represent them, and use the correct symbols when you write measurements.

Imagine a simple wooden ruler you might keep on a desk. You use it every day to check how long a notebook or a piece of paper is. When you line the ruler up with the edge of the notebook, you’re matching the notebook’s length to the marked “centimeter” and “meter” marks on the ruler. That act of matching the object’s size to a standard scale is what we call **measuring length**.  

The first SI base unit is the **metre (m)**, which defines length. It’s based on the distance light travels in a vacuum in 1⁄299 792 458 seconds.  

What do you notice about how we use a ruler to measure length?

The second is one of the seven SI base units, so it cannot be the one that is not a base unit.

The newton is a derived unit. It is defined as the force needed to accelerate one kilogram of mass at one metre per second squared, so its symbols are kg·m·s⁻². Only the metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, and candela are base units in the SI system.

Imagine ordering a table "four hands wide" from a carpenter with bigger hands than yours. You'd get the wrong table — not because anyone measured badly, but because you weren't using the same ruler. That's the whole reason SI units exist: the world agreed on ONE ruler for each kind of quantity, so a metre in Tokyo is exactly a metre in Delhi. There are only seven of these agreed base units — metre for length, kilogram for mass, second for time, ampere for current, kelvin for temperature, mole for amount of substance, candela for light intensity. Every other unit you will ever meet is built out of these seven, the way every word is built from letters.

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Let’s look at a simple real‑world case that uses the units you just saw on the figure.

A small box of books has a **mass of 4 kilograms** (the unit “kilogram” is shown at the top‑right of the figure). 
If you push the box so that it speeds up at **1.5 metres per second squared** (the unit “metre” is at the top of the figure, and “second” is at the right), the force you apply is

\[
\text{force}= \text{mass} \times \text{acceleration}
\]

\[
F = 4\;\text{kg} \times 1.5\;\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}
 = 6\;\text{kg·m/s}^2
\]

The product “kilogram·metre per second‑squared” is exactly the definition of a **newton**, which the figure lists at the bottom (“1 N = 1 kg·m/s²”). So the force you exert is **6 newtons**.

The kelvin scale starts at absolute zero, the point where molecular motion stops. Celsius values are offset from kelvin by 273.15 degrees, so 0 °C equals 273.15 K. Using kelvin ensures calculations are based on an absolute reference, which is required for scientific work.

If you count 5 apples, "apples" tells you WHAT you counted — it does not tell anyone how big anything is. That is the difference between a thing and a unit. A unit is an agreed amount that everybody has promised to compare against, and an apple is not one, because apples come in different sizes. Two people can both honestly say "5 apples" and be holding very different amounts of fruit.

Try it with a recipe. "Add 200 grams of sugar" works anywhere in the world. "Add 3 apples of sugar" does not, because nobody knows how much sugar an apple is. The gram is a unit; the apple is a thing.

So a number in science answers two questions, not one. HOW MANY is the count. OF WHAT AGREED AMOUNT is the unit. Counting apples only ever answers the first one.
