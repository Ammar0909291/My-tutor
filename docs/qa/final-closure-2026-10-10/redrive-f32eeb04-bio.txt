**Lesson: Innate Behaviour and Instinct**  
In this lesson we’ll explore behaviours that animals are born with – they don’t have to learn them. By the end you will be able to:

* describe what a *fixed action pattern* is and give an example,  
* recognize the *sign stimulus* that triggers such a pattern,  
* tell the difference between a simple reflex and a more complex instinct, and  
* explain how genetics and natural selection shape these innate behaviours.
Let’s start with the first idea: **fixed action patterns**.

Imagine a **paper airplane** that you fold exactly the same way every time. No matter who makes it, the folds follow the same steps and the finished plane always looks alike.

Now picture a **hummingbird** that sees a bright red flower. It darts straight toward the flower, hovers, inserts its long bill, sips nectar, and then flies away – all in a smooth, unchanging sequence.

The hummingbird’s whole feeding routine looks like a short, rehearsed dance that never changes, no matter the individual bird.

A fixed action pattern (often called a “stereotyped behaviour”) is a series of actions that an animal performs automatically in the same order every time a particular cue appears. The animal doesn’t need to think about each step; the whole pattern runs like a pre‑programmed script.

Fixed action pattern (FAP)  
**New term:** *stereotyped* – meaning the behaviour looks the same each time it occurs.

Now, let’s see this in action. A classic example is the **egg‑rolling behavior of the common cuckoo**. When a cuckoo chick hatches, it has an instinct to push other eggs out of the nest. The chick doesn’t learn this; it simply performs the same rolling motion whenever it feels the presence of other eggs.

Fixed action patterns (FAPs) are stereotyped (performed the same way across individuals of a species) and largely unmodifiable behavioural sequences — once triggered, a FAP typically runs to completion in its characteristic form, largely independent of ongoing feedback. Critically, a FAP is not spontaneous: it requires a specific sign stimulus (a specific, often simple environmental cue) to release it — the sequence is performed only when that specific trigger is present. Innate behaviour must be further distinguished from simple reflexes: a reflex is a direct, typically single-muscle-group stimulus-response (like the knee-jerk reflex) — simple, immediate, minimal neural processing. A fixed action pattern is substantially more complex, coordinated, and multi-step (like an elaborate courtship display or nest-building sequence) — many more coordinated movements over an extended time, though still triggered by a specific sign stimulus and still largely stereotyped once initiated. Innate behaviour also has a genetic basis: it is a heritable trait shaped by natural selection over evolutionary time, exactly as physical structures are — a FAP that reliably improves survival or reproduction can be favoured by selection and become a stable, species-typical trait.

**Photosynthesis** – the process plants use to turn sunlight into food. By the end of this lesson you’ll be able to describe how light and dark reactions work, name the two photosystems, explain electron transport and photophosphorylation, and outline the Calvin cycle as well as the C₄ and CAM adaptations.

Think of a **solar‑powered garden light**: the tiny panel on its back catches sunlight and turns it into electrical energy that powers the bulb. In a real leaf, tiny “solar panels” called **chloroplasts** do the same thing, but instead of electricity they make chemical energy that the plant can use to grow.

Picture each chloroplast as a miniature factory: the roof (the thylakoid membranes) captures light, and inside the factory workers (molecules) assemble sugar from carbon dioxide and water.

In plain language, **photosynthesis** is the set of reactions that convert light energy into the chemical energy stored in sugars, using water, carbon dioxide, and a pigment called **chlorophyll** that absorbs the light.

The light‑dependent reactions actually consist of two linked protein complexes called **photosystem II** and **photosystem I** that sit in the thylakoid membrane.  

First, photosystem II captures photons and uses that energy to split water molecules, releasing oxygen and passing the freed electrons into an electron‑transport chain. As the electrons move along the chain, their energy is used to pump protons into the thylakoid space, creating a gradient that will later drive the synthesis of ATP.  

When the electrons reach photosystem I, they are boosted again by another burst of light. This second boost lets photosystem I transfer the electrons to the carrier molecule NADP⁺, forming NADPH.  

Together, the ATP made by the proton gradient and the NADPH produced by photosystem I supply the energy and reducing power that the Calvin cycle (the dark reaction) needs to turn carbon dioxide into sugar.

Let’s walk through a single, concrete “snapshot” of photosynthesis in a leaf cell.

First, imagine a chlorophyll molecule in the thylakoid membrane catching a photon of red light. That energy splits a water (H₂O) molecule—photolysis—into two electrons, two protons (H⁺), and one oxygen atom that quickly pairs with another to become O₂, which you see bubbling out of the leaf. The two electrons travel through the electron‑transport chain, helping pump protons into the thylakoid space. When the protons flow back through ATP synthase, one molecule of ATP is made. At the end of the chain, the electrons reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH.

Now the light‑dependent reactions have produced **3 ATP** and **2 NADPH** (roughly the yield from about 8 absorbed photons). Those energy carriers flow via the grey arrow in the figure into the right‑hand side, the Calvin Cycle, which sits in the stroma.

In the Calvin Cycle, three molecules of carbon dioxide (CO₂) are fixed onto three molecules of ribulose‑1,5‑bisphosphate (RuBP) with the help of the enzyme RuBisCO. Using the **3 ATP** and **2 NADPH** that arrived, the cycle reduces the attached carbon atoms to two molecules of glyceraldehyde‑3‑phosphate (G3P). One G3P can leave the cycle to eventually become glucose, while the other three are used to regenerate RuBP, allowing the cycle to continue.
