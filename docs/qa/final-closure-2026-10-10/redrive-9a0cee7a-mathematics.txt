**Lesson: Linear Equation in One Variable**

Imagine a simple kitchen scale. You place a bag of flour on one side, and on the other side you add a known weight (say, a 2‑kg brick) plus an unknown amount of flour you want to find. The scale balances when the total weight on both sides is the same.

In everyday life, this is like buying fruit: you know the total cost you paid, you know the price per kilogram, and you want to figure out how many kilograms you bought.

🟦 = 🟥 + ❓ (known weight + unknown weight = total weight)

We can write a statement that says “the known part plus the unknown part equals the total.” That statement is called an *equation*. When the equation involves only one unknown number (the ❓), and the unknown is multiplied by a number and maybe added to another number, we have a **linear equation in one variable**.

**Key terms:**  
- **Variable** – the unknown quantity (we’ll use *x*).  
- **Coefficient** – the number multiplied by the variable (the “a” in *a x*).  
- **Constant term** – a number added or subtracted (the “b” in *a x + b*).  

When everything is balanced, the equation looks like:

*a x* + *b* = *c*, with *a* ≠ 0.

Consider the equation  

\[
x + 3 = 7
\]

(the left side has the unknown \(x\) plus three, the right side is seven). To keep the balance, we do the same operation on **both** sides: subtract 3.

\[
\begin{aligned}
x + 3 \;-\; 3 &= 7 \;-\; 3 \\
x &= 4
\end{aligned}
\]

Now check the solution by putting \(x = 4\) back into the original equation:  

\(4 + 3 = 7\) which is true, so the answer is correct. 

**Balance principle:** whatever you do to one side of an equation, you must do identically to the other side to preserve equality.

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Imagine a kitchen scale that is perfectly balanced. On the left pan you place **3 × x** (three times the weight of an unknown number of apples, x) together with a bag that weighs **5 units**. On the right pan you put a bag that weighs **20 units**. The scale stays level, so the total weight on each side is the same.

In words, this situation is described by the equation 

\[
3x + 5 = 20
\] 

(three x plus five equals twenty). Our goal is to find the single value of x that makes the two sides balance.

**Step 1 – Remove the constant term:** 
To get rid of the + 5 on the left, we subtract 5 from **both** sides of the equation (the balance principle says we must do the same thing to each side). 

\[
3x + 5 - 5 = 20 - 5 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 3x = 15
\]

**Step 2 – Isolate the variable term:** 
Now the unknown x is multiplied by 3. To undo this multiplication, we divide **both** sides by 3. 

\[
\frac{3x}{3} = \frac{15}{3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad x = 5
\]

**Check:** Put x = 5 back into the original equation: 

\[
3(5) + 5 = 15 + 5 = 20,
\] 

which is true, so the solution is correct.

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=analogyRepeat]
Imagine a simple playground seesaw that stays level only when the total weight on the left side equals the total weight on the right side. 
If you put a 5‑kilogram backpack on the left, the seesaw tilts. To bring it back level you can either add the same 5 kilograms to the right side **or** take the 5 kilograms off the left side. The key idea is that whatever you do to one side, you must do the same to the other side to keep the balance.

A linear equation works the same way. One side of the equation is like the left side of the seesaw, the other side is the right side. The “ x ” term is a weight we don’t know yet, and the numbers are other known weights. To find the value of x, we keep the seesaw balanced by performing the same arithmetic operation on both sides—just like adding or removing weight on both ends of the seesaw.

For example, with the equation 3x + 5 = 20, think of 3x as an unknown weight on the left and 5 kg as an extra weight already there. First we **remove** the 5 kg from the left side by taking 5 away from both sides (just like taking the same weight off both ends). That gives 3x = 15. Now we have only the unknown weight 3x on the left, and we know the total on that side must be 15 kg. To find the weight of one “x”, we **divide** both sides by 3 (splitting the total weight equally among the three identical “x” blocks). So x = 5.

**What do you notice about the two sides of the equation when we take the same number away from both sides?**
