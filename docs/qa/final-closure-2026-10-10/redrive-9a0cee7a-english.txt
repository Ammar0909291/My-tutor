**Lesson: Verbs**  

In this lesson we’ll look at words that tell us what someone or something does, or what state they are in. By the end you’ll be able to spot and use the main verbs and helping (auxiliary) verbs in simple sentences.  

Let’s start with something you see every day: a **ball**.  

Imagine you’re at the park and you **throw** the ball to a friend. In that moment, the word *throw* tells us the action that’s happening.  

Think of a short picture in your mind: a person’s arm moves, the ball flies, and the friend catches it. The word that describes that movement is a **verb**.  

In plain language, a **verb** is a word that expresses an action (like *run*, *eat*, *throw*) or a state of being (like *is*, *feel*, *seem*).  

**Verb** – the name for these action‑or‑state words.

Let’s look at a sentence that uses a helping (auxiliary) verb:

**She is reading a book.** 
Here the words **is reading** together tell us two things: the action **reading** is happening, and it is happening **right now**. The word **is** isn’t just extra; it is a verb that works with the main verb **reading** to show the present‑continuous tense. If we drop **is**, we get “She reading a book,” which is not a correct English sentence.

In English, certain verbs always act as helpers. They join with another verb to give information about **time (tense), duration (aspect), ability, permission, obligation, or possibility**. The most common helping verbs are:

- **be** forms: am, is, are, was, were 
- **have** forms: have, has, had 
- **modal** verbs: will, would, can, could, may, might, must, shall, should, do, does, did 

When a helping verb pairs with a main verb, the whole pair is called a **verb phrase**, and it functions as the single verb of the sentence.

Let’s look at a simple sentence that shows both a main verb and a helping (auxiliary) verb:

**Sentence:** *She has understood the instructions.*

**What’s happening here?** 
- **has** – this is the helping verb. It tells us the time (present perfect) and that the action is completed. 
- **understood** – this is the main verb. It carries the core meaning “to grasp” or “to get”. 

Together, **has understood** forms the complete verb phrase. Without the helping verb, *She understood the instructions* would be a simple past statement, and without the main verb, *She has* would be incomplete.

**Why does this matter?** 
The helping verb adds essential information about when the action happened and whether it’s ongoing, finished, possible, etc. Both words are needed for a grammatically correct sentence.

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Imagine a basketball game. The **player** who dribbles, passes, or shoots is doing the **action**—that’s like a main verb such as *run* or *eat*. But the **coach** on the sidelines also speaks: “You **must** pass now,” “You **can** shoot,” or “You **will** defend.” The coach’s words don’t describe a physical move themselves, yet they are essential to the play; they tell the player **when**, **how**, or **if** the action should happen. In the sentence “She **is** running,” *running* is the player’s action, while *is* is the coach’s instruction that tells us the action is happening right now. Both parts together make the full verb phrase, just like the player and coach together shape the game.

So, a **verb** is any word that shows an action (like the player’s move) **or** a state/condition (like the coach saying “is ready”). When a helping verb joins a main verb, it works like the coach’s guidance, adding tense, ability, or necessity to the action. Together they form the complete verb phrase that makes a sentence meaningful.
