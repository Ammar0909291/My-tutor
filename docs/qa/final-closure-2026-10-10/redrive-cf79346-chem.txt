**Lesson: Rate Law and Order**  

In this lesson we’ll discover how the speed of a chemical reaction depends on how much of each reactant is present. By the end you’ll be able to write a rate law, identify the order with respect to each reactant, find the overall order, understand what the rate constant k means (including its units), and use the initial‑rate method to determine the exponents.  

Imagine a clear solution in a beaker that suddenly turns deep blue when two invisible chemicals mix – like the classic “iodine clock” experiment. You start a stopwatch the moment you add the second chemical and watch how long it takes for the blue color to appear. If you add more of the first chemical, the blue appears faster; if you add less, it takes longer.  

That everyday observation is a mental picture of **reaction rate**: how quickly products form (or a visible change occurs). In plain language, the **rate law** tells us exactly how the reaction rate changes when we change the concentrations of the reactants.  

**Rate law** – a mathematical expression that relates the rate of a reaction to the concentrations of the reactants, each raised to a power (the order).  

*Key terms* – reaction rate, concentration, order, overall order, rate constant k.  

When the law is known, we write it as  

\[
\text{rate} = k\,[A]^{m}[B]^{n}
\]

*rate* is the speed of the reaction, *k* is the rate constant, *[A]* and *[B]* are the concentrations of reactants A and B, and *m* and *n* are their respective orders.  

**Question:** Looking at this expression, what do you notice about how the concentration of each reactant influences the overall rate?

Let’s see how the “order” part of a rate law really comes from experiment, not from the coefficients in the balanced equation.

Take the simple‑looking reaction 

\[
\text{H}_2(g) + \text{Br}_2(g) \;\longrightarrow\; 2\,\text{HBr}(g)
\]

If we measure how fast the products appear while changing the concentrations of the gases, we find the rate follows 

\[
\text{rate}=k\,[\text{H}_2]^1\,[\text{Br}_2]^{1/2}
\]

Notice the exponent for bromine is **one‑half**, not the whole number “1” you would read from the balanced formula. This tells us the reaction proceeds through a chain mechanism in which a bromine radical is formed first; the overall rate depends on the square‑root of \([\text{Br}_2]\). So the orders **must be determined experimentally**.

Now, because the rate law always has the form 

\[
\text{rate}=k\,[\text{A}]^{m}[\text{B}]^{n}
\]

we can solve for the units of the rate constant \(k\) by rearranging:

\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{A}]^{m}[\text{B}]^{n}}
\]

The rate has units of **moles per litre per second** (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹). If the overall order \(m+n\) is 0 (zero‑order), \(k\) carries the same units as the rate: **mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹**. If the overall order is 1 (first‑order), \(k\) has units of **seconds⁻¹**. If the overall order is 2 (second‑order), \(k\) has units of **litre mol⁻¹ s⁻¹**. 

For the H₂ + Br₂ example the overall order is \(1 + 0.5 = 1.5\). Plugging into the formula gives 

\[
k\; \text{units}= \frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{1.5}}
= \text{L}^{0.5}\,\text{mol}^{-0.5}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

So the units change with the total order, but the constant \(k\) itself does **not** change when concentrations change; it only varies with temperature (and sometimes solvent).

A reaction’s **order** tells how the rate changes when a reactant’s concentration changes. A positive order means the rate rises with more concentration; a negative order means the rate falls. The overall order is the sum of the individual orders (here –1 + 1 = 0), and it determines the units of the rate constant k. For a zero‑order overall reaction, k has units of moles per litre per second (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹); for a first‑order overall reaction, k has units of seconds⁻¹ (s⁻¹); for a second‑order overall reaction, k has units of litre per mole per second (L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹), and so on. The figure you see outlines the **initial‑rate method** for finding these orders. First, you prepare several reaction mixtures with different concentrations of each reactant while keeping the others constant. Then you start each reaction and record the initial rate. By plotting **log(initial rate) against log(concentration)** of a single reactant, the slope of the straight line equals that reactant’s order. Repeating this for every reactant gives all individual orders; adding them yields the overall order, and finally you can calculate k from the rate law and state its appropriate units.

Let’s look at the pieces of a rate law one by one.

A **rate law** tells you how fast a reaction proceeds based on the concentrations of the reactants that are actually influencing the speed. It always has the form  

**rate = k [A]ᵐ [B]ⁿ**  

where:

* **[A]** and **[B]** are the concentrations of the two reactants (how many moles of each are in a litre of solution).  
* **m** and **n** are the **orders** with respect to A and B. They tell you how strongly each concentration changes the rate. If m = 1, doubling [A] doubles the rate; if m = 0, changing [A] does nothing; if m = –1, doubling [A] cuts the rate in half.  
* **k** is the **rate constant**. At a fixed temperature, k is just a number that makes the units work out; it does not depend on the concentrations.

The **overall order** of the reaction is the sum m + n. It tells you the total power to which concentration appears in the law and determines the units of k.

**What you need to do:**  
1. Measure the initial rate of the reaction in a few experiments.  
2. Keep one reactant’s concentration the same while changing the other’s.  
3. Compare how the rate changes; the ratio of the rates equals the ratio of the concentrations raised to the order (the initial‑rate method).

Now, have you seen a rate‑law expression like **rate = k [A]² [B]** before, and can you point out which part tells you how the concentration of A affects the rate?

Corrosion (like iron RUSTING) is essentially an unwanted, spontaneous ELECTROCHEMICAL process — a tiny galvanic cell forms directly ON the metal surface. For iron: at ANODIC regions, Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation, iron dissolves); electrons travel through the metal to CATHODIC regions where O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ (reduction, using dissolved atmospheric oxygen and water). The Fe²⁺ and OH⁻ then combine and further oxidize (by more O₂) to form Fe₂O₃·xH₂O (rust — a hydrated iron oxide, NOT metallic iron, and structurally porous/flaky, unlike protective oxide layers on some other metals). Corrosion needs BOTH water AND oxygen present simultaneously — neither alone is sufficient (dry iron doesn't rust; iron in oxygen-free water doesn't rust either). PREVENTION strategies exploit this electrochemistry directly: SACRIFICIAL PROTECTION (attaching a MORE reactive metal like zinc/magnesium — using the activity series covered earlier — which corrodes preferentially, "sacrificing" itself to protect the iron, since it becomes the anode instead), GALVANIZING (coating iron with a zinc layer, combining a physical barrier with sacrificial protection if the coating is scratched), and simple BARRIER COATINGS (paint, oil) that block water/oxygen access entirely.

When a steel nail is left outdoors, it soon develops a thin, reddish‑brown layer called rust. If you cover that nail with a thin sheet of zinc and later the coating gets a small scratch, the exposed zinc and the iron underneath form a tiny electrochemical cell. Because zinc’s standard reduction potential (‑0.76 V) is more negative than iron’s (‑0.44 V), zinc becomes the **anode** and dissolves first, while the iron acts as the **cathode** and stays protected. This is called a *sacrificial anode*—the coating sacrifices itself to keep the underlying metal safe, even through the scratch.

Now picture the same nail coated with tin instead of zinc. Tin’s potential (‑0.14 V) is less negative than iron’s, so when the tin coating is breached the iron becomes the anode and corrodes faster, while the tin acts as the cathode. In this case the coating works only as a physical barrier; once that barrier is broken, it actually speeds up the rusting of the iron underneath.

The key idea is that the metal with the more negative electrode potential in contact with iron will always be the anode and will corrode preferentially, providing true sacrificial protection. If the coating’s potential is less negative, it cannot act sacrificially and only blocks moisture and oxygen while intact.

Let’s look at a common real‑world case: **an iron nail driven into a wooden fence that is left outdoors in a coastal town**. 

The salty sea‑air supplies water that contains dissolved sodium chloride (NaCl). This water acts as the electrolyte, letting ions move between different spots on the nail’s surface. Where the nail is in a tiny crevice or under a thin film of rust, the oxygen concentration is low; that spot becomes the **anode**. Where the nail is fully exposed to air, oxygen is plentiful; that spot becomes the **cathode**.

At the anodic area the iron loses electrons:

\[
\text{Fe (s)} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2e^- \quad\text{(iron oxidation)}
\]

At the cathodic area oxygen takes up the electrons and combines with water to make hydroxide ions:

\[
\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4e^- \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^- \quad\text{(oxygen reduction)}
\]

The Fe²⁺ ions produced at the anode travel through the salty water to the cathode region, where they meet the OH⁻ ions and form iron(II) hydroxide, which quickly oxidises to the reddish‑brown rust we see.

**Why does the rust appear away from the actual metal loss?** Because the Fe²⁺ ions migrate through the electrolyte before they combine with OH⁻, so the visible rust forms where the two meet, not where the iron first dissolved.

But once it’s wet, tiny spots on the sponge that are less exposed to air (like the parts tucked under a plate) start to lose tiny pieces of the sponge material – they become the “anode.” The parts that are fully exposed to air gain electrons and become the “cathode.” The lost sponge pieces dissolve into the water, travel to the oxygen‑rich area, and combine with the oxygen there to form a brown, flaky coating – just like the rust you see on iron.

In this picture, the rust isn’t forming right where the metal is disappearing. The metal loss happens in the low‑oxygen, wet spot (the hidden part of the sponge). The rust appears where the dissolved iron meets oxygen (the exposed surface). The water provides a path for ions to move, while the metal itself provides a path for electrons to flow. This whole process is an electrochemical reaction, not just simple oxidation by air.

Because the corrosion needs both water (or any electrolyte) and oxygen, keeping metal dry or coating it so water can’t reach the surface stops the “sponge” from getting wet in the first place. Some coatings, like zinc, act like a sacrificial sponge: they dissolve first, protecting the iron underneath. Others, like tin, are just a barrier; if the barrier cracks, the iron underneath becomes the anode and corrodes faster.

Let’s look at the five standard curved‑arrow types by walking through a concrete reaction step for each and checking the formal charges before and after the arrow moves.

**1 → Lone‑pair → atom (nucleophilic attack)** 
In an SN2 reaction, the lone pair on ammonia (NH₃) attacks the carbon of methyl chloride (CH₃Cl). Before the step: N is neutral (5 valence – 2 lone‑pair e⁻ – ½·3 bonding e⁻ = 0); C and Cl are also neutral. The curved arrow starts at the N lone pair and ends at the C atom, forming a new N–C bond. After the step: N now has four bonds and no lone pair, giving it a formal charge of +1; the C–Cl bond breaks heterolytically, giving Cl a full electron pair and a formal charge of –1, while the carbon remains neutral.

**2 → π‑bond → atom (addition to a carbonyl)** 
When a hydride (H⁻) adds to formaldehyde (H₂C=O), the π bond of the C=O moves onto oxygen, and the hydride lone pair forms a new C–H bond. Before: carbonyl C and O are neutral. After the arrows: oxygen now carries the two electrons from the broken π bond, becoming O⁻ (formal charge –1); the carbon gains a C–H bond and stays neutral. The overall charge is conserved.

**3 → σ‑bond → atom (heterolytic bond cleavage, e.g., E2 elimination)** 
A base removes a β‑hydrogen from a substrate while the C–Br σ bond breaks. One arrow goes from the C–Br bond to Br, giving Br⁻; another arrow goes from the base’s lone pair to the H, forming a B–H bond. Before: C, Br, and the base are neutral. After: Br acquires the electron pair from the σ bond and carries a –1 charge; the carbon remains neutral, while the base becomes positively charged because it now has an extra bond.

**4 → Lone‑pair → forming a π‑bond (collapse of a tetrahedral intermediate)** 
In the hydrolysis of an ester, the alkoxide oxygen’s lone pair reforms the C=O double bond, pushing the electrons of the C–O⁻ bond onto the leaving alkoxide. Before: the alkoxide oxygen bears a –1 charge; the carbonyl oxygen is neutral. After: the carbonyl oxygen regains the π bond and is neutral again, while the departing alkoxide leaves with a full electron pair and retains the –1 charge. No net charge change occurs.

**5 → Bond → bond (1,2‑shift in a carbocation rearrangement)** 
Consider a hydride shift from a neighboring carbon to a carbocation. An arrow starts at the C–H σ bond and ends at the positively charged carbon, moving the hydride pair. Before: the carbocation carries a +1 charge; the donor carbon is neutral. After the shift, the original carbocation site is now neutral, and the donor carbon becomes the new carbocation, still bearing a +1 charge. The total charge stays the same.

These examples show how each arrow type moves exactly one electron pair, changes formal charges in a predictable way, and conserves the overall charge of the system.

Ionic bonding happens when the electronegativity difference between two atoms is large (>1.7 on the Pauling scale, roughly) — one atom essentially takes electron(s) from the other. Metal atoms (low ionization energy — easy to lose electrons) transfer electrons to nonmetal atoms (high electron affinity — eager to gain electrons). **Lesson: Batteries and Fuel Cells**  

In this lesson we’ll explore how chemical energy is turned into electricity. By the end you will be able to:

* Distinguish **primary** (single‑use) and **secondary** (re‑chargeable) batteries.  
* Explain the basic idea of a **hydrogen fuel cell**.  
* Discuss the **efficiency** and **environmental impact** of these devices.  

---

Let’s start with the first topic: **primary and secondary batteries**.

Imagine a small **flashlight** you keep in a drawer for emergencies. Inside the flashlight is a little cylinder – a **battery** – that powers the bulb when you switch it on.  

Now picture a **re‑chargeable AA battery** that you might use in a wireless game controller. You can plug it into a charger, use it many times, and the controller works just the same each time.  

Both of these devices store chemical energy and release it as electric current, but they behave differently after the energy is used up.  

In plain words, a **primary battery** is a one‑time‑use cell: once its chemicals are exhausted, you throw it away. A **secondary battery** can be **re‑charged**; the chemical reactions are reversed by applying an external electric current, so the battery can deliver power again.  

*Key vocabulary*:  
* **Electrochemical cell** – the basic unit that converts chemical energy to electricity.  
* **Anode** – the electrode where oxidation occurs (loss of electrons).  
* **Cathode** – the electrode where reduction occurs (gain of electrons).
