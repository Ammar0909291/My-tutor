Imagine we mix hydrogen gas (H₂) with bromine gas (Br₂) in a sealed flask and watch the reaction that makes hydrogen bromide (2 HBr). If we measure how fast the product appears while changing the concentrations, the data fit the law 

\[
\text{rate}=k[{\rm H_2}]^{1}[{\rm Br_2}]^{0.5}
\]

Here the exponent for H₂ is **1** and for Br₂ is **0.5**. Notice that the balanced equation, H₂ + Br₂ → 2 HBr, shows one molecule of each reactant, yet the order for Br₂ is **not** the stoichiometric coefficient (which would be 1). This tells us that the orders **must be found experimentally**, not read from the equation.

Now let’s see what the units of the rate constant k look like for this reaction. The overall order is the sum of the individual orders:

\[
\text{overall order}=1+0.5=1.5
\]

The rate has units of mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ (change in concentration per second). Rearranging the rate law,

\[
k=\frac{\text{rate}}{[{\rm H_2}]^{1}[{\rm Br_2}]^{0.5}}
\]

so the units of k are

\[
\frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{1}(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{0.5}}
= \text{L}^{0.5}\,\text{mol}^{-0.5}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

In words: **liter to the one‑half power, per mole to the one‑half power, per second**. The unit changes with the overall order, confirming that k itself does not depend on concentration—only on temperature and the reaction’s intrinsic details.

Let’s look at a real reaction that many textbooks use as a model:

**Reaction:** 2 A + B → products

Experiments have shown that its rate law is

\[
\text{rate}=k[\text{A}]^{2}[\text{B}]
\]

(rate equals k times the concentration of A squared times the concentration of B).  
Here the order with respect to A is 2, the order with respect to B is 1, so the overall order is 2 + 1 = 3.

---

Suppose the rate constant at the temperature we’re working at is

\[
k = 0.50\;\text{L}^{2}\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

(the units L² mol⁻² s⁻¹ come from “rate (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹) divided by [A]²[B] (mol L⁻¹)³”).

Now take the concentrations

\[
[\text{A}] = 0.20\;\text{mol L}^{-1}, \qquad [\text{B}] = 0.10\;\text{mol L}^{-1}.
\]

Plug them into the rate law:

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{rate} &= k[\text{A}]^{2}[\text{B}] \\
 &= 0.50\;\text{L}^{2}\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}\;
 (0.20\;\text{mol L}^{-1})^{2}\;
 (0.10\;\text{mol L}^{-1}) \\
 &= 0.50 \times 0.040 \times 0.10\;\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1} \\
 &= 0.0020\;\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\]

So the reaction proceeds at a rate of **2.0 × 10⁻³ moles per litre per second** under those conditions.

Imagine a busy intersection with two incoming streets, A and B. Cars line up in each lane, and a traffic light lets a few cars through every minute. The speed at which cars leave the intersection (the **rate**) depends on how many cars are waiting in each lane (their **concentrations**) and on the timing of the light (a fixed **rate constant**). If lane A gets more cars while lane B stays the same, more cars can pass, but the exact increase isn’t just “one‑for‑one”; it follows a specific power‑law relationship that we call the **rate law**. The exponent on \([A]\) tells us how strongly the traffic from lane A controls the overall flow—this is the **order with respect to A**. The same idea applies to lane B, and adding the two orders gives the **overall order** of the whole intersection.

In plain words, the rate law tells us *how* the flow of cars changes when we change the number of cars waiting in each lane, and the rate constant captures everything else that stays the same (like the light’s cycle time). The orders are determined by watching the traffic and measuring how the flow responds, not by looking at the road map of the intersection.

**Key term introduced:** *order* – the exponent that shows how a reactant’s (or lane’s) concentration influences the reaction rate (or traffic flow).

**Lesson: Electron Flow and Arrow Notation**  

In this lesson we’ll learn how chemists show the movement of electrons with curved arrows. By the end you’ll be able to read and draw these arrows to show bond breaking, bond making, and to follow the steps of SN2, E2, and addition reactions.

Imagine a **ball rolling down a small hill** and then jumping onto a neighboring hill. The ball’s path shows where something (the ball) moves from one place to another, just like an electron moves from one atom to another in a molecule.

In many organic reactions we see a **curved arrow** drawn on the paper. The arrow starts where the electron pair is (the “ball” at the top of the hill) and points toward where the electrons will end up (the next hill). The arrow’s tail tells us the source of the electrons, and the head tells us the destination.

A curved arrow is a visual shorthand that says, “A pair of electrons leaves this bond or lone pair and goes to this other atom or bond.”

Curved‑arrow notation (or simply arrow notation).

- **Lone pair** – two non‑bonding electrons on an atom.  
- **Bond‑forming arrow** – arrow pointing to a new bond site.  
- **Bond‑breaking arrow** – arrow pointing away from a bond that is being broken.

Now, look at a simple drawing of a carbon‑chlorine bond with a curved arrow starting at the C–Cl bond and ending on the chlorine atom.

Let’s look at the five ways a curved arrow can be used, and see how the formal‑charge bookkeeping works for each one. I’ll follow the flow shown in the diagram on your screen: the first two boxes (“Identify nucleophile and electrophile” → “Draw nucleophilic attack arrow”) illustrate the first two arrow types, and the third box (“Show bond‑breaking arrow”) lets us add the remaining three types.

**1. Lone‑pair → atom (forming a new σ‑bond)** 
*Example:* an SN2 attack of hydroxide (HO⁻) on methyl chloride (CH₃Cl). - **Before:** OH⁻ has a lone pair on O (–1 charge); the carbon of CH₃Cl is neutral. - **Arrow:** from the O lone pair to the carbon. - **After:** O now shares the electron pair with C, so O becomes neutral and the C–Cl bond breaks (Cl⁻ leaves). Formal charges: O 0, C 0, Cl –1 – the total charge (–1) is unchanged.

**2. π‑bond → atom (breaking a double bond and forming a new σ‑bond)** 
*Example:* protonation of an alkene, CH₂=CH₂ + H⁺ → CH₃–CH₂⁺. - **Before:** the C=C π‑bond holds two electrons; both carbons are neutral. - **Arrow:** from the π‑bond to the proton, creating a C–H σ‑bond. - **After:** one carbon now bears a positive formal charge (it lost the two π‑electrons), the other carbon stays neutral. The overall charge (+1) matches the incoming proton.

**3. σ‑bond → atom (heterolytic bond cleavage)** 
*Example:* the leaving‑group step in the same SN2 reaction above. - **Before:** the C–Cl σ‑bond contains two electrons, both assigned to Cl⁻ after cleavage. - **Arrow:** from the C–Cl bond to the chlorine atom. - **After:** Cl gains the two electrons, becoming Cl⁻ (–1 charge); the carbon loses the bond but already formed the new bond to O, so its charge stays 0. Charge is conserved.

**4. Lone‑pair → forming a π‑bond** 
*Example:* the collapse of a tetrahedral alkoxide intermediate in ester hydrolysis, where an O⁻ lone pair reforms a C=O double bond. - **Before:** the carbonyl carbon is double‑bonded to O (neutral), the alkoxide O⁻ has a lone pair and a –1 charge. - **Arrow:** from the O⁻ lone pair to the carbonyl carbon, creating a new π‑bond. - **After:** the carbonyl regains its C=O double bond, the alkoxide O loses the lone pair and becomes neutral, and the carbonyl carbon remains neutral. The –1 charge moves from O⁻ to the leaving group that departs, keeping the total charge unchanged.

**5. Bond → bond (σ‑bond shift, e.g., a 1,2‑hydride shift)** 
*Example:* a carbocation rearrangement where a C–H σ‑bond moves to an adjacent positively‑charged carbon. - **Before:** a carbocation on C₂ (formal charge +1) and a C₁–H σ‑bond. - **Arrow:** from the C₁–H bond to C₂, delivering the two electrons to the empty orbital on C₂. - **After:** C₂ now has a new C–H bond and loses its positive charge; C₁ becomes a carbocation (+1). The positive charge simply migrates, and the total charge (+1) is conserved.

In every case the curved arrow shows **exactly two electrons moving** from a clearly defined source (the tail) to a destination (the head), and a quick formal‑charge check confirms that the overall charge of the system does not change.

- **Show bond‑breaking arrow** – this arrow begins at the bond that is breaking (often a leaving group) and points to the atom that will keep the electron pair after the bond splits. 
- **Complete product structures** – after the arrows are placed, you finish the drawing by showing the new bonds that have formed and the fragments that have left.

The arrows that connect the four boxes simply indicate the order in which you perform these steps when writing a reaction mechanism.

**Why does the nucleophilic‑attack arrow start at the nucleophile’s electron pair rather than at the electrophile?**
[learner] explain simpler
[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=exampleQualified]
Think of the curved arrow like a courier delivering a package of two items (the two electrons). The package sits in a specific spot – either a lone‑pair “shelf” on an atom or the “bond” that already holds two electrons. The courier’s **tail** is the location where the package is picked up (the shelf or the bond), and the **head** points to the destination where the package is dropped off (the atom or the new bond being formed). The courier never starts from the house itself (the atomic nucleus); it always starts from the place where the electrons actually reside. So in the picture, the arrow that begins at a lone‑pair on the nucleophile is the courier picking up the electron pair from that lone‑pair shelf and delivering it to the electrophile’s empty spot, forming a new bond there.

In the same way, when you see an arrow that begins at a σ‑bond, imagine the courier taking the whole pair of electrons from that bond (the bond is the “warehouse”) and delivering them to the atom that will keep them after the leaving group departs. The key rule is: **the tail is always at the electron source, never at the atom’s nucleus** – just like a courier never starts a delivery from the house that will receive the package.
