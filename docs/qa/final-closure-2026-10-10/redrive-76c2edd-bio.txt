**Lesson: Innate Behaviour and Instinct**  

In this lesson we’ll explore behaviours that animals are born with – they don’t have to learn them. By the end you will be able to:

* Describe what a **fixed action pattern** is and why it is stereotyped.  
* Identify the **sign stimulus** that triggers such a pattern.  
* Distinguish simple reflexes from more complex instinctive actions.  
* Explain how genetics and natural selection shape innate behaviours.  
Let’s start with the first idea: **fixed action patterns**.

Imagine a tiny sea‑turtle hatchling on a dark beach at night. Its tiny body is wet, the sand is cool, and the ocean is only a short distance away. The hatchling must reach the water quickly to avoid predators and drying out. As soon as it sees the bright glow of the moonlit horizon, it begins to crawl straight toward it, following the same smooth, unchanging path every time a hatchling is born.

* **Concrete everyday picture:** a newborn turtle on a beach.  
* The turtle needs to get to the sea to survive.  
* The hatchling moves in a straight line toward the brightest light, without stopping or hesitating.  
* This is a built‑in, automatic sequence of movements that the turtle performs the moment a particular cue (the light) appears. The sequence is the same for every hatchling and does not change with experience.  

**Fixed Action Pattern (FAP)** – a stereotyped, largely unmodifiable behavioural sequence that is released by a specific cue.

* *stereotyped* means the behaviour looks the same each time; *innate* means the animal is born with it, not learned.

Fixed action patterns (FAPs) are stereotyped (performed the same way across individuals of a species) and largely unmodifiable behavioural sequences — once triggered, a FAP typically runs to completion in its characteristic form, largely independent of ongoing feedback. Critically, a FAP is NOT spontaneous: it requires a specific sign stimulus (a specific, often simple environmental cue) to release it — the sequence is performed only when that specific trigger is present. Innate behaviour must be further distinguished from simple reflexes: a reflex is a direct, typically single-muscle-group stimulus-response (like the knee-jerk reflex) — simple, immediate, minimal neural processing. A fixed action pattern is substantially more complex, coordinated, and multi-step (like an elaborate courtship display or nest-building sequence) — many more coordinated movements over an extended time, though still triggered by a specific sign stimulus and still largely stereotyped once initiated. Innate behaviour also has a genetic basis: it is a heritable trait shaped by natural selection over evolutionary time, exactly as physical structures are — a FAP that reliably improves survival or reproduction can be favoured by selection and become a stable, species-typical trait.

**Photosynthesis** – the process plants use to turn sunlight into food and oxygen. By the end of this lesson you’ll be able to describe the light and dark reactions, name the two photosystems, explain how electrons move and make ATP, and outline the Calvin cycle as well as the C₄ and CAM adaptations.  

Think of a small solar‑powered calculator you might have seen on a desk. It has a tiny panel that catches sunlight and turns it into electric energy so the calculator can work without batteries. In a green leaf, millions of tiny “solar panels” called chloroplasts do a very similar job: they catch sunlight and use it to power a series of chemical steps that build sugars.  

Picture each chloroplast as a miniature factory where light is the raw material, and the factory’s workers (proteins and pigments) pass tiny packets of energy along a conveyor belt, finally producing a sugar product that the plant can use for growth.  

In plain language, **photosynthesis** is the set of reactions that capture light energy and convert carbon dioxide and water into glucose and oxygen. The first set of steps, called the **light reactions**, happen in the thylakoid membranes and turn light into chemical energy (ATP and NADPH).

Photosynthesis converts light energy into chemical energy (glucose), using CO2 and water as raw materials. Overall equation: 6CO2 + 6H2O + light energy → C6H12O6 + 6O2. Two stages occur in the chloroplast. Light-dependent reactions (thylakoid membranes): light energy is absorbed by chlorophyll; water is split (photolysis), releasing O2 as a by-product; the energy is used to produce ATP and NADPH (energy carriers). Light-independent reactions / Calvin cycle (stroma): ATP and NADPH from stage 1 drive the fixation of CO2 into glucose via a cycle of reactions. CO2 combines with RuBP (a 5-carbon compound) via RuBisCO enzyme; the products are reduced using ATP and NADPH to produce G3P, which is used to make glucose. RuBP is regenerated to continue the cycle. Key point: O2 is released from water splitting, not from CO2.

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Here’s a concrete walk‑through of one full photosynthetic event in a leaf:

1. **Light‑dependent reactions (left side of the figure)** – Sunlight excites chlorophyll in photosystem II, which uses that energy to split two water molecules:

2 H₂O → O₂ + 4 H⁺ + 4 e⁻

The released electrons travel through the electron‑transport chain, powering the synthesis of **ATP** and the reduction of **NADP⁺** to **NADPH**. Roughly, each pair of water molecules yields 2 NADPH and about 3 ATP (the exact ATP yield varies, but is roughly 3 per O₂ evolved).

2. **Calvin cycle (right side of the figure)** – The ATP and NADPH produced above are consumed in the stroma to fix carbon dioxide. Three CO₂ molecules combine with three molecules of the carrier **RuBP** (ribulose‑1,5‑bisphosphate). After a series of reductions, the cycle generates one molecule of G3P (glyceraldehyde‑3‑phosphate), which can be linked together to form one glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆).

Overall net reaction for six CO₂ and six H₂O:

6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + light → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂

This shows how the light reactions (producing ATP/NADPH) fuel the dark reactions (the Calvin cycle) to build sugar.
