**Rate law** is the mathematical expression that links the concentrations of reactants to the speed of the reaction. It tells us how changing a concentration will change the rate.

Let’s look at the **rate law** we just introduced:

\[
\text{rate} = k\,[\text{A}]^{m}\,[\text{B}]^{n}
\]

The **overall order** of the reaction is the sum of the individual orders:

\[
\text{overall order}=m+n
\]

---

### How to find the units of the rate constant **k**

1. **Write the units of the rate** – the rate of a reaction is a change in concentration per unit time, so its units are  

   **moles · litre⁻¹ · second⁻¹** (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹).

2. **Write the units of the concentration term** – each concentration \([\text{X}]\) has units **moles · litre⁻¹** (mol L⁻¹). When the concentration term is raised to the overall order, the units are \((\text{mol L}^{-1})^{\text{overall order}}\).

3. **Solve for the units of k** by rearranging the rate law:  

   \[
   k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{A}]^{m}[\text{B}]^{n}}
   \]

   Hence  

   \[
   \text{units of }k = \frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{\,m+n}}
   \]

   The denominator’s exponent is the overall order.

---

### Examples for different overall orders  

Overall order · Units of \(k\)
0 (zero‑order) · mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹
1 (first‑order) · s⁻¹
2 (second‑order) · L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹
\(n\) (nth‑order) · L^{\(n-1\)} mol^{\(1-n\)} s⁻¹

#### Worked example – first‑order reaction  

Consider a simple decomposition \( \text{A} \rightarrow \text{products} \) that is first order (overall order = 1). Measured rate: **0.020 mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹**  
Concentration of A: **0.10 mol L⁻¹**

\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{A}]} = \frac{0.020}{0.10} = 0.20\;\text{s}^{-1}
\]

The unit **s⁻¹** matches the table for a first‑order reaction.

#### Worked example – second‑order reaction  

Now take a bimolecular reaction \(2\text{A} \rightarrow \text{products}\) that is second order (overall order = 2). Measured rate: **0.005 mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹**  
Concentration of A: **0.05 mol L⁻¹**

\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{A}]^{2}} = \frac{0.005}{(0.05)^{2}} = 2.0\;\text{L mol}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

The unit **L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹** matches the table for a second‑order reaction.

These calculations show how the **order of the reaction determines the units of the rate constant**.

\( \text{H}_2(g) + \text{Br}_2(g) \rightarrow 2\,\text{HBr}(g) \)

Experiments are done at a fixed temperature, and we measure the initial rate of disappearance of H₂.

Experiment · \([\text{H}_2]\) (mol L⁻¹) · \([\text{Br}_2]\) (mol L⁻¹) · Initial rate (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹)
1 · 0.10 · 0.04 · \(2.0 \times 10^{-5}\)
2 · 0.20 (double) · 0.04 (same) · \(4.0 \times 10^{-5}\)
3 · 0.10 (same) · 0.16 (four‑fold) · \(4.0 \times 10^{-5}\)

**Step 1 – Order with respect to H₂** 
When \([\text{H}_2]\) is doubled (Experiment 2 vs 1) while \([\text{Br}_2]\) stays constant, the rate also doubles. 
\( \displaystyle \frac{\text{rate}_2}{\text{rate}_1}= \frac{4.0\times10^{-5}}{2.0\times10^{-5}} = 2 = \left(\frac{0.20}{0.10}\right)^{m}\) 
Thus \(m = 1\). The reaction is **first order in H₂**.

**Step 2 – Order with respect to Br₂** 
When \([\text{Br}_2]\) is increased from 0.04 M to 0.16 M (a factor of 4) while \([\text{H}_2]\) stays at 0.10 M, the rate again doubles. 
\( \displaystyle \frac{\text{rate}_3}{\text{rate}_1}= \frac{4.0\times10^{-5}}{2.0\times10^{-5}} = 2 = \left(\frac{0.16}{0.04}\right)^{n}\) 
Since \(4^{\,n}=2\), solving gives \(n = \tfrac{1}{2}\). The reaction is **half‑order in Br₂**.

**Step 3 – Write the rate law** 
Putting the orders together: 
\[
\text{rate} = k[\text{H}_2]^{1}[\text{Br}_2]^{1/2}
\] 
(“rate equals k times the concentration of hydrogen to the first power times the concentration of bromine to the half power.”)

**Step 4 – Overall order and units of \(k\)** 
Overall order = \(1 + 0.5 = 1.5\). 
Rate has units mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹, concentration has units mol L⁻¹. 
\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{H}_2]^{1}[\text{Br}_2]^{1/2}}
\]
so the units of \(k\) are 
\[
\frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{1+0.5}} = \text{mol}^{-0.5}\,\text{L}^{0.5}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]
or “M⁻¹ᐟ² s⁻¹”.

**Lesson: Electron Flow and Arrow Notation**

In this lesson we’ll see how chemists draw the movement of electrons with curved arrows, just like you might picture water flowing from a faucet. By the end you’ll be able to read and write these arrows to show which bonds break, which new bonds form, and how reactions such as SN2, E₂, and addition proceed.

Imagine a kitchen faucet: when you turn the handle, water streams out in a clear direction. In a chemical reaction, the “water” is the electrons, and the arrows we draw tell us where those electrons travel as bonds are broken and made.  

Think of the curved arrow as a tiny “road sign” that points from the place where an electron pair starts (the source) to the place where it ends up (the destination).  

This visual cue is called **electron‑flow arrow notation**. The most common symbol is a curved arrow with a dot (or two dots) at the tail, showing a lone‑pair or bond‑pair of electrons, and the head pointing to the atom that receives them.

Let’s look at a classic SN2 reaction: hydroxide ion ( OH⁻ ) attacks methyl bromide ( CH₃Br ) to give methanol ( CH₃OH ) and bromide ion ( Br⁻ ).

1. **Identify nucleophile and electrophile** – In the figure’s first box, the nucleophile is the OH⁻ (lone‑pair donor) and the electrophile is the carbon attached to bromine in CH₃Br. 
2. **Draw nucleophilic attack arrow** – In the second box we draw a curved arrow that starts at the lone pair on OH⁻ (the electron source) and points to the carbon atom (the electron destination). This arrow shows the formation of a new C–O bond. 
3. **Show bond‑breaking arrow** – In the third box we draw a second curved arrow that starts at the C–Br σ‑bond and points to the bromine atom. Both electrons of that bond move onto bromine, so bromine leaves as Br⁻. 
4. **Complete product structures** – The fourth box now contains methanol, where carbon is bonded to O (the new bond) and to three hydrogens, and the separate Br⁻ anion.

**Formal‑charge check** 

Species · Before · After
OH⁻  (nucleophile) · –1 (lone pair) · 0 (now part of C–O bond)
CH₃Br  (electrophile) · 0 · 0 (carbon now neutral, bromine leaves)
Br⁻  (leaving group) · 0 · –1 (carries both electrons)
CH₃OH  (product) · – · 0 (neutral molecule)

The total charge stays –1 throughout, confirming the arrows obey the formal‑charge conservation rule.

**Key term:** *leaving group* – the atom or group that departs with the electron pair when a σ‑bond is broken (here, bromine).
