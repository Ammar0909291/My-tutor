**Lesson: Rate Law and Order**  

In this lesson we’ll explore how chemists describe the speed of a reaction with a *rate law*. By the end you’ll be able to write a rate law, identify the order with respect to each reactant, find the overall order, understand the rate constant \(k\) and its units, and use the initial‑rate method to determine these quantities.  

Imagine you’re baking a cake and you notice the batter thickens faster when you add more sugar. The sugar molecules are like “reactants” that collide more often, making the “reaction” (thickening) happen quicker. Picture the kitchen counter as a busy crossroads where many cars (molecules) meet; the more cars of a certain type, the more collisions you get, and the faster traffic moves through the intersection.  

In plain language, the **rate law** tells us how the speed of a chemical reaction depends on the concentrations of the reactants. It is written as  

\[
\text{rate} = k\,[A]^m\,[B]^n
\]

where \(k\) is the **rate constant**, \([A]\) and \([B]\) are the concentrations of reactants A and B, and \(m\) and \(n\) are the **orders** with respect to each reactant.  

🔍 **Question:** Looking at the expression \(\text{rate} = k\,[A]^m\,[B]^n\), what do you notice about the role of the concentrations \([A]\) and \([B]\) in determining the reaction rate?

Imagine you are watching a kitchen where two ingredients, **A** (like flour) and **B** (like sugar), are mixed to make a cake batter. The speed at which the batter thickens depends on how much flour and sugar you add. Chemists write that dependence as a **rate law**:

**rate = k [A]² [B]** 

*Plain‑language*: the reaction gets faster when you have more flour, and it speeds up twice as much for each doubling of flour (the “²” exponent). Sugar also helps, but only linearly (the single **[B]**).

**Step‑by‑step numbers**

1. **Experiment 1** – you start with 0.10 mol L⁻¹ of A and 0.10 mol L⁻¹ of B. You measure the initial rate as 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. 
2. **Experiment 2** – you keep B the same (0.10 mol L⁻¹) but double A to 0.20 mol L⁻¹. The measured initial rate becomes 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹.

Because B didn’t change, the ratio of the two rates tells us the order with respect to A:

\[
\frac{\text{rate}_2}{\text{rate}_1}= \left(\frac{[A]_2}{[A]_1}\right)^{m}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\frac{8.0\times10^{-4}}{2.0\times10^{-4}} = \left(\frac{0.20}{0.10}\right)^{m}
\]

\[
4 = 2^{\,m} \;\Longrightarrow\; m = 2
\]

So the reaction is **second‑order in A**. Since the exponent of B is 1, the reaction is **first‑order in B**. The **overall order** is \(m+n = 2+1 = 3\).

Now solve for the rate constant **k** using the data from experiment 1:

\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[A]^2[B]}
 = \frac{2.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}}
 {(0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1})^{2}\,(0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1})}
 = \frac{2.0\times10^{-4}}{1.0\times10^{-3}}
 = 0.20\ \text{L}^{2}\text{mol}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}
\]

Because the overall order is three, the units of **k** are \(\text{L}^{2}\text{mol}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}\) (liter‑squared per mole‑squared per second).

**Quick check** – If we keep the concentration of A at 0.10 mol L⁻¹ and double the concentration of B from 0.10 mol L⁻¹ to 0.20 mol L⁻¹, how will the reaction rate change?

**Electron Flow and Arrow Notation** – In this lesson we’ll see how chemists draw the tiny movements of electrons with curved arrows. By the end you’ll be able to read and write those arrows to show bonds breaking, bonds forming, and the steps of SN2, E2, and addition reactions.

Think of a garden hose that you bend to point water toward a plant. The water (like electrons) can travel from the hose’s end (where it starts) to the plant (where it ends). In a chemical reaction, the “hose” is a lone‑pair or a bond, and the “plant” is the atom that receives the electrons.

Picture a curved arrow that begins at a dot (a lone pair) or at the middle of a line (a bond) and sweeps toward another atom or bond. The arrow tells us the direction the electrons move during the step.

When we draw a curved arrow, we are simply showing the path of electron flow: **start at the source of electrons (a lone pair or a bond) and point to the place where those electrons will end up (a new bond or a new lone pair).** This convention lets us follow the step‑by‑step changes in a reaction mechanism.

What do you notice about where the arrow starts and where it points in this description?

**Concrete situation** 
The nucleophile is the hydroxide ion OH⁻, which carries a lone pair on oxygen. The substrate is methyl chloride CH₃Cl, where the carbon‑chlorine σ‑bond is ready to leave.

**Step‑by‑step arrow work**

**Formal‑charge check** 

Species · Before · After
OH⁻ (oxygen) · –1 (lone pair) · 0 (now part of C–O bond)
C in CH₃Cl · 0 · 0 (still neutral, now bonded to O)
Cl⁻ (chlorine) · 0 · –1 (now carries the two electrons)

The total charge stays –1 throughout, confirming that the arrow sequence conserves charge.

**What this shows** 
- The **tail** of each curved arrow is anchored at the exact electron source (a lone pair or a bond). - The **head** points to where that electron pair ends up (a new bond or a lone pair on another atom). - Every arrow moves **exactly two electrons**, and the formal‑charge bookkeeping validates the step.

The two electrons of the lone pair move to form a new O‑H bond, while the original O‑H bond in water breaks and its two electrons go to the oxygen of the water molecule, making it a hydroxide ion.  

- **Key point:** The arrow never starts on the hydrogen atom itself; it starts on the electron pair that belongs to the hydroxide oxygen. The electrons are the “actors,” not the nuclei.  

This picture you’re looking at follows exactly that rule: each arrow’s tail is on a specific electron source, and the head shows where those electrons are transferred. Once you keep that idea in mind, reading any arrow‑pushing diagram becomes much clearer.
