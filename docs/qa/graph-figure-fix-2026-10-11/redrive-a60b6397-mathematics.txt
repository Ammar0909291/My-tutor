**Square Roots** – In this lesson we’ll discover what a square root is and how it tells us the length of a side when we know the area of a square. By the end you’ll be able to find the square‑root of a number and understand the idea of n‑th roots for whole‑number n.  

Imagine a garden that is shaped like a perfect square. If the garden covers 16 square meters, you might wonder how long each side must be to give that area. Picture a square drawn on paper: the whole shape is the “area” (the number 16), and the length of one side is the number that, when multiplied by itself, recreates that area. In plain words, the square root of a number is the side length of a square whose area equals that number.  

We call this side length the **square root**, written as √x, where x is called the *radicand* (the number under the root sign). The square root is always the non‑negative number y that satisfies y² = x.  

If you have a square whose area is 9 square meters, what do you notice about the relationship between its side length and its area?

Now notice the difference between the square‑root symbol and solving a quadratic equation. If we write √36, we get a single answer, 6. But if a problem asks “solve y² = 36,” we must list both numbers whose square is 36, so the solutions are y = 6 and y = –6. The √ sign is a function that returns only the non‑negative root, while “solve y² = x” asks for every number that satisfies the equation. Let’s look at how roots behave with odd versus even indices. An even‑index root, like a square root, cannot be taken of a negative number in the real numbers—√(–9) is undefined. In contrast, an odd‑index root, such as the cube root, works for negatives because an odd power preserves the sign: the cube root of –8 is –2, since (–2)³ = –8. This shows why the rule about non‑negative radicands applies only to even‑indexed roots. Whenever you’re ready, just let me know and we can try another example together.

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Imagine a square whose **area** is 20 square units. 
The side length of that square is the number y that satisfies y² = 20, so the side length is √20.

We know the perfect squares 4² = 16 and 5² = 25. 
Since 16 < 20 < 25, the square‑root function (the curve you see on the graph) tells us that the side length must lie between the side lengths of those two perfect‑square squares. 
Therefore 4 < √20 < 5 — the side length is a non‑integer number a little bigger than 4 but smaller than 5.

You’re looking at the curve that plots \(y=\sqrt{x}\). Notice how the line starts at the origin (0, 0) and then rises slowly: for each \(x\)‑value on the horizontal axis, the height of the curve tells you the side length of a square whose area is \(x\). For example, when \(x=9\) the point on the curve is at \(y=3\) because a square of area 9 has sides 3 units long. The graph therefore shows the **inverse** of squaring: squaring takes a side length \(y\) and gives an area \(y^{2}\); the square‑root curve takes an area \(x\) and returns the side length \(y\) that satisfies \(y^{2}=x\). The symbol \(\sqrt{x}\) always picks the non‑negative side length, so the curve stays in the first quadrant.

In this picture you can also see why \(\sqrt{60}\) is about 7.7: the point with \(x=60\) lies between the points \(x=49\) (\(y=7\)) and \(x=64\) (\(y=8\)), so the height of the curve there must be a little more than 7 and a little less than 8 — roughly 7.7. That’s the idea behind estimating square roots using the graph.
