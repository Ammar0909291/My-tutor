**Lesson: Stress, Strain, and Elastic Moduli**  
In this lesson we’ll see how pulling on everyday objects—like a rubber band—helps us describe the internal forces inside a material. By the end you’ll be able to tell what **stress** and **strain** are, and how their ratio gives the **elastic modulus** of a material.

Imagine a thin rubber band stretched between your fingers. As you pull harder, the band becomes tighter and its length increases. Picture the rubber band as a tiny collection of many little “springs” that each feel the pull.

**Stress** is simply the force you apply divided by the area over which that force acts. In other words, it tells us how much “push” or “pull” each tiny part of the material experiences.

*Question:* When you stretch a rubber band, what do you notice about how the force you feel changes as the band gets longer?

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Let’s look at a simple, real‑world case.

Imagine a wooden rod that is **2 metres long** and has a **cross‑sectional area of 0.005 square metres**. 
You pull on it with a force of **2000 newtons** and it stretches a little, **0.001 metre**.

1. **Stress** – the force spread over the area: 
 \[
 \sigma = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{2000\ \text{N}}{0.005\ \text{m}^2}= 400{,}000\ \text{Pa}
 \] 
 (four hundred thousand pascals).

2. **Strain** – the fractional change in length: 
 \[
 \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L} = \frac{0.001\ \text{m}}{2\ \text{m}} = 5\times10^{-4}
 \] 
 (a very small, dimension‑less number).

3. **Young’s modulus** – the material’s intrinsic “stiffness”: 
 \[
 E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}= \frac{400{,}000\ \text{Pa}}{5\times10^{-4}} = 8.0\times10^{8}\ \text{Pa}
 \] 
 which is about **0.8 gigapascals**, lower than the typical value for wood (usually around 10 GPa).

So the same force produces a much larger stress if the area were smaller, and the ratio of stress to strain (the Young’s modulus) stays the same for the same material, no matter the size of the piece.

**Question:** If we doubled the cross‑sectional area of the rod while keeping the 2000 N force the same, how would the stress change – would it increase, decrease, or stay the same?

A material fails when its stress exceeds its ultimate tensile strength, a property measured in pascals, never in newtons alone.
