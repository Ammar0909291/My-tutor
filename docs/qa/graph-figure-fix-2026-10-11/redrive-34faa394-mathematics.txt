**Square Roots** – In this lesson we’ll explore what a square root is and how it lets us find the original number when we know its square. By the end, you’ll be able to identify a square root, write it using the √ symbol, and work with simple examples.

Imagine a square piece of chocolate that’s 4 cm on each side. If you count how many little squares fit inside, you get 4 cm × 4 cm = 16 cm² of chocolate. The number 16 tells us the total area, while 4 tells us the length of each side.

In everyday life, you might know the area of a garden (say 25 m²) and need to figure out how long each side of the square garden is. You’re looking for the number that, when multiplied by itself, gives the area you have.

Think of the side length as the “hidden” number that creates the area when it’s squared. The square root is the name for that hidden number.

**Square root** (written √x) of a non‑negative number x is the non‑negative number y such that y² = x.

Now, if a square has an area of 25 square units, what length must each side be?

On the graph you’re seeing, the curve passes through the point where the horizontal coordinate is 36 and the vertical coordinate is 6. That tells us the square‑root function gives √36 = 6, because the “output” y on the curve is the side‑length whose square equals the “input” x. 

Now ask yourself what numbers square to 36. Both 6 and ‑6 do, because 6 × 6 = 36 and (‑6) × (‑6) = 36. When a problem says “solve y² = 36,” the answer set is y = ±6 (two numbers). When it says “what is √36?” the symbol √ by definition picks only the non‑negative one, so the answer is just 6. 

For an even‑indexed root like the square root, a negative radicand has no real result: √(‑9) is undefined in the real numbers. But for an odd‑indexed root, the sign is preserved: the cube‑root of ‑8 is ‑2, because (‑2)³ = ‑8. This shows the parity rule—even roots need non‑negative inputs and give the non‑negative result; odd roots accept any real input and give a single real result.

Taking a square root undoes squaring: √49 = 7 because 7² = 49. If squaring asks "how many counters in this square?", the root asks "how long is the side?".

Both 7 and −7 square to 49, so the equation x² = 49 has two solutions. The symbol √ is defined to give only the non-negative one, which is why √49 is 7 and not ±7 — the plus-or-minus in "x = ±7" is written in deliberately, because the symbol alone will not supply it.

Most roots are not whole numbers, and trapping them between two squares is the honest way to handle it: 49 < 60 < 64, so √60 is between 7 and 8, and nearer 8 because 60 is nearer 64. Answering "about 7.7" is a real answer, not a failure to find the exact one.
