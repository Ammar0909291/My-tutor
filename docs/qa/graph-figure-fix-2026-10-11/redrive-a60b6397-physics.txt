**Lesson: Stress, Strain, and Elastic Moduli**

In this lesson we’ll see how forces spread through materials, how those materials deform, and how the ratio of force to deformation tells us how “stiff” a material is. By the end you’ll be able to identify stress and strain in everyday situations and calculate the basic elastic modulus of a simple solid.

Let’s start with **stress**.

Imagine you’re holding a kitchen sponge and you press down on it with your fingertip. The sponge squishes a little under your finger, but if you spread all five fingers over the same spot, the feeling is softer. The same total force is applied, but because it is spread over a larger area, each tiny part of the sponge feels less “push”.

Now picture a carpenter hammering a nail into wood. The hammer’s head delivers a force, but that force is concentrated on the small tip of the nail. The wood right around the tip experiences a very high push, which is why the nail can bite into it.

Think of a blanket being pulled tight. If you pull on one corner, the tension (pulling force) is felt over the whole area of the blanket. If you press a finger into the blanket, the force is focused on a tiny spot, making a deeper dent.

*Stress* is simply how much force is applied to each unit of area of a material. It tells us how “intense” the push or pull is at a specific spot.

**Stress** (symbol σ)

**Key words:**  
- **Force (F):** a push or pull measured in newtons (N).  
- **Area (A):** the surface over which the force acts, measured in square meters (m²).  

When we’re ready for the formula, we write **σ = F / A**, read as “stress equals force divided by area”. For now, just keep the idea that more force or a smaller area means larger stress.

First, let’s recall what the graph showed: the straight‑line part at the lower‑left is where stress ( F / A ) is proportional to strain ( ΔL / L ); the slope of that line is called Young’s modulus E.

Now imagine two steel rods that are **the same length** – 1.0 metre – but have different thicknesses. - Rod A has a cross‑sectional area **A₁ = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m²** (relatively thick). - Rod B has a cross‑sectional area **A₂ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ m²** (much thinner). 

We pull on each rod with the **same tensile force** **F = 500 newtons**.

**Step 1: calculate the stress**  
Stress σ = F / A. - For Rod A: σ₁ = 500 N / 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m² = 5.0 × 10⁶ pascal. - For Rod B: σ₂ = 500 N / 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ m² = 2.0 × 10⁷ pascal. 

**Step 2: find the extension** using the rearranged Hooke’s law ΔL = F L / (A E). We don’t know E yet, but we can express the strain directly: strain ε = ΔL / L = F / (A E). 

**Step 3: compute the strain** (still keeping E unknown). - For Rod A: ε₁ = 500 N / (1.0 × 10⁻⁴ m² · E) = 5.0 × 10⁶ / E. - For Rod B: ε₂ = 500 N / (2.5 × 10⁻⁵ m² · E) = 2.0 × 10⁷ / E. 

**Step 4: determine Young’s modulus** by taking the ratio σ / ε for each rod (the E cancels). - For Rod A: E = σ₁ / ε₁ = (5.0 × 10⁶ Pa) / (5.0 × 10⁶ / E) = E. - For Rod B: E = σ₂ / ε₂ = (2.0 × 10⁷ Pa) / (2.0 × 10⁷ / E) = E. 

Both calculations give the **same value of E**, which for steel is about **200 gigapascals**. Even though the stresses and strains are different because the areas differ, their ratio – the elastic modulus – is identical. This shows that **Young’s modulus is a property of the material itself, not of the particular shape or size of the piece**.

Let’s look at a simple, real‑world case that matches the picture you see on the screen.

A steel rod that is 1 metre long and has a circular cross‑section of 10 millimetres diameter. 
**Situation** – you hang a weight from the bottom of the rod, pulling it straight down.

1. **What happens?** The weight exerts a pulling force \(F\) on the rod. Because the rod’s cross‑sectional area \(A\) is small, the force is spread over that area, giving a **tensile stress**. On the graph the label “stress \(F/A\)” (top left) tells us exactly what we’re measuring.

2. **Calculate the stress** 
 *Area of the rod*: 
 \[
 A = \pi\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^2
 = \pi\left(\frac{0.010\ \text{m}}{2}\right)^2
 \approx 7.85\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2
 \] 
 *Force applied*: suppose the hanging weight is 500 newtons. 
 \[
 \sigma = \frac{F}{A}
 = \frac{500\ \text{N}}{7.85\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2}
 \approx 6.4\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}
 \] 
 That number is the **stress** shown by the “stress \(F/A\)” label.

3. **What about the stretch?** The rod lengthens by a tiny amount \(\Delta L\). Let’s say it stretches 0.032 millimetres (0.000032 m). The **strain** is the fractional change in length, which the graph calls “strain \(\Delta L/L\)” (bottom right):
 \[
 \varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L}
 = \frac{0.000032\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{m}}
 = 3.2\times10^{-5}
 \]

4. **Young’s modulus** – the slope of the straight‑line part of the curve is labelled “slope = E” (blue). It tells us how much stress is needed for a given strain:
 \[
 E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}
 = \frac{6.4\times10^{6}\ \text{Pa}}{3.2\times10^{-5}}
 \approx 2.0\times10^{11}\ \text{Pa}
 \] 
 That value (about 200 gigapascals) is the **elastic modulus** of the steel – a property of the material, independent of the rod’s size.

Young's modulus E and spring constant k are easy to blur together, but they answer genuinely different questions. E = σ/ε is a pure MATERIAL property — steel is always E ≈ 200 GPa whether you test a short thick rod or a long thin wire, the same way "steel" always means the same substance no matter how it's shaped. Spring constant k = EA/L, by contrast, mixes that material property together with the OBJECT's own geometry (its area A and length L) — so a short, thick steel rod and a long, thin steel wire share the identical E but have wildly different k values (the rod is far stiffer to physically deform). Confusing the two leads to treating a geometry change as if it changed the material itself, when only k has moved.
