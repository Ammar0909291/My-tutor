**Lesson: Square Roots**  

In this lesson we’ll explore what a square root is. By the end you’ll be able to identify the square‑root of a number and understand how it relates to squaring (multiplying a number by itself).  

Imagine a small square tile that is exactly 4 centimetres on each side. If you place four of those tiles together in a row, they form a longer line that is 16 centimetres long.  

Now picture a square floor made of those same tiles. If the whole floor measures 16 square centimetres in area, how long is each side of the floor?  

In plain words: the *square root* of a number x is the non‑negative number y that, when multiplied by itself (y × y), gives x.  

We call the number under the root sign the **radicand** (the “x” in √x).  

🟢 **Question:** Looking at the tile example, what do you notice about the relationship between the side length (4 cm) and the total area (16 cm²)?

- The curved line that starts at the origin (0, 0) and rises gently to the right is the plot of **\(y = \sqrt{x}\)**. For each point on this curve, the y‑coordinate is the non‑negative number whose square equals the x‑coordinate.
- The grid behind the curve helps us see how the function grows: as x gets larger, the increase in y gets slower, reflecting the fact that taking a square root “flattens” numbers.

Overall, the figure demonstrates the relationship between a number and its non‑negative square root: every positive x has exactly one point on the curve, showing that the square root is a single‑valued (function) operation.

Imagine a square whose total area is 20 square centimetres. 
The side length \(s\) must satisfy \(s^{2}=20\) because the area of a square equals side × side. 

Since we know the perfect squares \(4^{2}=16\) and \(5^{2}=25\), the side length must lie between 4 and 5 centimetres. 
Thus the square‑root symbol gives the **non‑negative** number that solves this: 

The details of this example are not in your lesson materials, so treat them as an illustration of the idea, not as facts to learn.

\[
\sqrt{20}=s\approx 4.47\text{ cm}
\]

If we were asked to “solve \(y^{2}=20\)”, we would write \(y=\pm\sqrt{20}\), giving two answers \(+4.47\) and \(-4.47\). The √‑symbol itself picks only the positive one because it represents a function.

Taking a square root undoes squaring: √49 = 7 because 7² = 49. If squaring asks "how many counters in this square?", the root asks "how long is the side?".

Both 7 and −7 square to 49, so the equation x² = 49 has two solutions. The symbol √ is defined to give only the non-negative one, which is why √49 is 7 and not ±7 — the plus-or-minus in "x = ±7" is written in deliberately, because the symbol alone will not supply it.

Most roots are not whole numbers, and trapping them between two squares is the honest way to handle it: 49 < 60 < 64, so √60 is between 7 and 8, and nearer 8 because 60 is nearer 64. Answering "about 7.7" is a real answer, not a failure to find the exact one.
