**Lesson: “Rate Law and Order”**  
In this lesson we’ll see how the speed of a chemical reaction depends on the amounts of the substances present, learn what “order” means for each reactant and for the whole reaction, and discover how to use the rate constant k and the initial‑rate method. By the end you’ll be able to write a rate law, identify reaction orders, calculate the units of k, and determine k from experimental data.  

Imagine a kitchen faucet. When you turn the tap a little, only a thin stream of water drips out; turn it more, and a strong stream rushes out. In the same way, the “rate” of a chemical reaction (how quickly reactants turn into products) depends on how much of each reactant is present—just as water flow depends on how open the faucet is. This relationship is called the **rate law**.

Let’s see how the units of the rate constant k change when the overall order of a reaction changes.

The rate of a reaction is measured as concentration per time, usually **moles · litre⁻¹ · second⁻¹**. In the rate law

\[
\text{rate}=k[\text{A}]^{m}[\text{B}]^{n}
\]

the concentration terms together have the unit **(mole · litre⁻¹)^(m+n)**. To keep the equation balanced, k must carry whatever units are needed so that the product on the right‑hand side ends up as **mole · litre⁻¹ · second⁻¹**.

So, for different overall orders ( m + n ):

Overall order · Units of k (written out)
0 (zero‑order) · mole · litre⁻¹ · second⁻¹
1 (first‑order) · second⁻¹
2 (second‑order) · litre · mole⁻¹ · second⁻¹
n (nth‑order) · litre^( n‑1 ) · mole^( 1‑n ) · second⁻¹

**Why this works:** 
If the reaction is second‑order (overall order = 2), the concentration term contributes \((\text{mole · litre}^{-1})^{2} = \text{mole}^{2}\,\text{litre}^{-2}\). To end up with a rate unit of \(\text{mole · litre}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}\), k must supply \(\text{litre · mole}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}\).

---

1. second⁻¹ 
2. litre · mole⁻¹ · second⁻¹ 
3. mole · litre⁻¹ · second⁻¹

Let’s look at a real reaction that chemists have studied: 

**Reaction:** H₂ + Br₂ → 2 HBr 

Experiments were done at the same temperature, and the initial rates were measured while changing the concentrations of the reactants.

Experiment · [H₂] (mol L⁻¹) · [Br₂] (mol L⁻¹) · Initial rate (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹)
1 · 0.10 · 0.10 · 2.0 × 10⁻⁴
2 · 0.20 · 0.10 · 4.0 × 10⁻⁴
3 · 0.10 · 0.20 · 2.8 × 10⁻⁴

**Step 1 – Order with respect to H₂ (m).** 
Experiments 1 and 2 keep [Br₂] the same, so the rate ratio equals the concentration ratio raised to *m*:

\[
\frac{\text{rate}_2}{\text{rate}_1}= \left(\frac{_2}{_1}\right)^{m}
\]

\[
\frac{4.0\times10^{-4}}{2.0\times10^{-4}} = \left(\frac{0.20}{0.10}\right)^{m}
\]

\[
2 = (2)^{m}\;\Longrightarrow\; m = 1
\]

**Step 2 – Order with respect to Br₂ (n).** 
Experiments 1 and 3 keep [H₂] the same:

\[
\frac{\text{rate}_3}{\text{rate}_1}= \left(\frac{[Br_2]_3}{[Br_2]_1}\right)^{n}
\]

\[
\frac{2.8\times10^{-4}}{2.0\times10^{-4}} = \left(\frac{0.20}{0.10}\right)^{n}
\]

\[
1.4 = (2)^{n}\;\Longrightarrow\; n = \log_{2}(1.4) \approx 0.49 \approx \tfrac12
\]

So the rate law is 

\[
\text{rate}=k\,[\text{H}_2]^{1}\,[\text{Br}_2]^{1/2}
\]

**Step 3 – Overall order and the rate constant.** 
Overall order = m + n ≈ 1 + 0.5 = 1.5 (a fractional order). 
Using experiment 1 to find *k*:

\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{H}_2]^{1}[\text{Br}_2]^{1/2}}
 = \frac{2.0\times10^{-4}}{(0.10)(0.10)^{1/2}}
 = \frac{2.0\times10^{-4}}{0.10\times0.316}
 = \frac{2.0\times10^{-4}}{0.0316}
 \approx 6.3\times10^{-3}\ \text{(L}^{1/2}\text{ mol}^{-1/2}\text{ s}^{-1})
\]

The units of *k* follow from “rate = k [A]¹ [B]½”: 

\[
k\; \text{units}= \frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}}{(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{1}(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{1/2}}
= \text{L}^{1/2}\,\text{mol}^{-1/2}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

Think of making a sauce on the stove. The “rate” at which the sauce thickens depends on two things you can control: how much flour you add (the concentration of reactant A) and how hot the pan is (the concentration of reactant B). If you double the amount of flour while keeping the heat the same, the sauce will thicken faster, because there are more flour particles to collide with the liquid. If you keep the flour amount steady but turn the heat up, the thickening also speeds up, because the particles move more quickly and collide more often.

The rate law includes a negative order for A, so a higher [A] reduces the rate. Doubling [A] makes the rate one‑half of its original value, not double it.

A negative reaction order means the species acts as an inhibitor. As its concentration rises, it interferes with the reaction pathway, lowering the rate. This behavior is common in enzyme kinetics where a substrate can bind to an inhibitory site, causing the overall rate to drop as the inhibitor concentration increases.
