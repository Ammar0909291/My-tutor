**Rate Law and Order** – In this lesson we’ll explore how the speed of a chemical reaction depends on the amounts of the substances involved. By the end you’ll be able to write a rate law, identify the order with respect to each reactant, find the overall order, determine the units of the rate constant \(k\), and use the initial‑rate method to get those numbers from data.  

Imagine a kitchen where you’re making a sauce. The more garlic you add, the faster the sauce gets that strong flavor; the more heat you turn on, the quicker the sauce thickens. Your mind’s picture is a “speed dial” that turns up or down depending on how much of each ingredient you put in.  

In plain language, the **rate law** tells us how the reaction rate changes when we change the concentrations of the reactants. It’s written as  

\[
\text{rate} = k\,[A]^{m}[B]^{n}
\]

where \(k\) is the rate constant, \([A]\) and \([B]\) are the concentrations of the reactants, and \(m\) and \(n\) are the orders with respect to each reactant.  

What do you notice about how the reaction speed might change when you increase the amount of one reactant while keeping the others the same?

Now let’s see how the **rate constant k** gets its units. The rate law we wrote is

\[
\text{rate}=k\,[A]^{m}[B]^{n}
\]

where the reaction rate has units **moles per litre per second** ( mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ ) and each concentration \([A]\) or \([B]\) has units **moles per litre** ( mol L⁻¹ ).

If we solve the equation for k, we get

\[
k=\frac{\text{rate}}{[A]^{m}[B]^{n}}
\]

so the units of k are the units of the rate divided by the units of the concentration terms raised to their powers. Because the overall order is the sum \(m+n\), the unit pattern depends only on that total:

Overall order ( m + n ) · Units of k
0 (zero‑order) · mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹
1 (first‑order) · s⁻¹
2 (second‑order) · L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹
n (nth‑order) · L^( n‑1 ) mol^( 1‑n ) s⁻¹

For example, if a reaction is overall **second order** (say m = 1, n = 1), then

\[
k = \frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}}{(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{1+1}} = \text{L mol}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}
\]

If the overall order were **third order**, the denominator would have \((\text{mol L}^{-1})^{3}\), giving

\[
k = \frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}}{(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{3}} = \text{L}^{2}\text{mol}^{-2}\text{s}^{-1}
\]

Let’s work through a real reaction and see how the rate law tells us what the speed will be.

**Reaction** 
\(2\text{NO}(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \;\rightarrow\; 2\text{NO}_2(g)\)

Experiments have shown that its rate law is 

\[
\text{rate} = k[\text{NO}]^{\,2}[\text{O}_2]^{\,1}
\]

* The exponent 2 is the **order with respect to NO**. 
* The exponent 1 is the **order with respect to O₂**. 
* The **overall order** is \(2 + 1 = 3\) (third‑order overall).

---

### Step 1 – Find the rate constant \(k\)

Suppose at 300 K we measured an initial rate of 

\[
\text{rate}_1 = 2.0 \times 10^{-4}\;\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

when the concentrations were 

\[
[\text{NO}]_1 = 0.10\;\text{mol L}^{-1}, \qquad [\text{O}_2]_1 = 0.05\;\text{mol L}^{-1}
\]

Insert these numbers into the rate law and solve for \(k\):

\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}_1}{[\text{NO}]_1^{\,2}[\text{O}_2]_1}
 = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-4}}{(0.10)^2 \times 0.05}
 = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-4}}{5.0 \times 10^{-4}}
 = 0.40\;\text{L}^{2}\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

*Units*: 
Rate has units **mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹**; the denominator \([\text{NO}]^{2}[\text{O}_2]\) has units \((\text{mol L}^{-1})^{3}\). 
Dividing gives \( \text{L}^{2}\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}\), which matches the third‑order pattern.

---

### Step 2 – Predict a new rate when we change a concentration

Now keep \([\text{O}_2]\) the same but double the NO concentration:

\[
[\text{NO}]_2 = 0.20\;\text{mol L}^{-1}, \qquad [\text{O}_2]_2 = 0.05\;\text{mol L}^{-1}
\]

Using the same \(k\):

\[
\text{rate}_2 = k[\text{NO}]_2^{\,2}[\text{O}_2]_2
 = 0.40 \times (0.20)^2 \times 0.05
 = 0.40 \times 0.04 \times 0.05
 = 0.40 \times 0.002
 = 8.0 \times 10^{-4}\;\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

The rate **quadrupled** because the reaction is second‑order in NO (doubling \([\text{NO}]\) multiplies the rate by \(2^2 = 4\)).

---

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Imagine you are filling a bathtub that has **two separate taps** – one for hot water (A) and one for cold water (B). The total amount of water flowing into the tub each second is the **reaction rate**. How fast the water comes in depends on two things:

* **How open each tap is** – this is like the concentration of each reactant. If you open the hot‑water tap a little more, the flow changes; if you open the cold‑water tap, the flow also changes. 
* **How the flow responds to each tap** – this is the **order** (the exponent *m* for hot water and *n* for cold water). If the order is 1, doubling the opening of that tap doubles its contribution to the flow. If the order is 2, doubling the opening makes that part of the flow four times larger. 

The **rate constant k** is like the overall pipe size and the water pressure that stay the same while you adjust the taps. So the overall flow can be written as 

\[
\text{flow rate} = k \times (\text{hot‑tap opening})^{m} \times (\text{cold‑tap opening})^{n}
\]

just as a rate law is rate = k [A]^m [B]^n.

Have you ever noticed that turning a faucet a little more sometimes makes a big jump in water flow, while other times the change is much smaller?

Let’s look at a specific reaction and see exactly how the rate law works.

**Example:**  
Consider the reaction  

\[
\text{A} + \text{B} \;\rightarrow\; \text{C}
\]

and suppose experiments have shown that its rate follows  

\[
\text{rate} = k\,[\text{A}]^{2}\,[\text{B}]
\]

*Step 1 – Choose concentrations.*  
Take an initial condition where \([\text{A}] = 0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\) and \([\text{B}] = 0.20\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\).  

*Step 2 – Plug into the rate law.*  

\[
\text{rate} = k \times (0.10)^{2} \times (0.20)
           = k \times 0.01 \times 0.20
           = k \times 0.002\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}
\]

So the measured rate is \(0.002\,k\) (the exact number depends on the value of \(k\) at the given temperature).

*Step 3 – Change one concentration and see the effect.*  
Now double the concentration of A while keeping B the same: \([\text{A}] = 0.20\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\).  

\[
\text{rate}' = k \times (0.20)^{2} \times (0.20)
            = k \times 0.04 \times 0.20
            = k \times 0.008\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}
\]

The new rate is \(0.008\,k\), which is **four times** the original rate because the concentration of A appears squared in the law (doubling \([\text{A}]\) multiplies the rate by \(2^{2}=4\)).

*Step 4 – Change the other reactant.*  
If instead we keep \([\text{A}] = 0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\) and double \([\text{B}]\) to \(0.40\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\):

\[
\text{rate}'' = k \times (0.10)^{2} \times (0.40)
             = k \times 0.01 \times 0.40
             = k \times 0.004\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}
\]

Now the rate has **doubled** because B appears to the first power.

This demonstration shows how the exponents (the *orders*) in the rate law tell us exactly how changing each reactant’s concentration will change the overall reaction speed.
