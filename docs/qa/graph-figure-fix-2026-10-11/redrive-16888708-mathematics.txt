Hello QA chem‑redrive! Today’s lesson is **Square Roots**. By the end of it you’ll know what a square root is, how to recognise it in everyday situations, and how to find the square root of a number.

Imagine a perfect square chocolate bar that’s 9 small squares wide and 9 squares tall.  
If you wanted to share that bar by cutting it into a smaller square piece that has the same total amount of chocolate, you’d look for the length of one side of the big square.  
Picture the side length as the “hidden” number that, when multiplied by itself, gives you the area of the whole bar.

In plain words, the **square root** of a non‑negative number x is the non‑negative number y that satisfies y² = x.  
We call x the *radicand* and write the square root as √x.

Now let’s see how the square‑root sign differs from solving a squaring equation. If we **compute** √36, we get 6 because 6 × 6 = 36 and we choose the non‑negative result. If we **solve** the equation y² = 36, we must list **both** numbers whose square is 36: y = 6 or y = −6. The symbol √ only picks the first of these. Next, consider roots of different orders. For an even index (the square root), a negative radicand has no real result: √(−9) is undefined in the real numbers because no real number squared gives −9. For an odd index, the sign is preserved: the cube root of −8 is −2, since (−2)³ = −8. Here there is no sign ambiguity, so the result is simply −2. Now let’s look at a square whose area is 20 square units. The side length \(s\) of a square satisfies \(s^2 =\) area, so here \(s^2 = 20\). To find \(s\) we need the square root of 20: \(s = \sqrt{20}\). We know the perfect squares 4² = 16 and 5² = 25. Since 16 < 20 < 25, the side length must be between 4 and 5. Trying a midpoint, 4.5² = 20.25, which is a little larger than 20, so the true side length is just under 4.5. Thus \(\sqrt{20}\) is a number a bit less than 4.5, lying between 4 and 5. **Why does knowing the squares of 4 and 5 tell us that the side length must lie between 4 and 5?**

You have answered every new practice question I have on Square Roots in this lesson; a question you have already seen comes back once more a little later. Here is the idea once more.

Taking a square root undoes squaring: √49 = 7 because 7² = 49. If squaring asks "how many counters in this square?", the root asks "how long is the side?".

Both 7 and −7 square to 49, so the equation x² = 49 has two solutions. The SYMBOL √ is defined to give only the non-negative one, which is why √49 is 7 and not ±7 — the plus-or-minus in "x = ±7" is written in deliberately, because the symbol alone will not supply it.

Most roots are not whole numbers, and trapping them between two squares is the honest way to handle it: 49 < 60 < 64, so √60 is between 7 and 8, and nearer 8 because 60 is nearer 64. Answering "about 7.7" is a real answer, not a failure to find the exact one.
