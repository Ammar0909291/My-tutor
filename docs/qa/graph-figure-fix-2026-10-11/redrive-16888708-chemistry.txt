**Lesson: “Rate Law and Order”**  

In this lesson we’ll explore how chemists describe the speed of a reaction. By the end you’ll be able to write a rate law, identify the order of each reactant, find the overall order, understand the rate constant \(k\) and its units, and use the initial‑rate method to determine those numbers.

Let’s start with the first piece: **the rate law**.

Imagine you’re baking cookies. The more chocolate chips you stir into the dough, the faster the cookies look “chocolate‑y” as they bake. If you add a lot of chips, the chocolate flavor appears quickly; with just a few chips, it takes longer for the flavor to spread.

In a chemical reaction, something similar happens: the speed at which reactants turn into products depends on how much of each reactant is present. The relationship between the concentrations of the reactants and the reaction speed is called the **rate law**.

Think of the rate law as a recipe that tells you how the “cooking speed” changes when you change the amounts of the ingredients (the reactants).  

In plain words: *the rate law expresses the reaction rate as a product of the concentrations of each reactant raised to a power (the order), multiplied by a constant \(k\).*  

Rate law  

**Key term:** *order* – the exponent that tells how strongly the rate depends on a particular reactant’s concentration.

Let’s see how the units of the rate constant k change with the overall order of a reaction. The rate we measure is usually expressed as **mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹** (moles per litre per second). The concentration of each reactant is in **mol L⁻¹**.

From the generic rate law

\[
\text{rate}=k[\text{A}]^{\,n}
\]

we can solve for k:

\[
k=\frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{A}]^{\,n}}
\]

So the units of k are the units of the rate divided by the units of concentration raised to the power n.

- **Zero‑order (n = 0):** \(k\) has the same units as the rate → **mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹**. - **First‑order (n = 1):** \(k\) = (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹) / (mol L⁻¹) → **s⁻¹**. - **Second‑order (n = 2):** \(k\) = (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹) / (mol L⁻¹)² → **L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹**.

Imagine the reaction 

\[
\text{H}_2(g) + \text{Br}_2(g) \;\longrightarrow\; 2\text{HBr}(g)
\]

Experimental work shows its rate law is 

\[
\text{rate} = k\,[\text{H}_2]^1\,[\text{Br}_2]^{1/2}
\]

Here the order with respect to H₂ is **1** (first‑order) and the order with respect to Br₂ is **½** (half‑order). 
The overall order is \(1 + \tfrac12 = 1.5\).

Suppose at a certain temperature the rate constant is 

\[
k = 0.020\; \text{L}^{0.5}\,\text{mol}^{-0.5}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

and the concentrations are 

\[
[\text{H}_2] = 0.10\;\text{mol L}^{-1}, \qquad
[\text{Br}_2] = 0.04\;\text{mol L}^{-1}
\]

First calculate the half‑order term:

\[
[\text{Br}_2]^{1/2} = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.20\;\text{(mol L}^{-1})^{1/2}
\]

Now plug everything into the rate law:

\[
\text{rate} = 0.020 \times 0.10 \times 0.20
 = 0.00040\;\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

So the reaction proceeds at \(4.0 \times 10^{-4}\) moles per litre per second.

1. **Start with the reaction mixture** – we have the reactants in a flask. 
2. **Measure the initial concentrations** of each reactant. 
3. **Record the initial reaction rate** (how fast product appears or reactant disappears). 
4. **Change the concentration of one reactant** while keeping the others the same. 
5. **Measure the new rate** and compare it to the original. 
6. **Use the ratio of rates to concentrations** to determine the order (the exponent) for that reactant; repeat for the other reactants to get the overall rate law.

---

### Everyday analogy

Think of baking a cake where the “speed of baking” is how quickly the cake rises. 

- **Step 1:** You start with a batter (the reaction mixture). 
- **Step 2:** You note how much flour and how many eggs you put in (initial concentrations). 
- **Step 3:** You watch how fast the cake rises in the oven (initial rate). 
- **Step 4:** Next you make a second batch, but this time you double the flour while keeping the eggs the same (change one concentration). 
- **Step 5:** You see whether the cake rises faster, slower, or the same (new rate). 
- **Step 6:** By comparing the two rising speeds, you figure out how important flour is for the rise (the order with respect to flour). Then you do the same with eggs to get the full “baking‑speed law.”

Just as you can’t guess the effect of each ingredient just from the recipe, you can’t read the reaction orders from the balanced chemical equation. You have to **experiment**—change one thing at a time and watch what happens.

**How it works**

1. **Keep** the concentration of B the same in both experiments. 2. **Change** only the concentration of A (double it from 0.10 M to 0.20 M). 3. **Measure** the initial reaction rate each time (Rate₁ and Rate₂). 4. **Compare** the rates:

\[
 \frac{\text{Rate}_2}{\text{Rate}_1}
 = \left(\frac{[A]_2}{[A]_1}\right)^{m}
 \]

Plug in the numbers:

\[
 \frac{8.0 \times 10^{-6}}{2.0 \times 10^{-6}} = \left(\frac{0.20}{0.10}\right)^{m}
 \]

\[
 4 = 2^{m} \;\Longrightarrow\; m = 2
 \]

So the reaction is **second order** in A.

Let’s look at what a rate law is by starting with what you already know: the **rate of a reaction** tells us how fast the amount of product (or how quickly reactants disappear) changes with time.  

We already see that changing the concentrations of the reactants changes the rate, but we need to know **how** they change it. That “how” is written in the rate‑law expression:

**rate = k [A]ᵐ [B]ⁿ**

* **k** – the rate constant (a fixed number for a given temperature)  
* **[A]** and **[B]** – the concentrations of the reactants A and B  
* **m** and **n** – the *orders* with respect to A and B; they tell us the power to which each concentration is raised  

If **m = 1**, doubling [A] doubles the rate (a linear effect).  
If **m = 2**, doubling [A] makes the rate four times larger (a squared effect).  
If **m = 0**, changing [A] does nothing to the rate (no effect).  

The **overall order** of the reaction is simply the sum **m + n**. It determines the units of the rate constant **k**. For example:

* overall order = 0 → k has units of mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹  
* overall order = 1 → k has units of s⁻¹  
* overall order = 2 → k has units of L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹  

To find the values of **m** and **n** we use the **initial‑rate method**: run a few experiments, keep one reactant’s concentration fixed while changing the other, and compare how the initial rates change. The ratio of the rates equals the ratio of the concentrations raised to the unknown order, letting us solve for that order.
