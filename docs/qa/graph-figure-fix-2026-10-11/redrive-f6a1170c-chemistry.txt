Welcome to **Rate Law and Order** – a lesson about how fast chemical reactions happen and how we can describe that speed with simple math. By the end, you’ll be able to write a rate law, tell the order of a reaction, find the overall order, understand the rate constant k and its units, and use the initial‑rate method to figure out those numbers from data.

Imagine a kitchen sink with the drain plug partially lifted. When the plug is opened a little, water drips out slowly; open it wider and the water rushes out much faster. The amount of water leaving per minute is the **flow rate**, and it changes depending on how big the opening is.

Now picture a chemical reaction the same way: the “opening” is how many reactant molecules are around and how energetic they are. If there are more molecules (or they move faster), the reaction “drains” reactants more quickly, just like water flows faster through a larger opening.

Think of a crowded hallway: the more people trying to get through a doorway at once, the quicker the crowd moves through. In chemistry, the “people” are reacting molecules, and the “doorway size” is related to how the concentration of each reactant influences the speed.

A **rate law** tells us exactly how the reaction speed depends on the concentrations of the reactants. It looks like: rate = k [ A ]^m [ B ]^n, where k is the **rate constant**, m and n are the **orders** with respect to each reactant, and the sum m + n is the **overall order**.

🧪 **Order** means how strongly the rate changes when you change a reactant’s concentration. If doubling a reactant doubles the rate, that reactant is first‑order (exponent = 1). If the rate doesn’t change at all, it’s zero‑order (exponent = 0).

Now, looking at the sink example: what do you notice about how the water flow changes when you open the plug a little more versus a lot more?

Imagine mixing hydrogen gas (H₂) with bromine gas (Br₂) in a flask. The overall balanced equation is

H₂ + Br₂ → 2 HBr

If we measure how fast HBr forms while changing the concentrations, we find the rate follows

rate = k [H₂]¹ [Br₂]½

so the reaction is first order in H₂ (the exponent is 1) but only half‑order in Br₂ (the exponent is ½). The stoichiometric coefficient of Br₂ is also 1, yet the order is **not** 1. This shows that the exponents m and n (the reaction orders) cannot be read from the balanced equation; they must be determined experimentally.

Let’s work through a specific reaction and see the rate law in action.

**Example reaction** 
A + B → C 

Suppose experiments show the rate follows 

\[
\text{rate}=k\,[\text{A}]^{2}[\text{B}]
\]

Here the order with respect to A is 2 (because the concentration of A is squared) and the order with respect to B is 1. The overall order is 2 + 1 = 3.

**Choose numbers** 
- Initial concentration of A: \([\text{A}] = 0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\) 
- Initial concentration of B: \([\text{B}] = 0.20\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\) 
- Rate constant at the given temperature: \(k = 0.50\ \text{L}^{2}\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}\) 

(The units of \(k\) come from “rate ÷ ([A]²[B])”. Rate has units mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹, \([\text{A}]^{2}[\text{B}]\) has units \((\text{mol L}^{-1})^{3} = \text{mol}^{3}\,\text{L}^{-3}\). Dividing gives \(k\) units of L² mol⁻² s⁻¹, which matches the overall third order.)

**Calculate the rate** 

\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{rate} &= 0.50\ \text{L}^{2}\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}\times (0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1})^{2}\times (0.20\ \text{mol L}^{-1})\\
&= 0.50 \times 0.01 \times 0.20\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}\\
&= 0.001\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}.
\end{aligned}
\]

So the reaction proceeds at a speed of \(1.0\times10^{-3}\) mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ under these conditions.

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Think of a kitchen where you’re making a smoothie. The **speed** at which the smoothie is ready (the reaction rate) depends on two things you can control: 

* how many **fruits** you put in (call this “A”), and 
* how fast the **blender** spins (call this “B”). 

If you double the amount of fruit but keep the blender speed the same, the smoothie might finish a little faster, but not twice as fast—maybe only 1.5 times faster. That “1.5” is the **order** *m* for the fruit. If you double the blender speed while keeping the fruit amount fixed, the smoothie could finish four times faster; that “4” comes from the order *n* for the blender speed (in this case *n = 2, because 2² = 4). The overall order is simply the sum *m + n*. The constant *k* is like the recipe’s base efficiency: it tells you the speed you’d get when you have one unit of fruit and the blender running at one unit of speed, and it only changes if you change the temperature of the kitchen or the type of blender.

So the rate law “rate = k [A]^m [B]^n” is just a way of writing: **how fast the smoothie is made = (recipe efficiency) × (fruit amount raised to its order) × (blender speed raised to its order**). The exponents *m* and *n* are found by trying different amounts and speeds and seeing how the finishing time changes—they’re not taken from the list of ingredients.
