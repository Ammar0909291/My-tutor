**Rate Law and Order** – In this lesson we’ll explore how the speed of a chemical reaction depends on the amounts of the substances involved, learn how to write the mathematical expression (the rate law), and see how to tell the reaction’s order and the rate constant k. By the end you’ll be able to write a rate law from experimental data, identify the order with respect to each reactant, find the overall order, and determine the units of k using the initial‑rate method.

Imagine a kitchen sink full of water. When you pull the plug, the water rushes out quickly at first and then slows down as the level drops. The speed at which the water leaves (the flow rate) depends on how much water is still in the sink – more water means a faster flow.

In a chemical reaction, something similar happens: the reaction’s speed (its rate) depends on how much of each reactant is present. If we picture the reactants as the water in the sink, the rate law is the rule that tells us exactly how the amount of each reactant controls the overall speed of the reaction.

**Rate law** – the mathematical relationship that links the reaction rate to the concentrations of the reactants, each raised to a power (the order).  

*Order* – the exponent that tells how strongly a particular reactant’s concentration influences the rate.  

*Overall order* – the sum of the individual orders for all reactants.  

*Rate constant (k)* – the proportionality factor that makes the equation work; its units depend on the overall order.

The rate law for a reaction involving two reactants A and B is written as 

\[
\text{rate}=k\,[A]^{m}[B]^{n}
\]

where **k** is the rate constant, **m** is the order with respect to A, and **n** is the order with respect to B. 

Now imagine the simple reaction 

\[
\mathrm{H_{2} + Br_{2} \;\longrightarrow\; 2\,HBr}
\]

If we looked only at the balanced equation, we might expect the rate to depend on the concentrations of H₂ and Br₂ to the first power each, because the stoichiometric coefficients are both 1. However, experiments show that the actual rate law is 

\[
\text{rate}=k\,[\mathrm{H_{2}}]\,[\mathrm{Br_{2}}]^{1/2}
\]

Here the order in Br₂ is **½**, not **1**. This mismatch tells us that the reaction’s order cannot be read from the stoichiometric coefficients; the orders must be determined **experimentally** because they reflect the underlying mechanism, not the overall equation. 

Because the overall order is the sum \(m+n\), the units of the rate constant **k** depend on that sum. Starting from 

\[
k=\frac{\text{rate}}{[A]^{m}[B]^{n}}
\]

and using the fact that rate has units of mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ and concentration has units of mol L⁻¹, we obtain:

* **Zero‑order overall (m + n = 0):** k has units of mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. * **First‑order overall (m + n = 1):** k has units of s⁻¹. * **Second‑order overall (m + n = 2):** k has units of L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹. 

These unit patterns let us check whether a proposed rate law is consistent with the measured overall order.

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=moneyAsMath]
Imagine you place a small sealed flask that contains hydrogen gas (H₂) and bromine vapor (Br₂). As they react, hydrogen bromide (HBr) forms, and you can measure how quickly the amount of HBr increases – that’s the reaction rate.

For this reaction the experimentally‑determined rate law is 

\[
\text{rate} = k\,[\text{H}_2]^{\,1}\,[\text{Br}_2]^{\,\tfrac12}
\]

(rate equals k times the concentration of H₂ to the first power times the concentration of Br₂ to the one‑half power). 
- The **order with respect to H₂** is 1 (first‑order). 
- The **order with respect to Br₂** is ½ (half‑order). 
- The **overall order** is the sum: \(1 + \tfrac12 = 1.5\).

Suppose at a certain temperature you measure a rate of \(2.0 \times 10^{-5}\) mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ when \([\text{H}_2] = 0.10\) mol L⁻¹ and \([\text{Br}_2] = 0.04\) mol L⁻¹. 
You can solve for the rate constant \(k\):

\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{H}_2]^{1}[\text{Br}_2]^{1/2}}
 = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-5}}{(0.10)(0.04)^{1/2}}
 = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-5}}{0.10 \times 0.20}
 = \frac{2.0 \times 10^{-5}}{0.020}
 = 1.0 \times 10^{-3}\ \text{L}^{1/2}\,\text{mol}^{-1/2}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

The units of \(k\) come from rearranging the rate law: because the overall order is 1.5, \(k\) must have units of \(\text{L}^{1/2}\,\text{mol}^{-1/2}\,\text{s}^{-1}\) so that the whole expression yields \(\text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}\).

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Imagine you’re baking a cake, and you want to know how quickly the batter will rise in the oven. The “rise speed” depends on how much **flour** you add and how many **eggs** you whisk in. If you double the flour and the batter rises twice as fast, we say the rise speed is **first‑order** in flour (the effect is linear). If you double the eggs and the rise speed becomes four times faster, the rise speed is **second‑order** in eggs (the effect is squared). The overall “recipe rule” that tells you how the rise speed changes with each ingredient is just like the **rate law** for a chemical reaction:

**rate = k [flour]ᵐ [eggs]ⁿ**

where *m* and *n* are the orders (how strongly each ingredient influences the speed) and *k* is a constant that depends on the oven temperature.
