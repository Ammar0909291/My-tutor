**Lesson: Stress, Strain, and Elastic Moduli**  

In this lesson we’ll explore how forces change the shape of materials. By the end, you’ll be able to describe what stress and strain are, tell how they are measured, and understand why their ratio—called the elastic modulus—is useful for comparing how “stiff” different materials are.  

Imagine a simple rubber band you keep on your desk. When you pull it gently, it stretches a little and feels soft. If you pull harder, it stretches more and you feel a stronger pull. This everyday object shows us two important ideas: the force you apply (the pull) and how much the band changes its length.  

Now picture using that rubber band to hold a few pencils together. The band is stretched between the pencils, and the force you apply is spread over the thin cross‑section of the band. The way the band resists the pull is what we call **stress**—the force applied per unit area of the material.  

At the same time, the rubber band gets longer. The amount it lengthens compared to its original length is called **strain**—a dimension‑less measure of how much the material deforms.  

So, in plain words:  
* **Stress** tells us how much “push” or “pull” is acting on each tiny piece of a material.  
* **Strain** tells us how much that material’s shape changes relative to its original shape.  

These two ideas lead to the **elastic modulus** (or Young’s modulus), which is simply the ratio of stress to strain for a material that returns to its original shape after the force is removed.

Let’s see how the ideas on the screen work with a simple, concrete case. 

Imagine a steel wire that is 1 metre long and has a cross‑sectional area of 2 × 10⁻⁶ square metres. If we pull on it with a force of 200 newtons, the **stress** on the wire is the force divided by the area:

\[
\sigma = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{200\ \text{N}}{2\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2}=1.0\times10^{8}\ \text{Pa}
\]

Now suppose the wire stretches by 0.5 millimetres (that’s ΔL = 5 × 10⁻⁴ m). The **strain** is the change in length divided by the original length:

\[
\varepsilon = \frac{\Delta L}{L} = \frac{5\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}}{1\ \text{m}} = 5\times10^{-4}
\]

The ratio of stress to strain gives the **Young’s modulus** of the material:

\[
E = \frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}= \frac{1.0\times10^{8}\ \text{Pa}}{5\times10^{-4}} = 2.0\times10^{11}\ \text{Pa}
\]

You can see that the same material (steel) always yields the same \(E\) no matter how long or thick the piece is; only the geometry changes the stress and strain values.
