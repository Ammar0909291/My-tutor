**Rate Law and Order** – In this lesson we’ll see how the speed of a chemical reaction depends on the amounts of the substances that are reacting. By the end you’ll be able to write a rate law, tell the order of each reactant, find the overall order, work out the units of the rate constant k, and use the initial‑rate method to get k from experimental data.  

Imagine you’re making a pancake batter. You pour milk and whisk it with flour. If you use a lot of flour, the batter thickens quickly; with just a little flour it stays runny much longer. The “how fast the batter thickens” is like the reaction rate, and the amount of flour you added is like the concentration of a reactant.  

Think of the batter’s thickening as a road where cars (reaction events) move faster when there are more lanes (more reactant molecules). The more lanes you open, the quicker cars can pass through.  

In plain words, the **rate law** tells us how the reaction rate changes when we change the concentrations of the reactants.

We’ve written the rate law as

\[
\text{rate}=k\,[A]^{m}[B]^{n}
\]

where the exponents \(m\) and \(n\) are the orders with respect to each reactant. Now let’s see how the overall order determines the units of the rate constant \(k\).

The rate of a reaction is measured in **moles per litre per second** \((\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1})\). The concentration of a reactant is in **moles per litre** \((\text{mol L}^{-1})\).

If the overall order is \(m+n\), we rearrange the rate law:

\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[A]^{m}[B]^{n}} . \]

So the units of \(k\) are the units of the rate divided by the units of concentration raised to the overall order.

**Example:**
Consider a reaction that is overall second order (e.g., \(m=1\) and \(n=1\)). The rate might be \(0.020\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}\) when \([A]=0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\) and \([B]=0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\).

\[
k = \frac{0.020\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{(0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1})(0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1})}
= \frac{0.020}{0.010}\ \frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{2}}
= 2.0\ \text{L mol}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}. \]

Thus, for a second‑order overall reaction, the rate constant \(k\) has units **litres per mole per second** \((\text{L mol}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1})\).

Let’s look at the gas‑phase reaction 

\[
\text{2 NO(g) + O}_2\text{(g) → 2 NO}_2\text{(g)}
\]

Experimental studies show its rate law is 

\[
\text{rate} = k[\text{NO}]^{2}[\text{O}_2]^{1}
\]

Here the exponent 2 is the **order with respect to NO**, the exponent 1 is the **order with respect to O₂**, and the **overall order** is \(2+1 = 3\). Because the overall order is three, the units of the rate constant \(k\) must be 

\[
\text{L}^{2}\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

(so that when multiplied by \([\text{NO}]^{2}[\text{O}_2]\) the result has the units of a rate, mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹).

Now plug in some numbers. Suppose at a certain temperature 

* \([\text{NO}] = 0.10\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\) 
* \([\text{O}_2] = 0.05\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\) 
* \(k = 1.2 \times 10^{3}\ \text{L}^{2}\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}\)

Then 

\[
\text{rate} = (1.2 \times 10^{3}) \times (0.10)^{2} \times (0.05)
 = 1.2 \times 10^{3} \times 0.01 \times 0.05
 = 1.2 \times 10^{3} \times 0.0005
 = 0.60\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

So the reaction proceeds at 0.60 mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ under those conditions.

Recurring trap: "The rate law exponents equal the stoichiometric coefficients." FALSE in general — only true for ELEMENTARY reactions (single-step mechanisms). Most reactions you balance are OVERALL equations representing multiple steps, and the rate law reflects only the SLOWEST (rate-determining) step's stoichiometry. For 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂, the coefficients are 2 and 1, but the EXPERIMENTAL rate law is rate = k[NO]²[O₂] — coincidentally matching here, but you can't assume this without experimental data. For H₂ + Br₂ → 2HBr, the actual rate law is rate = k[H₂][Br₂]^(1/2) / (1 + k'[HBr]/[Br₂]) — nothing like the simple coefficients (1,1) would suggest! Always determine rate laws EXPERIMENTALLY.

Imagine you’re baking a cake and you want it to rise quickly. Two main ingredients affect the rising speed: **flour** (which supplies the starch that holds the batter together) and **baking powder** (the chemical that creates bubbles). The “rate law” works like a recipe rule that tells you how the rise‑time changes when you change the amount of each ingredient.

The exponents (the 2 and the 1) are not taken from the list of ingredients; they are found by actually testing the batter and seeing how the rise‑time responds.
