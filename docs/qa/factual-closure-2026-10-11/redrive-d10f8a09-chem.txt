**Lesson: Rate Law and Order**  

In this lesson we’ll explore how chemists describe the speed of a reaction with a *rate law*. By the end, you’ll be able to write a rate law, tell the order of a reaction with respect to each reactant, find the overall order, understand the rate constant \(k\) and its units, and use the initial‑rate method to determine these values.  

Imagine a kitchen sink where water drains faster when the pipe is wide and slower when the pipe is narrow. The water level drops at a rate that depends on how big the opening is.  

Now picture a chemical reaction as that draining sink: the “opening” is the concentration of each reactant, and the “water level” is the amount of product forming. The larger the concentration (the wider the “pipe”), the faster the reaction proceeds.  

In plain words, the **rate law** tells us how the reaction rate changes when we change the concentrations of the reactants.

When we write the rate law 

\[
\text{rate}=k[\text{A}]^{m}[\text{B}]^{n}
\]

the **rate constant k** must have units that make the whole right‑hand side match the units of the reaction rate (usually mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹). 

**Step‑by‑step demonstration**

1. **Identify the overall order**: \(m+n\). 2. **Write the units of each term**: 
 * rate → mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ 
 * \([\text{A}]\) and \([\text{B}]\) → mol L⁻¹ 

3. **Solve for the units of k** by rearranging 

\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{A}]^{m}[\text{B}]^{n}}
\]

and substituting the units.

Overall order ( \(m+n\) ) · Example rate law · Units of k
0 (zero‑order) · rate = k · mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹
1 (first‑order) · rate = k[\text{A}] · s⁻¹
2 (second‑order) · rate = k[\text{A}][\text{B}] (or k[\text{A}]²) · L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹
n (nth‑order) · rate = k[\text{A}]ⁿ · L^(\,n‑1) mol^(\,1‑n) s⁻¹

**Why this works** 
Take the second‑order case where the rate law is rate = k[\text{A}][\text{B}]. - Rate units: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ 
- \([\text{A}][\text{B}]\) units: (mol L⁻¹)·(mol L⁻¹) = mol² L⁻² 

Therefore 

\[
k = \frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}}{\text{mol}^2\text{L}^{-2}} = \text{L mol}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1},
\]

which matches the table.

So, once you know the overall order from experiment, you can immediately write the correct units for k.

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Let’s work through a real reaction and see how the rate law is built from experimental data.

**Step 1 – The reaction** 
Consider the gas‑phase reaction 

\[
2\,\text{NO}(g) + \text{O}_2(g) \;\longrightarrow\; 2\,\text{NO}_2(g)
\]

**Step 2 – Measuring initial rates** 
We run two experiments, changing only the concentration of NO while keeping \([\text{O}_2]\) the same.

Experiment · \([\text{NO}]\) (mol L⁻¹) · \([\text{O}_2]\) (mol L⁻¹) · Initial rate (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹)
1 · 0.10 · 0.10 · 2.0 × 10⁻⁴
2 · 0.20 · 0.10 · 8.0 × 10⁻⁴

**Step 3 – Finding the order in NO** 
The rate law has the form 

\[
\text{rate}=k[\text{NO}]^{m}[\text{O}_2]^{n}
\]

Because \([\text{O}_2]\) is unchanged, the ratio of the two rates equals the ratio of the \([\text{NO}]\) terms raised to the power *m*:

\[
\frac{\text{rate}_2}{\text{rate}_1}= \left(\frac{[\text{NO}]_2}{[\text{NO}]_1}\right)^{m}
\]

Insert the numbers:

\[
\frac{8.0\times10^{-4}}{2.0\times10^{-4}} = \left(\frac{0.20}{0.10}\right)^{m}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
4 = 2^{m}
\]

Since \(2^{2}=4\), we obtain **\(m = 2\)**. 
So the reaction is second‑order in NO.

**Step 4 – Finding the order in O₂** 
Now we vary \([\text{O}_2]\) while keeping \([\text{NO}]\) fixed (data not shown). The rate doubles when \([\text{O}_2]\) doubles, indicating **\(n = 1\)** – first‑order in O₂.

**Step 5 – Overall order and the rate constant** 
Overall order = \(m + n = 2 + 1 = 3\).

To see the units of \(k\), rearrange the rate law:

\[
k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[\text{NO}]^{2}[\text{O}_2]^{1}}
\]

The rate has units **mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹**; each concentration contributes **mol L⁻¹**. 
Thus 

\[
k\; \text{units}= \frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1}}{(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{3}}
= \text{L}^{2}\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}
\]

**Step 6 – The final rate law** 

\[
\boxed{\text{rate}=k[\text{NO}]^{2}[\text{O}_2]^{1}}
\]

*(rate equals k times the concentration of NO squared times the concentration of O₂ to the first power)*

[tutor provider=groq fig=none card=none flags=-]
Think of a simple kitchen task: you’re making a batch of pancakes. - **Ingredient A** is the amount of flour, **Ingredient B** is the amount of milk. - The **speed** at which the batter thickens (our “reaction rate”) depends on how much flour and milk you add.

If the recipe says the thickening speed follows

\[
\text{rate} = k \,[\text{flour}]^{m}\,[\text{milk}]^{n}
\]

then:

* **k** is a fixed “cook‑time constant” that only changes if you change the stove temperature. * **m** tells you how strongly the amount of flour controls the speed. * If **m = 1**, doubling the flour doubles the thickening speed. * If **m = 2**, doubling the flour makes the speed four times faster (because \(2^{2}=4\)). * **n** does the same for milk.
